Your seed phrase — 12 or 24 ordinary English words generated when you first create a wallet — is the single most valuable piece of information in your crypto life. It can recreate your entire wallet, on any device, anywhere in the world. Whoever holds those words owns your crypto. This lesson covers exactly how to create, store, and protect it.

## What a Seed Phrase Really Is

When you set up a self-custody wallet (like MetaMask, Trust Wallet, or a hardware wallet), the app generates a seed phrase, also called a recovery phrase or mnemonic phrase. It looks something like this:

`canyon talent mixture ... orchard`

Those words are not random decoration. They encode your wallet's master private key using a standard called BIP-39. Type them — in the correct order — into any compatible wallet, and your entire wallet reappears: same addresses, same balances, same transaction history.

This is both the beauty and the danger of crypto self-custody. There is no company that can reset it for you, and there is no way to change it once it is exposed. If someone photographs your seed phrase, they have a permanent copy of your wallet's master key.

## The Golden Rules

Memorise these five rules. They are non-negotiable.

### 1. Write it down by hand, on paper or metal

When your wallet shows you the seed phrase during setup, write each word down carefully, numbered, in order. Paper works; a metal backup plate (steel sheets you stamp or engrave) is better because it survives fire, water, and time. Do this in a private place where no camera — phone, laptop webcam, CCTV — can see.

### 2. Never store it digitally. Ever.

No screenshots. No photos. No notes app. No Google Docs, no email drafts, no password managers, no cloud storage. Every digital copy can be stolen by malware, a hacked cloud account, a phone backup you forgot about, or someone scrolling through your gallery. Paper cannot be hacked remotely.

### 3. Never share it with anyone

No support agent, no exchange employee, no "validator," no friend, no YouTuber, no government official needs your seed phrase. Anyone who asks for it is trying to rob you. Real support will never ask — this is true for every legitimate wallet and exchange in existence.

### 4. Never type it into a website

Your seed phrase should only ever be entered into a genuine wallet app during recovery — ideally one you installed yourself from an official source. Any website asking for your seed phrase is a phishing site, without exception.

### 5. Verify your backup before funding the wallet

After writing the words down, most wallets ask you to re-enter them to confirm. Take this seriously. Then, for large amounts, do a full test: delete the wallet app (or use a second device), restore from your written backup, and confirm the same addresses appear. A backup you have never tested is a backup you cannot trust.

## Where to Store the Physical Backup

You need a spot that is safe from theft, fire, water, and forgetfulness:

- A home safe or a bank locker is ideal for significant amounts.
- Keep a second copy in a separate location — a trusted family member's safe, for example — so a single fire or burglary cannot destroy your only backup.
- Do not label it "CRYPTO SEED PHRASE." An unmarked envelope in a safe is far less interesting to a burglar than a labelled one.
- Tell one trusted person where it is (a spouse or heir), so your crypto is not lost forever if something happens to you. Consider a sealed envelope they open only in an emergency.

## What to Do If Your Seed Phrase Is Exposed

If you suspect anyone has seen, photographed, or otherwise obtained your seed phrase — even once, even briefly — treat the wallet as compromised:

1. Immediately create a brand-new wallet (which generates a brand-new seed phrase).
2. Transfer all funds from the old wallet to the new one.
3. Never use the old wallet or its seed phrase again.

Do not wait to "see if anything happens." The attacker may simply be waiting for your balance to grow.

## Common Mistakes Beginners Make

- **"I'll just keep it in my email drafts."** Email accounts get hacked constantly. This is one of the most common ways seed phrases are stolen.
- **Reading the words aloud** on a phone call with "support." Voice calls can be recorded, and the support agent is fake anyway.
- **Storing the phrase and the wallet on the same device.** If the device is compromised, both are gone together.
- **Splitting words across hiding spots "cleverly"** (half in a book, half in a drawer) without a reliable system — people forget their own schemes. Two complete copies in two secure locations is simpler and safer.
- **Laminating paper and assuming it is permanent.** Lamination helps against water, but ink fades and paper degrades. Check your backup every year or two, and consider metal for long-term storage.

## India-Specific Notes

- If you use a bank locker for your seed phrase backup, remember locker access requires your presence — make sure your succession plan accounts for this so heirs can legally access it.
- Be cautious of "crypto recovery services" advertised to Indian users after exchange issues or hacks. No third party can recover a lost seed phrase; anyone claiming they can is running an advance-fee scam.
- When travelling, do not carry your seed phrase backup with your devices. Keep them separated — a stolen bag should never contain both your phone and your keys.

## Recap Checklist

- I understand my seed phrase regenerates my entire wallet and can never be changed once exposed.
- My seed phrase is written by hand on paper (or metal), stored in a safe offline location, with a second copy elsewhere.
- There are zero digital copies: no screenshots, photos, notes, emails, or cloud files.
- I have never shared it and never will — no legitimate party ever asks for it.
- I tested my backup with a small amount before trusting it with more.
- I know that if it is ever exposed, I must immediately move funds to a brand-new wallet.

Your seed phrase is the root of your crypto security. Everything else — 2FA, hardware wallets, careful browsing — protects the branches. Guard the root, and the tree stands. Next up: two-factor authentication, the extra lock that protects your exchange and email accounts.
