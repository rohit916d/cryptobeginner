Sooner or later you will need to check your crypto away from home — at a cafe, an airport, a hotel, or a co-working space. Public Wi-Fi is convenient, but it is also one of the least trustworthy networks you will ever use. This lesson explains the real risks and the practical habits that keep your funds safe on the go.

## Why Public Wi-Fi Is Risky

When you join a public network, your internet traffic travels through equipment you do not control, alongside strangers you do not know. The main threats:

- **Snooping.** On poorly secured networks, an attacker on the same Wi-Fi can observe unencrypted traffic — which sites you visit and what data you send.
- **Evil twin hotspots.** An attacker sets up a hotspot named "Cafe_Free_WiFi" or "Airport_WiFi_Free." You connect to the attacker's equipment instead of the real network, and they sit in the middle of everything you do.
- **Man-in-the-middle attacks.** Even on a legitimate network, attackers can try to intercept or alter traffic between you and the sites you visit.
- **Session hijacking.** If an attacker captures your logged-in session, they can impersonate you on that site without needing your password.

The good news: modern encryption (HTTPS) protects most of your traffic's contents. The bad news: attackers do not need to break encryption if they can trick you onto a fake site or steal an active session. Defence is about layers, not perfection.

## The Golden Rule: Avoid Sensitive Actions on Public Wi-Fi

The simplest and strongest protection is behavioural: **do not perform sensitive crypto operations on public Wi-Fi.** That means:

- Do not log in to your exchange account.
- Do not send or receive crypto transactions.
- Do not enter seed phrases, passwords, or 2FA codes.
- Do not access your email (which can reset everything else).

Checking prices or reading news? Fine. Moving money? Wait until you are on a trusted network. Most crypto tasks are not urgent — and urgency, as you have learned, is often a scammer's tool anyway.

## When You Must Transact: Use Your Mobile Data

If you genuinely need to do something sensitive while out, use your phone's mobile data (4G/5G) instead of public Wi-Fi. Mobile networks are significantly harder for a random stranger in a cafe to intercept than an open hotspot. Tethering your laptop to your phone's hotspot is far safer than joining the cafe's network.

## If You Must Use Public Wi-Fi: The Safety Stack

Sometimes there is no choice. In that case, layer these protections:

### 1. Use a reputable VPN

A virtual private network encrypts all traffic between your device and the VPN server, so nobody on the local network can snoop or tamper with it. Choose a well-known paid VPN provider — free VPNs often make money by logging and selling your data, which defeats the purpose. Turn the VPN on before joining the network, and keep it on the whole time.

### 2. Verify HTTPS and the real URL

Before logging in anywhere, check that the address bar shows `https://` and the exact correct domain. Attackers running evil-twin hotspots rely on you not noticing you are on `coindcx-login.com` instead of the real site. Your bookmarks are your friend here, too.

### 3. Forget the network afterwards

After using public Wi-Fi, tell your device to "forget" the network so it does not auto-reconnect next time you walk past. Auto-join is how evil-twin attacks catch people a second time.

### 4. Keep 2FA on everything

If an attacker does capture a password, 2FA still blocks them. This is one more reason the previous lesson's advice matters everywhere.

### 5. Never install anything the network asks you to

Captive portals (the "click to connect" pages) sometimes prompt you to install a "security certificate" or an app. Decline. Installing a stranger's certificate lets them decrypt your HTTPS traffic.

## The Evil Twin Test

Before joining any public hotspot, do a thirty-second sanity check:

- Ask staff for the exact network name. If there are two similar names ("CafeWiFi" and "CafeWiFi_Free"), ask which is real.
- Be suspicious of open networks with no password in places where you would expect one.
- If your device warns about an unsafe network or a certificate problem, believe the warning and disconnect.

## Common Mistakes Beginners Make

- **"It's just a quick check of my balance."** Logging in is the sensitive action — the balance check is what exposes your session.
- **Using public Wi-Fi for password resets.** Email access on a hostile network can cascade into every account linked to that email.
- **Leaving auto-join enabled.** Your phone silently reconnecting to "Airport_WiFi" — which is now an attacker's hotspot — is a classic trap.
- **Trusting a VPN to fix everything.** A VPN protects traffic in transit; it does not protect you from phishing sites, fake apps, or malware already on your device.
- **Doing "just one transaction" because it feels urgent.** Urgency plus an untrusted network is exactly how mistakes happen. Almost nothing in crypto cannot wait an hour.

## India-Specific Notes

- Free Wi-Fi at Indian railway stations, airports, and malls (including government-backed hotspots) is convenient but shared with thousands of strangers — treat all of it as untrusted.
- Mobile data in India is among the cheapest in the world. There is rarely a good reason to risk public Wi-Fi for crypto when a few rupees of mobile data buys a far safer connection.
- Be cautious of SMS OTPs while roaming or on unfamiliar networks; if your phone suddenly loses signal in a public place, consider the possibility of SIM-swap activity and check your accounts from a trusted device.

## Recap Checklist

- I avoid sensitive crypto actions on public Wi-Fi; price checks and reading are fine, transactions wait.
- When out, I prefer mobile data or a phone tether over public hotspots.
- If I must use public Wi-Fi, I use a reputable VPN, verify HTTPS URLs via bookmarks, and forget the network afterwards.
- I never install certificates or apps prompted by a hotspot portal.
- I keep 2FA enabled so a captured password alone is not enough.

Public Wi-Fi safety boils down to one instinct: treat every public network as if a stranger is watching — because one might be. Next, you will put all the security lessons together into a set of best practices you can live by.
