## A New Kind of Money

Bitcoin is the world's first decentralized digital currency. It was created in 2009 by an anonymous person — or group — known only as **Satoshi Nakamoto**. Unlike the rupees in your bank account or the cash in your wallet, Bitcoin is not controlled by any government, bank, or company. Nobody can print more of it, freeze your account, or block your payment.

Think of it this way. When you send money through your bank's app in India, you are really asking your bank to update its private records. The bank is the middleman: it verifies you, moves the numbers, and takes a cut or a few hours to do it. Bitcoin removes that middleman entirely. Instead of one company's private records, Bitcoin uses a shared public record — the **blockchain** — that thousands of computers around the world maintain together.

## Why Was Bitcoin Invented?

Bitcoin appeared in the aftermath of the 2008 global financial crisis. Banks had taken enormous risks with people's money, and when everything collapsed, ordinary savers paid the price while governments bailed out the institutions that caused the mess.

Satoshi's idea was radical: build money that does not need banks at all. Money that no single authority can inflate away, censor, or confiscate. The famous first block of the Bitcoin blockchain even contains a newspaper headline from January 2009 — "Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks" — a quiet timestamp of what Bitcoin was reacting against.

That history matters because it explains Bitcoin's core design goal: **trust through mathematics instead of trust through institutions**.

## How Does Bitcoin Actually Work?

Don't worry — you don't need to understand the cryptography. But the basic flow is worth knowing, because it demystifies every scary headline you'll ever read about crypto.

When you send Bitcoin to someone, here is what happens:

1. **Your wallet signs the transaction.** Your wallet uses a secret number (your *private key*) to create a digital signature — mathematical proof that you own the coins and approve the transfer. This is like signing a cheque, except the signature cannot be forged.
2. **The transaction is broadcast.** Your wallet announces the transaction to the whole network: thousands of computers called *nodes* that each hold a copy of the blockchain.
3. **Miners verify and bundle.** Special network participants called *miners* check that your transaction is valid, bundle it with others into a *block*, and compete to add that block to the chain. This process is called *mining*, and it is what makes the system trustworthy without a central authority.
4. **The ledger is updated.** Once your transaction is in a block, every node updates its copy. The recipient's wallet now shows the Bitcoin — usually within about 10 minutes.

**Try it yourself (mentally):** Imagine you and nine friends each keep a notebook recording who paid whom. Whenever anyone spends money, they announce it aloud, and all ten of you write it down. To change an old entry, a cheater would have to rewrite nine other notebooks — and get caught instantly. That shared-notebook idea *is* the blockchain.

## The 21 Million Rule

There will only ever be **21 million Bitcoin**. This limit is written into the code and cannot be changed without the agreement of the entire network — which makes it practically unchangeable.

This is the opposite of how normal currency works. When the Reserve Bank of India or other central banks expand the money supply, each existing rupee or dollar quietly loses a little purchasing power. Bitcoin's fixed supply is why many people call it **"digital gold"** — not because it is backed by gold, but because, like gold, it is scarce and hard to produce more of.

New Bitcoin enters circulation through mining. Roughly every four years, the reward miners earn for adding a block is cut in half — an event called the **halving**. This predictable, shrinking supply schedule is public and transparent: anyone can verify exactly how much Bitcoin exists and how much will ever exist.

## Bitcoin in India: What a Beginner Should Know

A few India-specific realities, so you learn with your eyes open:

- **Bitcoin is not legal tender in India.** No shop is required to accept it, and it is not an official currency like the rupee.
- **Crypto gains are taxed at 30%** (plus surcharge and cess), and a **1% TDS** is deducted on most crypto transfers on Indian exchanges. This applies regardless of how long you hold.
- **Indian exchanges** like CoinDCX and CoinSwitch let you buy Bitcoin in INR with UPI or bank transfer, after completing KYC (identity verification).
- **The RBI has repeatedly warned** about crypto risks. Reading their guidance alongside this course is sensible.

None of this means you cannot learn about Bitcoin — education is free and completely legal. It just means the Indian context comes with tax paperwork and official caution, which this course will keep reminding you about.

## Common Beginner Mistakes

1. **Thinking Bitcoin is anonymous.** It is not. It is *pseudonymous*: transactions are permanently public, tied to wallet addresses instead of names. Investigators have traced many "anonymous" transactions.
2. **Confusing Bitcoin with "crypto" in general.** Bitcoin is one network with one purpose — being money. The thousands of other coins are separate projects with different designs, and very different risk profiles.
3. **Assuming it is either a guaranteed fortune or an obvious scam.** Bitcoin has survived crashes of 80% or more multiple times and recovered — and it has also left late buyers nursing heavy losses. Treat it as what it is: a high-risk, experimental technology worth understanding.

## Key Properties — A Recap

- **Decentralized:** No company or government runs the network.
- **Limited supply:** A maximum of 21 million coins, ever.
- **Borderless:** Send value to anyone, anywhere, at any time — including across borders, 24/7.
- **Volatile:** The price can swing dramatically. This is an educational course, not a reason to rush into anything.
