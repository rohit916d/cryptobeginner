Most crypto theft does not involve Hollywood-style hacking. Nobody is breaking encryption or cracking blockchains. Instead, attackers simply trick people into handing over their keys, passwords, or signatures voluntarily. That trick has a name: phishing. It is the number one way beginners lose crypto, and this lesson teaches you to see through it.

## What Phishing Actually Is

Phishing is impersonation with a goal. An attacker pretends to be someone you trust — your exchange, your wallet app, a famous crypto personality, even a friend — and convinces you to take an action that gives them access to your funds: clicking a malicious link, entering your seed phrase on a fake site, or signing a malicious transaction.

Think of it like a con artist in the physical world. They do not pick your lock; they knock on your door wearing a uniform and ask you to hand over the keys. The defence is the same in both worlds: verify identity before you trust.

## The Most Common Phishing Tricks in Crypto

### 1. Fake websites

You search for your exchange or wallet, click the top result — and land on a pixel-perfect copy of the real site. You log in, and your credentials go straight to the attacker. These fake sites are often promoted through paid ads, so they appear above the real site in search results.

**Defence:** Bookmark the real sites you use and always navigate via your bookmarks. Check the URL character by character before logging in — attackers use lookalike domains like `coindcx-support.com` or `metamask-secure.io`.

### 2. Fake support staff

You post a question in a Telegram group or on X, and within seconds a "support agent" messages you privately offering help. They are friendly, professional-sounding, and they eventually ask for your seed phrase or ask you to "validate" your wallet on a site they link.

**Defence:** Real support teams never message you first, and no legitimate support agent will ever ask for your seed phrase or private key. Anyone who does is a scammer, with zero exceptions. Ask for help only through official support channels.

### 3. Airdrop and giveaway scams

"Send 0.1 ETH and receive 0.2 ETH back!" or "Claim your free tokens — connect your wallet here!" These promise free money to lure you into connecting your wallet to a malicious site, which then drains it the moment you approve a transaction.

**Defence:** There is no free money. Any offer that asks you to send crypto to receive more crypto is a scam. Before connecting your wallet to any site, verify it through official announcements on the project's real channels.

### 4. Malicious transaction signatures

A dApp asks you to "sign" something to claim a reward or mint an NFT. The signature request is deliberately confusing, and signing it actually grants the attacker permission to move tokens out of your wallet.

**Defence:** Never sign a transaction you do not fully understand. If a site you barely know asks for a signature, close the tab. Legitimate sites explain clearly what each signature does.

### 5. Email and SMS phishing

You receive an official-looking email: "Suspicious login detected — verify your account now." The link leads to a fake login page. SMS versions ("Your OTP is...") try to panic you into clicking.

**Defence:** Never click login links in emails or texts. Open your exchange or wallet independently through your bookmark. Turn on 2FA so a stolen password alone is not enough.

### 6. Impersonation on social media

Fake accounts copy the name, photo, and bio of well-known founders or exchanges, then reply to real posts with scam links. On X and Telegram, the fake often appears directly under the genuine post, which makes it look endorsed.

**Defence:** Check handles carefully, look for verification marks, and remember: genuine projects announce through their official channels only — never through replies asking you to click.

## How to Verify Before You Trust

Make this your reflex sequence whenever crypto and urgency meet:

1. **Stop.** Phishing relies on speed. Slow down.
2. **Check the source.** Is this the official website, app, or handle? Verify the URL or username independently.
3. **Never share secrets.** Seed phrase, private keys, and passwords are never needed by support, giveaways, or "validators."
4. **Use bookmarks.** For every exchange and wallet you use, bookmark the real site once and always go through the bookmark.
5. **When in doubt, ask publicly.** Post your question in the official community channel rather than trusting a DM.

## Common Mistakes Beginners Make

- **Trusting search results blindly.** Paid ads can put fake sites at the top of Google. Bookmarks beat search.
- **Believing urgency.** "Act in 10 minutes or lose access" is manipulation, not customer service.
- **Connecting wallets to random sites** for airdrops or free NFTs. Each connection is a potential open door — disconnect afterwards.
- **Approving signatures without reading.** If you cannot explain what a signature does, do not sign it.
- **Assuming a familiar face means safety.** Video deepfakes and cloned accounts now impersonate real people convincingly. Verify through a second channel.

## India-Specific Notes

- WhatsApp and Telegram are the main phishing battlegrounds for Indian crypto users. Scam groups with names like "CoinDCX Official Support" or "Binance India Signals" are not official — real exchanges do not offer support or signals through random Telegram groups.
- Be wary of UPI-related crypto scams: fraudsters ask for small UPI payments to "activate" an account or "unlock" a bonus. Legitimate platforms never work this way.
- If you receive phishing messages impersonating an Indian exchange, report them to the exchange's official support and to cybercrime.gov.in, India's national cybercrime reporting portal.

## Recap Checklist

- I know phishing means impersonation designed to steal my keys, credentials, or signatures.
- I can name the common tricks: fake sites, fake support, fake giveaways, malicious signatures, phishing emails, social media impersonation.
- I navigate to exchanges and wallets via bookmarks, never via links in messages.
- I never share my seed phrase or private key with anyone, for any reason.
- When something feels urgent or too good to be true, I stop and verify through official channels.

Phishing works because it targets human nature, not technology. The attacker cannot break the cryptography protecting your wallet — so they try to break you instead. Stay sceptical, stay slow, and you stay safe. Next, you will go deeper on the single most valuable secret you own: your seed phrase.
