## The Marketplace of Crypto

In the last lesson you learned that wallets hold your keys. But how do most people *get* crypto in the first place? Through a **crypto exchange** — a platform where you can buy, sell, and trade cryptocurrencies, much like a stockbroker lets you buy shares.

If you have ever used Zerodha or Groww to buy stocks, the basic idea will feel familiar: you create an account, add money, and place orders. The crucial difference is what happens *after* you buy. With stocks, your broker holds them in your demat account under your name, regulated by SEBI. With crypto on an exchange, the exchange holds the private keys — and as you learned: **not your keys, not your coins.**

Exchanges come in two fundamentally different flavors. Understanding both will save you from the most common beginner disasters.

## Centralized Exchanges (CEX): The Familiar Front Door

A centralized exchange is operated by a company. You sign up, complete **KYC** (identity verification — PAN card, Aadhaar-based verification, a selfie), deposit rupees via UPI or bank transfer, and trade.

**Examples Indians commonly use:** CoinDCX, CoinSwitch, and international platforms like Binance. (WazirX was once India's largest, but its troubles are a cautionary tale — more on that below.)

### Why beginners start here

- **Easy onboarding.** UPI deposits, INR pairs (like BTC/INR), and customer support in a language you understand.
- **High liquidity.** Lots of buyers and sellers, so your orders execute quickly at fair prices.
- **Familiar protections.** Password resets, support tickets, and transaction history — like a bank's app.

### The real risks

- **You don't own the keys.** Your crypto sits in the exchange's wallets. You have an *IOU*, not the coins themselves.
- **Hacks happen.** Mt. Gox (2014) lost 850,000 BTC of customer funds. Billions have been stolen from exchanges since.
- **Bankruptcies happen.** FTX, once the world's second-largest exchange, collapsed in 2022 and customer funds vanished into its bankruptcy. Indian users of foreign exchanges have very limited legal recourse.
- **Withdrawals can freeze.** During market panics or regulatory action, exchanges sometimes halt withdrawals — exactly when you most want your money.

None of this means centralized exchanges are evil. They are businesses providing a useful service. It means you should treat them as **a place to transact, not a place to store wealth long-term**.

## Decentralized Exchanges (DEX): Trade Without Permission

A decentralized exchange is not a company at all — it is a set of **smart contracts** running on a blockchain. You connect your own wallet directly, trade peer-to-contract, and disconnect. No account, no KYC, no one holding your funds.

**Examples:** Uniswap, PancakeSwap, Curve.

### Why people use them

- **You keep your keys.** Your crypto never leaves your wallet until the moment of the trade. "Not your keys, not your coins" is automatically satisfied.
- **Permissionless.** Anyone, anywhere, can trade any listed token. No approval needed.
- **No single point of failure.** There is no company to go bankrupt or get raided.

### Why beginners should be careful

- **Steeper learning curve.** You manage gas fees, slippage settings, and wallet connections yourself. One wrong click can send funds to the wrong place — irreversibly.
- **Smart-contract risk.** The code itself can have bugs or be malicious. Audits help but guarantee nothing.
- **No customer support.** If you make a mistake, there is no one to call. The blockchain does not do refunds.
- **Scam tokens.** Anyone can list a token on a DEX, including convincing fakes of real projects. Beginners get drained this way regularly.

## CEX vs. DEX at a Glance

| | Centralized (CEX) | Decentralized (DEX) |
|---|---|---|
| Run by | A company | Smart contracts |
| KYC required | Yes | No |
| Who holds your keys | The exchange | You |
| Ease of use | Beginner-friendly | Technical |
| Customer support | Yes | No |
| Main risks | Hacks, bankruptcy, frozen withdrawals | Scams, user error, contract bugs |

**Try it yourself (mentally):** Before using any exchange, ask: *If this platform disappeared tomorrow, would I still have my crypto?* On a DEX with your own wallet — yes. On a CEX where you left funds sitting — no. That single question tells you where long-term holdings belong.

## Buying Crypto in India: The Practical Picture

For an Indian beginner, the realistic path looks like this:

1. **Choose a reputable Indian exchange** (CoinDCX, CoinSwitch, or similar). Check that it is operational, has real support, and publishes proof of reserves or audits if available.
2. **Complete KYC.** This is legally required — PAN and identity verification. Any "exchange" that lets Indians trade large amounts with zero KYC is a red flag.
3. **Deposit a small amount via UPI.** Start tiny. Your first deposit is for learning, not investing.
4. **Buy, then learn to withdraw.** Practice moving a small amount to your own wallet (from the previous lesson). This single skill separates informed users from gamblers.
5. **Understand the tax before you trade.** India taxes crypto gains at a flat **30%** plus surcharge and cess, with **1% TDS** on transfers. Every trade — even crypto-to-crypto — can be a taxable event. Keep records from day one.

*Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.*

> Red flags that should make you walk away: guaranteed returns, "double your crypto" schemes, exchanges promoted only through Telegram groups, anyone asking for your seed phrase, and platforms with no verifiable company behind them.

## The WazirX Lesson

WazirX was once India's biggest crypto exchange. In 2024 it suffered a major hack, and users discovered how little protection they had: withdrawals frozen, funds stuck, and a long, uncertain recovery process. This wasn't a lecture from a textbook — it happened to lakhs of Indian users.

The lesson isn't "never use exchanges." It is: **never treat an exchange like a savings account.** Buy there, trade there if you must, but move anything you intend to keep into a wallet you control.

## Best Practice — The Pattern to Remember

> **Use a CEX to buy and sell. Withdraw long-term holdings to a self-custody wallet you control.**

This one sentence, followed consistently, would have prevented the majority of beginner losses in crypto history. Exchanges are the marketplace; your wallet is the vault. Don't store the vault's contents in the marketplace.

## Common Beginner Mistakes

1. **Leaving everything on the exchange "for now"** — and forgetting for two years.
2. **Trading on leverage as a beginner.** Exchanges offer 10x, 50x leverage buttons that can wipe out your money in minutes. Ignore them completely while learning.
3. **Falling for fake exchange apps and phishing sites.** Always type the URL yourself or use bookmarks; never click exchange links from DMs or search ads blindly.
4. **Not keeping tax records.** The 30% tax and 1% TDS don't pause while you "figure things out later." Export your trade history regularly.
