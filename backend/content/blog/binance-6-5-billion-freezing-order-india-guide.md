Binance has secured a **$6.5 billion worldwide freezing order** from the Abu Dhabi Global Market (ADGM) Courts, in what its lawyers describe as one of the largest publicly known freezing orders ever issued. The injunction, announced by the exchange on 18 November 2026 and reported by Indian and Gulf outlets through 19 November, is the latest step in the effort to recover cryptocurrency stolen in the record-breaking security breach of 29 October 2026. It targets unidentified perpetrators, covers about 26,000 wallet addresses believed to hold stolen assets, and — unusually — can be served through the blockchain itself. This guide explains what the order actually does, what the $6.5 billion figure does and does not mean, and what Indian users should take away from it.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a court order and a recovery effort; it does not recommend buying, selling or holding any asset.

## What Happened: The Facts at a Glance

The story, as reported on 19 November 2026 by Khaleej Times, The Hindu BusinessLine, The National, Gulf News, Cointelegraph and several other outlets, is:

- **Binance won a $6.5 billion worldwide freezing injunction from the ADGM Courts.** The order was granted by Justice William Stone SBS KC and announced by Binance in a blog post on 18 November 2026. It is a worldwide order: it is not limited to assets held in Abu Dhabi, and it is aimed at "persons unknown" — the unidentified perpetrators of the 29 October breach.
- **It is described as one of the largest freezing orders ever made.** M. Kendall Day of law firm Gibson Dunn, which represents Binance in the recovery proceedings, told reporters the $6.5 billion injunction is "one of, if not the, largest publicly known freezing orders ever made". The figure is roughly 102.5% of the approximately $6.34 billion in assets Binance says the breach affected.
- **About 26,000 wallet addresses have been identified as holding stolen assets.** Binance's lawyers identified the addresses, and the court order provides for a continuously updated list — a "living list" — to be shared with cryptocurrency exchanges, issuers and other market participants, so they can identify and freeze funds connected to the attack as the money moves.
- **The orders can be served through the blockchain.** In an unusual procedural detail, legal notices can be delivered directly to wallet addresses on the blockchain, rather than only through conventional service. That matters because the defendants are unknown: there is no person or company to hand papers to, but there are visible wallet addresses, and the court has accepted that serving those addresses counts.
- **The $6.5 billion is a ceiling, not a recovery tally.** Every outlet that reported the order made the same point: the figure is the amount the injunction covers, not an amount anyone has counted, frozen or returned. Binance has not disclosed the total amount stolen or the total recovered under the orders so far.
- **Binance says affected user assets have been fully replenished.** According to The Hindu BusinessLine's report, Binance has stated that all user assets affected by the October incident have been fully replenished and that the exchange absorbed the financial loss itself. That is the position Binance has held since the days after the breach.
- **US authorities are running a parallel recovery effort.** The US Department of Justice has said it obtained court authorisation to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion in assets stolen from Binance, as part of more than $2.7 billion frozen through official and private-sector efforts combined, according to reporting by Cointelegraph and Finance Biggo. The ADGM order runs alongside that government action; the two figures cannot simply be added together, because the $1.2 billion sits inside the $2.7 billion total.
- **The breach itself remains the largest crypto theft on record.** The heist of 29 October 2026 has been described as the single biggest theft of cryptocurrency ever recorded, exceeding the roughly $1.5 billion stolen from Bybit in February 2025. The FBI has attributed the attack to TraderTraitor, a North Korean state-sponsored hacking unit.

## What a Freezing Order Actually Is

For readers who have never dealt with a court injunction, the mechanics are worth explaining, because the headlines can make a freezing order sound like money in the bank. It is not. It is a legal tool — and a powerful one — but it only works when the money is still somewhere it can be reached.

A freezing injunction, sometimes called a freezing order, is a court order that stops a person from moving, spending or hiding their assets while a legal claim is being decided. In the crypto world, the classic problem is that the thief is unknown and the assets move in minutes. A worldwide freezing order against "persons unknown" solves the first problem by naming the defendants by description rather than by name — whoever carried out the fraud — and the second problem by giving third parties a legal reason to act.

That second part is the practical heart of this order. When stolen crypto lands on an exchange, or when a stablecoin issuer is asked to freeze tokens, the exchange or issuer often wants a formal court order before it will act. Without one, freezing a customer's funds exposes the company to legal risk: what if the funds are not actually stolen? What if the wrong wallet was flagged? Binance explained this in its own announcement of the order, saying that "in some cases, issuers, exchanges or other partners request a formal court order before frozen assets will be released, transferred or returned". The ADGM injunction supplies exactly that: a court-backed instruction that counterparties can rely on when they freeze or return traced funds.

The order also includes additional interim orders aimed at recovery, including orders requiring the delivery of assets, according to the Khaleej Times report. The full order itself has not been published, and no defendant has been named, no return date has been disclosed, and no portion of the $6.5 billion has been identified as located and recoverable. Those gaps are normal at this stage — a freezing order is the beginning of a recovery process, not its conclusion — but they are worth keeping in mind when the headline number is quoted.

## The 26,000-Address Living List

The most technically interesting part of the order is the list of about 26,000 wallet addresses believed to hold stolen assets, and the way it will be maintained.

In most asset-recovery cases, the list of frozen accounts is fixed when the order is made. Here it cannot be, because stolen crypto does not stay still: it is swapped, bridged to other blockchains, split across new wallets, and passed through decentralised exchanges. A fixed list would go stale within hours. The ADGM order therefore provides for a continuously updated list — what Binance and its lawyers call a "living list" — that will be refreshed as new wallet data is identified and shared with market participants.

For the crypto industry, that list is the order's most useful output. An exchange that receives the list can screen deposits against it, freeze matching funds, and refuse to let the assets move on. A stablecoin issuer can check whether tokens in circulation trace back to the theft. Other platforms can use it to decide whether a wallet is safe to deal with. The list turns a single court order into an ongoing, industry-wide screening tool — provided, of course, that the addresses on it are accurate, and that the list is genuinely kept up to date as the attackers keep moving the funds.

There is a limit to what a list can do. The attackers have already moved most of the stolen assets through the laundering routes this site has covered in earlier posts — converting stolen Solana into USDC, bridging it, swapping it into Ethereum and then Bitcoin through THORChain, and spreading it across thousands of wallets. Elliptic's updated report, covered here on 7 November, found that about $3.11 billion of the stolen assets were still sitting in linked addresses, mostly in Ethereum and Bitcoin, while about $2.85 billion had been frozen or seized. The living list helps with the first category — assets that have not moved — and with any new movement that can be traced. It cannot recover money that has already been cashed out through unregulated routes, and it cannot help if an exchange or issuer chooses not to act on it.

## Service Through the Blockchain

The other unusual feature of the order is how it can be delivered. Because the defendants are unknown, there is no office to visit and no person to hand documents to. The ADGM Courts have therefore allowed the orders to be served through the blockchain — legal notices delivered directly to the wallet addresses tied to the stolen assets.

This is not a new idea in crypto law, but it is still rare enough to be notable, and it solves a real problem. A wallet address is, in practice, the only reliable contact point for an unknown thief: whoever controls the private key will see the notice when they next interact with the wallet. Serving the order on-chain also creates a public, verifiable record that the notice was given, which matters if the case later returns to court and the question arises whether the defendants were told.

For ordinary users, the practical point is narrower: if you ever receive an on-chain message claiming to be a court order, treat it with the same caution you would treat any unexpected legal notice. Verify it through the exchange or the court, do not click links embedded in it, and never send funds in response to it. Scammers have already shown they will imitate official communications, and a genuine innovation in legal service is also a new template for fraud.

## What the $6.5 Billion Figure Does and Does Not Mean

The headline number deserves a careful reading, because it is easy to misinterpret in either direction.

What it is: the amount of assets the injunction covers. The court has authorised the freezing of up to $6.5 billion in assets connected to the theft. That figure is slightly above the roughly $6.34 billion Binance says the breach affected, which is why the order is described as covering the full scale of the incident, with a margin for price movements in the underlying assets.

What it is not: a recovery total. Binance has not said how much has been returned under the orders, and no outlet has reported a recovered figure tied to the ADGM order. The numbers that do exist are separate: about $2.85 billion frozen or intercepted as of 31 October, according to Binance's own statement at the time, and more than $1.2 billion under US court authorisation to seize or restrain, as part of more than $2.7 billion frozen through combined official and private efforts. Those are real figures, but they describe different things at different dates, and they should not be added to the $6.5 billion or to each other.

The market's reaction, for what it is worth, was muted. BNB, Binance's own token, traded at about $583 in a CoinGecko snapshot on 19 November, down about 2.35% over 24 hours while Bitcoin fell about 2.77% — the order barely registered against the wider market slide driven by the Federal Reserve's hawkish October minutes, which this site covered on 19 November. That is consistent with how the market has treated the recovery effort all along: the replenishment of user funds was already announced, so a court order that helps the recovery does not change the commercial picture for Binance in the short term. Its value is legal and operational, not financial.

## Why This Story Matters in India

The order was issued in Abu Dhabi, the defendants are unknown, and the theft happened on a global exchange. So why does it matter to an Indian reader? For four reasons.

First, Binance is one of the most widely used exchanges among Indian crypto users, including many who trade through its international platform. An Indian user whose funds were caught up in the October incident has already been told those assets were replenished — but the recovery effort is what makes that promise sustainable. If Binance could not recover the stolen assets, the loss would sit on its own balance sheet permanently. A court order that helps it claw money back is, indirectly, what keeps the replenishment credible.

Second, the case is a live demonstration of the legal machinery that now surrounds crypto crime — and India is building its own version of it. The Enforcement Directorate's searches at CoinDCX and Delta Exchange, covered here on 7 November, were about whether rupee deposits were converted into USDT and routed through foreign exchanges. The ED's Chennai investigation, covered on 6 November, involved more than 600 complaints and over ₹400 crore, with mule bank accounts and crypto used to move the money. The Andhra Pradesh CID case, covered on 5 November, ended with a court order directing seized crypto to be converted into USDT. These are Indian cases, in Indian courts, using Indian legal tools — and the ADGM order shows the same logic applied internationally: identify the wallets, get a court order, share the list, freeze the funds.

Third, the "living list" model is likely to reach Indian exchanges too. When a global list of 26,000 tainted addresses circulates among exchanges and issuers, Indian platforms that receive it will face the same question Binance's counterparties face: freeze matching deposits or risk handling stolen funds. Indian users should therefore expect more, not fewer, questions from exchanges about the source of funds — and should keep records of where their crypto came from, especially for larger deposits. That is already good practice under India's FIU-IND registration regime for virtual asset service providers, and the ADGM list gives it a sharper edge.

Fourth, the order is a reminder that the October hack is not over. The incident itself was covered here on 30 October, the attribution to North Korea on 1 November, the withdrawal reopenings through early November, and the tracing of the stolen funds on 31 October and 7 November. Each of those posts covered one stage of the story. This post covers the next stage: the legal recovery. The story will keep developing — the living list will grow, the DOJ proceedings will advance, and the recovered total will change — and this site will keep covering the stages that matter, rather than repeating the ones already covered.

## What Indian Users Should Do

For the overwhelming majority of Indian users, the freezing order requires no action at all. It targets wallets holding stolen assets, not ordinary accounts, and Binance has said affected user funds were fully replenished. But there are a few practical steps worth taking, especially if you use Binance or any large international exchange.

- **Keep your transaction records.** Save the details of your deposits and withdrawals — dates, amounts, wallet addresses, transaction hashes, and the source of the funds. If an exchange ever asks about the source of a deposit, or if a wallet you used is ever flagged, those records are your evidence that your funds are legitimate.
- **Check before you trust a wallet address.** If someone sends you crypto from an unfamiliar address, or asks you to receive funds on their behalf, pause. The living list of tainted addresses is not public in full, but the principle is: receiving stolen funds, even unknowingly, can get your account frozen while the exchange investigates. Do not act as a pass-through for someone else's crypto.
- **Do not pay anyone who offers to recover funds.** A freezing order does not create a refund scheme, and neither Binance nor any court will contact individual users offering to release frozen funds for a fee. Anyone who claims they can get your money back from the hack, for a payment, is running a scam. Report such approaches on the National Cybercrime Reporting Portal at cybercrime.gov.in or by calling the 1930 helpline.
- **Never share your seed phrase or private key.** No exchange, investigator, court or government official will ever ask for your recovery phrase. Anyone who asks is a scammer, whatever logo or letterhead they use — including one that imitates a court order.
- **Understand the tax position.** The freezing order does not change Indian tax rules. Gains from crypto are taxed at 30%, with 1% TDS on covered transfers, and losses cannot be set off against other income under the current rules. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.
- **Watch the recovery, not the headline.** The $6.5 billion figure will be quoted for weeks. What matters is the recovered total, the size of the living list, and whether exchanges actually freeze matching funds. Those are the numbers to look for in future updates — and the ones this site will report when they change.
