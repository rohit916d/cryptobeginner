Crypto cards in India have gone from a quiet workaround used by a small group of tech-savvy users to a topic the country's financial regulators are now examining closely. According to a report published by The Economic Times on 2 November 2026, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) and the Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU) are looking into offshore prepaid crypto cards that some Indian residents have been using for everyday spending — online shopping, mobile recharges, flight bookings, and even cash withdrawals from ATMs.

If you have seen these cards mentioned online, or wondered whether you could simply load crypto onto a card and spend it like rupees, this guide explains what is actually happening, how the cards work, why regulators are concerned, and what the risks are for Indian users. It is an explainer, not a recommendation: nothing here encourages you to get or use such a card.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto rules in India are complex and still evolving — this guide explains the situation as reported, not what you should do with your money.

## What Is a Crypto Card, Exactly?

A crypto card is a prepaid card, usually on the Visa or Mastercard network, that is funded with cryptocurrency instead of money from a bank account. The card itself looks and behaves like an ordinary debit card at the checkout: the shopkeeper sees a normal card payment. Behind the scenes, however, the balance comes from crypto held with a foreign card provider.

In most cases, the user first deposits crypto — often a stablecoin such as USDT or USDC, which is designed to track the US dollar — into a wallet controlled by the card provider. When the user pays, the provider converts enough crypto into fiat currency to settle the purchase. The merchant receives rupees (or another local currency), and the user's crypto balance falls by the corresponding amount.

These cards are issued by international fintech companies, not by Indian banks. One provider named in the recent reporting is RedotPay, a Hong Kong-based company offering Visa-backed prepaid cards. According to the Economic Times report, such cards typically cost around $40–50, carry a validity of about three years, and come with high daily transaction limits. They are popular in many countries outside India, where spending crypto through a card is an ordinary, lawful activity. The difficulty arises when the same product is used inside India, under Indian law.

## How a Crypto Card Payment Works in India

The journey of a single payment helps explain why regulators find this hard to track. Here is the simplified chain:

1. **You fund the card.** You transfer crypto from your own wallet to a deposit wallet controlled by the card provider. From that point, the provider holds the funds — this is called custodial holding, because you no longer control the private keys.
2. **You pay at a shop or website.** You tap, swipe, or enter the card details exactly as you would with a regular debit card.
3. **The provider converts the crypto.** At the moment of payment, the provider converts the needed amount of crypto into fiat currency.
4. **The merchant is paid in fiat.** The shop receives rupees through the normal card network, so from the merchant's side, nothing looks unusual.
5. **No Indian bank sees the crypto leg.** Because the crypto never passes through an Indian bank account or an Indian exchange, the transaction can escape the checks that normally apply to rupee payments and to crypto trades on Indian platforms.

The Economic Times report notes that some users have even withdrawn cash from ATMs of large Indian banks using physical versions of these cards, with crypto converted behind the scenes to pay the bank the withdrawn amount. It also notes that some card programmes advertise limited or no KYC (identity verification) requirements, which makes the trail even harder to follow.

## Why This Story Broke Now: RBI and FIU Scrutiny

Several threads came together in the last few days, which is why this became news on 2 November rather than remaining a niche topic.

First, according to the report, several Indian residents found over the past few days that their access to prepaid crypto cards and to the websites of card providers had been blocked. Second, since Thursday, social media posts by a number of users have complained that their crypto cards could no longer be used in India. Sources cited by the report did not rule out the possibility that card networks such as Visa may have begun deactivating crypto cards for transactions within India, although this has not been confirmed by the networks themselves.

Third, the scrutiny appears to have been triggered by research from Digital South Trust, a Bengaluru-based blockchain research organisation. According to the report, the organisation obtained a virtual Visa-backed RedotPay card, used it for multiple domestic online transactions in August 2026, and shared the card and purchase details with Indian authorities. Its founder is quoted as saying the organisation independently examined multiple crypto-card programmes, documented their use for everyday payments in India, and submitted its findings to the relevant authorities.

Fourth, the report says the RBI and FIU are understood to be in touch with Visa and Mastercard about the matter. Importantly, neither the payment firms nor the central bank had confirmed this when the report was published, so that part of the story rests on unnamed sources rather than an official statement. Readers should treat it as reported, not confirmed.

Separately, a guide tracking RedotPay's own restriction lists, refreshed on 1 November 2026, records that India is listed among the countries where new virtual and physical card issuance is currently restricted, based on the provider's official lists checked on 25 October 2026. That aligns with the reports of blocked access, though it is a provider restriction rather than an Indian government order.

## Why Regulators Are Concerned

The concern is not about one card or one company. It is about what the product does to several layers of Indian financial regulation at once. Here are the main issues, as explained by the experts quoted in the reporting:

**Crypto is not legal tender in India.** You can own crypto, and you can trade it on compliant platforms, but no shop in India is obliged to accept it, and it cannot officially function as currency. A card that converts crypto into rupees at the checkout effectively lets crypto function as spending money while bypassing that distinction.

**FEMA questions.** The Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA) governs how money crosses India's borders. A foreign crypto debit card, experts quoted in the report argue, effectively holds a wallet balance with a foreign custodian and converts it into fiat for payments in India and abroad. That raises questions about whether the arrangement circumvents rules on foreign exchange, especially since crypto is not a permitted purpose under schemes such as the Liberalised Remittance Scheme for buying crypto abroad.

**Payment-system rules.** A foreign crypto card may also sidestep India's prepaid instrument (PPI) and payment and settlement system (PSS) frameworks, which licensed payment providers in India must follow. A card issued abroad, running on a global network, does not go through the same licensing and oversight as an Indian prepaid card.

**Tax and anti-money-laundering trails.** When crypto is spent through an Indian exchange, the transaction leaves a record: KYC details, a 1% TDS trail, and tax reporting. When it is spent through an offshore card, that trail can disappear. The report notes concern that people with undisclosed crypto could store it in designated wallets and spend it without leaving a pattern visible to banks, tax authorities, or regulators. There is also a broader worry, raised by one legal expert quoted in the report, about the source of funds loaded onto cards — including whether tokens were stolen or hacked — and the ability of agencies to trace token movements using chain-analysis tools.

**Low or no KYC on some programmes.** Some offshore card programmes advertise limited identity checks. Combined with custodial wallets and cross-border conversion, that makes it harder for authorities to know who is spending what, and where the money came from.

None of this means every person who ever held such a card broke the law — the legal position depends on individual facts, and no court ruling on these specific cards is cited in the reporting. But the combination of issues explains why the RBI and FIU are paying attention now, rather than treating this as a harmless gadget.

## What It Means If You Already Have One

If you already hold an offshore crypto card, the practical message from the recent reporting is caution, not panic — but also not complacency:

- **Access can change without warning.** Cards and provider websites have reportedly been blocked for some Indian users in recent days. A card that worked last week may not work tomorrow, and there is no Indian regulator you can complain to if a foreign provider restricts your account.
- **Do not treat it as a bank account.** Money held with a card provider is custodial: the provider controls it. If the provider restricts your account, you may face a slow or difficult recovery process. Keeping only small amounts on such a card, if you use one at all, limits that exposure.
- **Keep complete records.** Save every deposit, payment, fee, and conversion. Spending crypto can be a taxable event, and you will need the records whether the card keeps working or not.
- **Do not use a card to hide income or holdings.** The reporting explicitly notes that agencies can use chain-analysis tools to trace token movements. Using an offshore card does not make crypto invisible, and attempting to hide income can create far bigger legal problems than the tax itself.
- **Watch for copycat sellers.** Whenever a product like this is in the news, scammers appear selling fake cards, fake top-ups, or "activation" services. Never share your wallet seed phrase or private keys with anyone offering a card — legitimate providers never need them.

If you are unsure about your own position, the safest step is to speak to a chartered accountant or lawyer who understands both crypto tax and FEMA, rather than relying on forum advice or a card provider's marketing.

## Crypto Cards vs the Ordinary Ways Indians Use Crypto

The table below compares the offshore card route with the routes most Indian beginners use. The point is not that one is "better" — it is that the ordinary routes leave a clearer legal and tax trail, which is exactly what regulators want.

| Route | How you spend or convert | Who holds your funds | Paper trail in India |
|---|---|---|---|
| Offshore crypto card | Crypto converted to fiat at checkout by a foreign provider | Foreign card provider (custodial) | Weak or absent; KYC may be limited |
| FIU-IND registered Indian exchange | Sell crypto for INR on the exchange, withdraw to your bank account | Exchange until withdrawal, then your bank | KYC, 1% TDS, bank record |
| Your own wallet + Indian exchange | Move crypto to the exchange only when you want to sell | You control your wallet until you transfer | Transfer record plus exchange record |
| Spending crypto directly with a merchant | Not an accepted payment method in India | Depends on the arrangement | No recognised framework |

For most Indian beginners, the second and third rows are the practical, compliant path: hold crypto in your own wallet or on a registered exchange, and convert to rupees through that exchange when you need to. It is slower than tapping a card, but it keeps your funds under your control and your records clean.

## The Tax Angle Indian Users Cannot Ignore

Every time you spend crypto — including through a card — you are generally disposing of it. That matters because India taxes crypto gains at a flat 30%, with 1% TDS applying to many transfers, and losses cannot be set off against other income. A card payment that converts stablecoins may produce little or no gain, since stablecoins are designed to hold their value, but the transaction still needs recording, and any gain on other coins spent through a card is taxable in the same way as a sale on an exchange.

Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

There is a second tax risk specific to cards: if payments bypass Indian platforms entirely, the 1% TDS that would normally be deducted may never be collected, and the income may never appear in your tax records. That does not make the tax go away — it makes the eventual correction messier. Keep a simple spreadsheet of every card-funded payment, with the date, the coin spent, the amount, and the rupee value at the time, so your tax filing matches reality.

## The Bigger Picture: Stablecoins Meet Traditional Card Rails

It is worth noting that crypto cards are not a purely Indian phenomenon. Globally, card networks have reported rapid growth in stablecoin-linked card spending, with Visa disclosing, as cited in the reporting, more than 150% growth in volume across 5 million cards and a stablecoin settlement run rate above $25 billion a year. One expert quoted in the report describes these cards less as a parallel payment system and more as an added funding layer on traditional networks — crypto balances converted into local currency at the point of sale.

That global growth is precisely why Indian regulators are unlikely to ignore the issue. If stablecoin cards become a routine way to spend money worldwide, the question for India becomes whether to build a regulated route for them, block them, or leave them in the grey zone they currently occupy. No answer to that question has been announced. For now, the only confirmed facts are the scrutiny itself, the reported access blocks, and the provider-side issuance restrictions — not a new law, not a ban, and not an approval.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are crypto cards legal in India?

Crypto itself is not banned in India, but it is not legal tender, and paying for goods with crypto raises questions under FEMA and India's payment rules. The RBI and FIU are examining offshore crypto cards, and some users have recently reported blocked access. There is no official approval for these cards in India, so using one carries legal and practical uncertainty. If in doubt, speak to a qualified professional before using one.

### What happens if my crypto card stops working in India?

A card issued by a foreign provider can be restricted or deactivated without warning, and any balance held with that provider may be hard to recover quickly. That is why beginners should never load large amounts onto an offshore card or treat it as a bank account. Keep records of every deposit and payment, and keep most of your crypto in a wallet or on a platform you can access independently of the card.

### Do I have to pay tax when I spend crypto with a card in India?

Spending crypto is generally treated as disposing of it, which can trigger tax on any gain, and India's crypto tax framework applies a flat 30% rate on gains with 1% TDS on many transfers, with no set-off of losses. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing, and keep a record of every card payment.

## The Bottom Line

Crypto cards in India sit at the intersection of three things that do not yet fit together neatly: a global product that works well abroad, an Indian legal framework that does not recognise crypto as currency, and regulators who are now actively examining how the product is used on Indian soil. The Economic Times report of 2 November 2026 does not announce a ban, and it does not quote any official RBI or FIU statement — it reports scrutiny, blocked access for some users, and possible card-network deactivations, all of which remain partly unconfirmed.

For beginners, the practical takeaway is simple. Understand how these cards work before you are tempted by one, keep your funds and records under your own control, use FIU-IND registered exchanges for converting crypto to rupees, and do not treat an offshore card as a safe or approved way to spend crypto in India. If the rules change — and in this space, they often do — a clear paper trail and a cautious approach will serve you far better than a card that worked quietly until it suddenly did not.
