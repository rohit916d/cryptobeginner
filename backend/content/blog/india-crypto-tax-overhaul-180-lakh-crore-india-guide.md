## India Crypto Tax Overhaul: ₹1.80 Lakh Crore at Stake

A new report by the TIOL Knowledge Foundation (TKF) says reforming India's tax framework for virtual digital assets could generate ₹1,80,814 crore in capital gains tax over five years — more than three times the ₹56,800 crore the current framework is expected to raise. The reason, the report argues, is that the current rules have already pushed almost all Indian crypto trading out of the country: nearly 95% of India's virtual digital asset trading volume, worth about ₹9 lakh crore, moved offshore in the 2025–26 financial year. This guide explains what the report found, what it recommends, what its numbers are — and are not — and what has not changed for anyone trading crypto in India today.

## What the TIOL Report Actually Says

The report is the second edition of the TIOL Knowledge Foundation's study titled "Impact of Taxation on Virtual Digital Assets in India", and its findings were carried by ANI and reported across Indian media on 28 November 2026. Its headline figures, as reported, are:

- **₹1,80,814 crore vs ₹56,800 crore.** A reformed tax framework could generate ₹1,80,814 crore in capital gains tax over five years, compared with about ₹56,800 crore under the existing regime.
- **95% of trading is offshore.** Nearly 95% of India's virtual digital asset trading volume, worth about ₹9 lakh crore, moved offshore in FY 2025–26. Domestic tax-compliant exchanges accounted for just 5.4% of trading volume, valued at ₹51,446 crore.
- **₹57,147 crore already lost.** The shift offshore has cost the exchequer an estimated ₹57,147 crore in uncollected TDS and capital gains tax since July 2022. That includes ₹8,172 crore in TDS alone during the nine months since October 2025.
- **Offshore volumes could keep growing.** If the current framework stays unchanged, the report projects offshore trading volumes could reach ₹15.27 lakh crore by FY 2030–31.
- **One platform's Indian traffic surged.** The report found that unique Indian visitors to one leading offshore platform rose 73% between July 2025 and April 2026, while purchase conversion on that platform increased 3.6 times over the same period.

The report's argument is not that Indians stopped trading crypto. It is that they kept trading — just somewhere the Indian tax system cannot easily see. That turns the tax question from a debate about how much to charge into a debate about where the activity happens at all.

## Why Trading Moved Offshore: The 1% TDS and 30% Tax Combination

The report blames two features of India's current framework, working together. Understanding them is the key to the whole story.

**The 1% TDS applies to the full trade value, not the profit.** TDS — Tax Deducted at Source — is deducted at 1% of the total value of a crypto transfer, not just the gain. For a frequent trader, that 1% is taken on every trade, locking up working capital again and again even when most trades made little profit. The TDS can later be claimed as a credit when filing a return, but in the meantime the money sits with the government — and for an active trader, the amount stuck can become very large.

**Losses cannot be set off.** Under the current rules, losses on one crypto trade cannot be used to reduce the tax on gains from another trade, and losses cannot be carried forward to a later year. A trader who gains ₹1 lakh on one coin and loses ₹1 lakh on another still pays 30% tax on the ₹1 lakh gain, as if the loss never happened. This is unusual: in most other investments, losses can at least partly offset gains. For crypto, the rule is flat — every gain is taxed at 30%, and every loss is yours alone.

**The combination has no parallel, the report says.** The study compared 15 major global crypto markets and found no comparable tax combination anywhere else — a 1% transaction-level TDS plus a flat 30% capital gains tax with no loss set-off. That comparison is the report's most striking claim, and also its hardest to check independently, because the report does not name the 15 markets or show the comparison itself.

**Enforcement did not bring traders back.** The report notes that the Financial Intelligence Unit blocked 25 offshore exchanges in October 2025 — but users largely shifted to other offshore platforms and peer-to-peer transactions instead of returning to domestic exchanges. Blocking platforms did not change the maths that made offshore trading cheaper, so the activity simply moved elsewhere. That is the report's strongest evidence for its central point: enforcement alone, without reform, does not bring trading onshore.

## What the Report Recommends

The report also proposes a package of changes, each aimed at a specific reason traders leave:

| Recommendation | What it would change | Why the report wants it |
|---|---|---|
| Cut TDS from 1% to 0.01% | The deduction on each trade falls a hundredfold | Makes trading on compliant Indian exchanges practical again for active traders |
| Allow loss set-off and carry-forward | Losses could reduce tax on gains, and be carried to later years | Removes the rule that taxes gains while ignoring losses |
| Review the 30% capital gains rate | The flat rate itself would be reconsidered | Brings crypto closer to how other assets are taxed |
| Clarify offshore and P2P tax obligations | Clear rules for trades on offshore platforms and peer-to-peer deals | Closes the grey area where offshore activity escapes the tax net |
| Mandate a local presence for platforms serving Indian users | Offshore platforms would need an Indian footprint | Makes enforcement possible instead of relying on blocking orders |
| Strengthen enforcement and reporting | Tighter reporting requirements alongside the reforms | Pairs reform with visibility, so lower rates do not mean less oversight |

The report's own modelling breaks the revenue gain into steps. Cutting TDS from 1% to 0.01% alone, it estimates, could bring domestic compliance back to roughly 59% of trading volume and generate about ₹1,25,506 crore in cumulative capital gains tax over five years. Adding loss set-offs and a lower capital gains rate takes the estimate to the headline ₹1,80,814 crore. That is the full package — the ₹1.80 lakh crore figure depends on all of the reforms happening together, not on any single change.

## The ₹1.80 Lakh Crore Number: What It Is and What It Is Not

A number this large deserves careful handling, because it is easy to read as a promise when it is actually a projection.

**It is a scenario, not a forecast.** The figures come from the report's own modelling of what could happen if its recommendations were adopted and if a large share of offshore trading returned to Indian exchanges. The report's estimates assume that lower taxes bring activity back onshore, that the activity then generates taxable gains, and that the tax system captures them. Each of those steps is an assumption, and the report does not publish its methodology, its data sources, the identity of the 15 markets compared, or the share of offshore volume it assumes would return. Independent coverage of the report has flagged exactly this gap: the evidence about what has already happened is the strongest part of the document, while the projection of what would happen is the part a reader cannot verify.

**It is not a government figure.** The TIOL Knowledge Foundation is a research foundation, not a government body. Its numbers are not the Finance Ministry's numbers, and no government agency has endorsed the ₹1.80 lakh crore estimate. The report is best understood as an advocacy document — a researched case for reform, made by an organisation that wants the rules changed — rather than as official data.

## What Has Not Changed: The Rules Today Are the Same

For anyone trading crypto in India right now, the most important part of this story is what it does not change: the report is a proposal, and nothing in the law has moved.

- **The 30% flat tax still applies.** Gains on virtual digital assets are taxed at a flat 30%, with no loss set-off and no carry-forward. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.
- **The 1% TDS still applies.** Transfers above the threshold still attract 1% TDS, deducted by the exchange or the buyer. The proposed 0.01% rate is a recommendation, not a notification.
- **No bill or budget announcement has adopted the report.** Parliament has not passed anything and the Finance Ministry has not announced a change.
- **Filing obligations are unchanged.** Income from crypto must still be reported, TDS credits must still be claimed through the normal return, and the records you keep this year should assume the current rules apply.

That gap — between a widely reported recommendation and an unchanged law — is where beginners get hurt. A headline saying a tax "could" be cut is not the same as a tax being cut. Until an official announcement changes the rules, the current framework is the one that applies.

## Why This Story Landed Now

The report itself was published a few days before the 28 November coverage, and an opinion piece in Moneycontrol on the same findings appeared on 26 November. The fresh news on 28 November is the ANI wire carrying the report's findings into mainstream Indian media — which is why the numbers are suddenly in front of a much wider audience.

The timing also fits a pattern. Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin warned at the Devcon 8 conference in Mumbai earlier in November that the same 30% tax and 1% TDS could burden smaller developers and discourage innovation. His warning was about developers; this report is about traders. Together they describe the same pressure from two sides: the tax framework is squeezing both the people who build crypto in India and the people who trade it there.

## What Indian Crypto Users Should Do Now

The report gives Indian users something practical, even though the law has not changed: a clearer picture of how the system currently works against them, and what to watch for.

- **Keep using FIU-registered exchanges where you can.** A registered exchange deducts TDS and reports to the authorities, so your trades leave a clean paper trail for your tax return. Offshore platforms may look cheaper, but they create filing headaches and exposure to enforcement action against offshore routing.
- **Keep proper records of every trade.** With no loss set-off, your return depends on accurate gain figures trade by trade. Save exchange statements, TDS certificates and transfer records, and keep them in one place so filing is a matter of adding up rather than reconstructing.
- **Do not trade on the assumption that TDS will fall to 0.01%.** The recommendation may be adopted, modified or ignored — plan your costs on the 1% rate that exists today.
- **Watch the Budget, not the headlines.** Tax changes arrive through the Union Budget or a Finance Ministry notification, not through a research report. The next real signal will be a Budget speech, a bill or an official statement.
- **Remember the FEMA angle.** Trading on offshore platforms already raises foreign-exchange questions under FEMA, and the Enforcement Directorate's searches at Indian exchanges this year have focused on exactly that offshore routing. A tax reform would not, by itself, settle those questions.
