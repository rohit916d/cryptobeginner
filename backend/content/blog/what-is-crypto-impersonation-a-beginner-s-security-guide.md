In cryptocurrency, the most important security skill is not technical — it is psychological. While hackers do attack code, a huge share of crypto theft happens through **social engineering**: tricking people into willingly handing over their assets or credentials. The most common form of this is **impersonation** — a scammer pretending to be someone trustworthy.

Because blockchain transactions are permanent and irreversible, impersonation scams are devastating. There is no bank to call, no chargeback to file. But the good news is that these scams all follow recognisable patterns. Once you learn the playbook, you become very hard to fool.

> **Educational note:** This is a security-awareness guide, not financial advice. Staying informed is your best defence.

## What Is Crypto Impersonation?

Crypto impersonation happens when a scammer poses as a trusted person or organisation to trick you into sending cryptocurrency or revealing sensitive credentials like your seed phrase.

Impersonators typically pretend to be:

- **Customer support agents** from wallets, exchanges, or blockchain networks — "helping" you resolve an issue.
- **Founders or executives** of well-known crypto projects — lending credibility to fake announcements.
- **Giveaway hosts** — promising to double any crypto you send them, supposedly as a promotion.
- **Friends or community members** — hijacked accounts of people you actually know, asking for "urgent help."
- **Regulatory or tax officials** — in India, scammers have posed as tax authorities demanding crypto payments to "clear" supposed dues.

The common thread: **borrowed trust**. The scammer does not need to hack anything if they can convince you to hand over the keys yourself.

## The Scammer's Playbook: 6 Tactics to Recognise

### 1. Fake social media accounts

Scammers create X (Twitter), Telegram, Discord, or Instagram accounts that look nearly identical to real projects or influencers. The tricks are subtle: a lowercase "l" swapped for an uppercase "I," an extra underscore, or the exact same profile picture on an account created last week. Always check the *exact* handle, follower count, and account age — not just the display name and photo.

### 2. The unsolicited DM

You post a question in a public crypto group or tag a project about a problem. Within minutes, "Official Support" messages you privately offering to help. **Real support staff almost never DM you first.** This is one of the most reliable red flags in crypto. If someone messages you first claiming to be support, assume it is a scam until proven otherwise through official channels.

### 3. Too-good-to-be-true giveaways

A verified-looking account announces that a famous entrepreneur is giving away crores in Bitcoin. To "verify your wallet" or "cover network fees," you must first send a small amount to a specified address — and you will supposedly receive double back. The address belongs to the scammer, the doubling never happens, and your coins are gone permanently. **No legitimate giveaway in history has required you to send money first.**

### 4. Fake support websites and apps

Scammers buy ads or manipulate search results so that a fake "support" site ranks above the real one. The site looks professional, has a live chat, and asks you to "validate your wallet" by entering your seed phrase. Bookmark the real sites of exchanges you use — CoinDCX, CoinSwitch, or whichever you prefer — and never arrive at them through ads, DMs, or social media links.

### 5. Urgency and fear

"Your account will be frozen in 30 minutes unless you verify now." "Suspicious activity detected — transfer your funds to this safe address immediately." Scammers manufacture panic because panicked people do not think clearly. **Any message demanding immediate action with your crypto is a manipulation tactic.** Legitimate companies do not operate this way.

### 6. The "helpful" stranger with a script

In community groups, scammers pose as fellow users who had "the same problem" and were helped by a specific support agent (who is also the scammer, on another account). They will even walk you through steps that end with you sharing your screen or entering your seed phrase somewhere. Be deeply suspicious of strangers who are *too* eager to help with your wallet problems.

## Your Defence Checklist

Protecting yourself requires habits, not expertise. Internalise these:

1. **Never share your seed phrase or private keys.** Not with support, not with founders, not with anyone, ever. No legitimate person will ever ask. Write this on paper if you must — it is the single most important rule in crypto.
2. **Verify through official channels only.** Have a problem? Go to the exchange's official website yourself (via your bookmark) and open a support ticket there. Do not trust DMs, reply-thread links, or search ads.
3. **Inspect handles carefully.** Check spelling, follower counts, join dates, and verification badges. When in doubt, find the link from the project's official website, not from social media.
4. **Slow down.** Urgency is the scammer's weapon. When you feel rushed, that is precisely the moment to stop, close the chat, and verify independently. A real issue will still be there in an hour.
5. **Keep support interactions in official tickets.** If "support" wants to move the conversation to Telegram or WhatsApp, that is a red flag. Real support stays in the official system.
6. **Educate the people around you.** In India, many victims are first-time users brought in by friends or family. Share these patterns with anyone you introduce to crypto — it takes five minutes and can save someone's savings.

## What to Do If You Have Been Targeted

If you already interacted with a suspected impersonator:

- **If you shared your seed phrase:** assume the wallet is compromised. Immediately create a brand-new wallet (new seed phrase, generated offline if possible) and transfer any remaining funds to it. Do this quickly — attackers often drain wallets within minutes.
- **If you sent crypto to a scammer:** unfortunately, the transaction cannot be reversed. You can report the receiving address to the exchange involved and file a complaint on India's cybercrime portal (cybercrime.gov.in), but recovery is rare. Document everything for the report.
- **If you only chatted but shared nothing:** block and report the account, warn others in the community group, and take it as a free lesson.

Do not feel ashamed — these scams are professionally designed and catch experienced users too. The only mistake is not learning from it.

## Why This Matters More in Crypto Than Anywhere Else

In traditional banking, fraud has safety nets: you can freeze a card, dispute a charge, and rely on the bank's fraud team. Crypto's greatest strength — no middlemen, final settlement — is also what makes impersonation so costly. **You are your own bank, which means you are also your own fraud department.**

That sounds daunting, but notice something: every defence on the checklist above is free, takes seconds, and requires no technical skill. Skepticism is the cheapest security tool ever invented.
