# Binance Reopens More Withdrawals: What India Must Know

Binance is reopening withdrawals in a second wave. After restoring Bitcoin withdrawals first on 1 November, the exchange said on Monday 2 November that it would reopen withdrawals for more assets across several more networks from 04:00 GMT on Tuesday 3 November — that is 09:30 IST on Tuesday morning. For Indian users who have been waiting to move coins off the exchange since the 29 October security incident, this is the next practical milestone in a recovery that has so far moved in careful phases rather than all at once.

This guide explains what the second wave covers, what is still paused, why Binance is reopening in stages instead of flipping one big switch, and what Indian users should check before they move anything. It is an explainer, not a recommendation, and it makes no prediction about prices, about Binance, or about any coin.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose.

## What Happened, in Brief

On 29 October 2026, roughly $6.3 billion of digital assets were moved from Binance-linked wallets in what became the largest exchange security incident on record. Binance paused withdrawals across several networks as a precaution, promised to cover user losses from company funds and its SAFU safety fund, and began a phased restoration.

The first phase arrived on Sunday 1 November: Bitcoin withdrawals first, then Ether and major stablecoins on selected networks, with Binance saying about 95% of the affected assets had been replaced. That phase was covered in our earlier guide on the resumption. The 3 November wave is the next step in the same plan — more networks, more assets, still in stages.

Understanding that this is one continuous recovery, not a series of separate events, matters for beginners. Each wave restores a specific set of routes after security checks on those routes. A coin that is back on one network may still be paused on another, and a route that is open today may still face delays if the exchange's checks flag something.

## Which Networks and Assets Are in the Second Wave?

According to Reuters' report of 2 November, Binance said it would reopen withdrawals for more assets on the BSC, ETH, SOL, XRP and SUI networks, beginning at 04:00 GMT on Tuesday 3 November. Some crypto assets on the ARBITRUM, OPTIMISM, TRX and BASE networks were also due to resume at the same time.

In plain language, here is what those network names mean for a beginner:

- **BSC (BNB Smart Chain)** is the network behind BNB, Binance's own chain, and a large family of tokens that run on it.
- **ETH (Ethereum)** is the network behind Ether and the majority of stablecoins and tokens in circulation.
- **SOL (Solana)** is the network behind SOL and its fast-growing token ecosystem.
- **XRP (XRP Ledger)** is the network behind XRP, a coin widely held by Indian users.
- **SUI (Sui)** is a newer network behind the SUI token.
- **ARBITRUM, OPTIMISM and BASE** are layer-2 networks built on top of Ethereum, designed to make transactions cheaper and faster.
- **TRX (Tron)** is the network behind TRX and a large share of USDT transfers.

Gadgets 360's 2 November coverage added practical detail to the same announcement, noting that Binance had already resumed USDT, USDC and ETH withdrawals on Ethereum, alongside BNB withdrawals on BNB Smart Chain, as part of this widening reopening. The two reports agree on the direction of travel: the reopening is broadening from the first phase's Bitcoin-led set to a much wider range of assets and networks.

One important caveat: "more assets on a network" does not mean "every asset on that network." Binance has consistently described the restoration as asset-by-asset, with each coin's withdrawal status depending on its own wallet checks. If you hold a smaller token on one of these networks, its route may reopen later than the network's headline coins. The only reliable check is the withdrawal page for your specific asset, or Binance's official status announcements — not a social media post claiming that everything is back.

## Why Reopen in Phases at All?

Beginners often ask why an exchange cannot simply turn everything back on once it says the problem is fixed. The answer is that a phased reopening is itself a security measure, and understanding it helps you read every future incident more calmly.

First, each network has its own wallets, its own hot-wallet and cold-wallet setup, and its own set of smart contracts and bridges. A security team cannot sign off on all of them with one check; each route needs its own audit, its own key rotation, and its own test transactions before real user funds flow through it again.

Second, a phased reopening limits the damage if something is still wrong. If a route that looked clean turns out to have a problem, only the funds on that route are exposed, and the exchange can pause that route again without shutting down everything else. It is slower for users, but it is deliberately slower.

Third, phased reopening protects the exchange's own operations. A sudden rush of withdrawals across every asset at once — the pattern seen in the first hours after the 1 November reopening, when Reuters reported $3.64 billion of net outflows — strains systems, creates queues, and makes it harder to spot genuine problems among the noise. Spreading the reopening over days keeps volumes manageable and makes anomalies easier to see.

For users, the practical lesson is patience with the process. A paused route is not necessarily a sign of a new problem; it may simply be a route whose checks are not finished yet. Equally, a reopened route is not a guarantee that nothing will ever go wrong again — it is a statement that, as of that check, the route met the exchange's criteria.

## What Is Still Paused, and What Is Still Unknown?

As of the 2 November announcement, Binance had not declared the recovery complete. Some wallets were still described as under audit, and the exchange had warned of possible delays even on reopened routes. The remaining roughly 5% of affected assets — described by Binance as illiquid assets, metacoins and assets being sourced from the open market and other exchanges — had not been fully replaced at the time of the first phase.

Beginners should also keep two wider uncertainties in view. The first is the investigation itself: the FBI has attributed the theft to North Korean actors under the TraderTraitor label, but the full forensic picture, including how the attackers moved funds through decentralised platforms and instant-swap services, is still being assembled. The second is the proof-of-reserves picture: Binance promised a full zero-knowledge proof-of-reserves verification, due around 3 November, which will let users check whether the exchange's claimed backing matches its actual holdings. Until that verification is published and independently examined, the 95% figure remains the exchange's own claim, not an audited fact.

None of this means Indian users should panic. It means they should treat the recovery as ongoing, verify claims against official sources, and avoid making irreversible moves — such as sending a large amount to an untested address — on the basis of a headline alone.

## What Indian Users Should Check Before Moving Funds

If you are an Indian user planning to withdraw in this second wave, work through this checklist before you press confirm.

**1. Confirm your exact asset and network are open.** Do not rely on the network name alone. Open the withdrawal page for your specific coin, check that its network is listed as available, and read any notice Binance shows for that asset. If your coin is on a network in the second wave but the coin itself is still paused, wait — forcing a transaction through an unsupported route is how funds get stuck.

**2. Test with a small amount first.** This is the single most valuable habit in crypto, and it costs almost nothing. Send a small test withdrawal to your destination wallet, confirm it arrives, and only then send the larger amount. A test catches wrong addresses, wrong networks, and incompatible wallets before they become expensive mistakes.

**3. Double-check the destination address and network match.** The most common beginner loss is not a hack at all — it is sending coins on the wrong network to an address that cannot receive them. USDT on Tron is not the same as USDT on Ethereum, even though the coin name looks identical. Copy the address from your wallet, paste it carefully, and confirm the network selection matches the wallet you are sending to.

**4. Expect queues and delays.** Binance has warned that reopened routes may still face delays, and the first phase showed that withdrawal demand spikes immediately after a reopening. If your withdrawal sits in "processing" for a while, check the official status page before assuming something is wrong — and never re-send the same withdrawal while the first one is still pending, or you may double-send.

**5. Beware of fake "reopening" messages.** Every major incident spawns impersonators. Expect phishing emails, fake support accounts, and Telegram or WhatsApp messages claiming that Binance needs you to "verify" your wallet to unlock withdrawals. Binance will never ask for your recovery phrase, your password, or a payment to release your funds. If a message asks for any of those, it is a scam.

**6. Keep records for tax filing.** Moving crypto from an exchange to your own wallet is not itself a sale, but you still need records: the date, the asset, the amount, the network, and any fee. If you later sell, those records are how you establish your purchase history. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

**7. Decide where the coins go, not just that they leave.** Withdrawing to a wallet you control is only safer if you actually know how to secure that wallet. If you have never used self-custody before, learn the basics — recovery phrases, offline storage, and test transactions — with a small amount first, rather than moving everything in one anxious rush. ## The India Angle: Offshore Exchanges, Local Rules, and Caution

The Binance recovery also lands in a moment of heightened Indian regulatory attention. Just a day before this announcement, The Economic Times reported that the RBI and the FIU-IND were examining offshore prepaid crypto cards, with some users reporting that their cards had stopped working and some provider websites blocked in India. The cards story is separate from the Binance incident, but it carries the same lesson for Indian users: offshore crypto services do not always work in India the way they work elsewhere, and regulators here are watching them closely.

For beginners, the practical takeaway is simple. Use FIU-IND registered Indian exchanges for new purchases where possible, since registration — while not a safety guarantee — means the platform is within the Indian compliance net. Treat offshore platforms as tools with extra homework attached: check whether the service is actually available to Indian residents, what happens to your funds if access is restricted, and how you would recover them. And keep your own records regardless of which platform you use, because Indian tax rules apply to your gains no matter where the trade happened.

None of this is a reason to rush. If your coins are on Binance and your asset's route is now open, you have time to withdraw carefully. If your asset is still paused, waiting is not losing — it is the exchange finishing its checks. The worst outcomes in incidents like this rarely come from waiting a day; they come from rushed transactions, wrong networks, and phishing messages that exploit urgency.

## How to Read the Next Headlines

The Binance story will keep generating headlines for weeks: the proof-of-reserves verification, the remaining asset replacement, outflow figures, and investigation updates. Here is how to read them without being whipsawed.

First, separate **routes** from **totals**. A headline about one network reopening tells you about that route; a headline about total outflows tells you about user behaviour across the whole exchange. They answer different questions, and neither one alone tells you whether your coins are safe.

Second, separate **claims** from **verifications**. When Binance says it has replaced 95% of assets, that is a claim. When an independent proof-of-reserves verification is published and examined, that is a verification. Both are useful, but only the second can be checked by outsiders.

Third, separate **your situation** from **the market's situation**. A $3.64 billion outflow figure describes the exchange as a whole; it does not describe your account. Your questions are narrower and more answerable: Is my asset's route open? Did my test withdrawal arrive? Do I control the destination wallet? Answer those, and the market headlines matter much less.

## The Bottom Line

Binance's second withdrawal wave, from 04:00 GMT on 3 November, extends the phased recovery from Bitcoin and the first set of major assets to a much wider range of networks — BSC, ETH, SOL, XRP and SUI, plus selected assets on ARBITRUM, OPTIMISM, TRX and BASE. It is a real step forward, and it is still a step, not the finish line: some wallets remain under audit, the proof-of-reserves verification is still due, and delays on reopened routes remain possible.

For Indian users, the right response is calm and procedural: confirm your exact asset and network are open, test with a small amount, match address and network carefully, expect queues, ignore phishing messages that exploit the reopening, keep tax records, and only move funds to a wallet you know how to secure. The recovery rewards patience and precision far more than speed.
