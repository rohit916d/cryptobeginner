Devcon 8 Mumbai is under way. On 3 November 2026, Ethereum's flagship developer conference opened at the Jio World Centre in Mumbai's Bandra Kurla Complex, running for four days until 6 November — and for the first time in the event's history, it is being held in India. Hundreds of developers, researchers, designers, and open-source contributors from India and around the world have gathered in one city to talk about the technology underneath the world's second-largest cryptocurrency network.

If you are an Indian beginner, you may be wondering why a developer conference in Mumbai should matter to you. The short answer is that Devcon is where the future of Ethereum gets discussed in public — which upgrades are coming, which problems builders are worried about, and which ideas are still just ideas. Understanding that difference is one of the most useful skills a crypto beginner can build, because headlines from conference week often sound like finished news when they are really just the start of a long process.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a public event; it does not recommend any coin or investment.

## What Is Devcon, Exactly?

Devcon is the Ethereum Foundation's global event for the communities building Ethereum and other open-source, decentralised technologies. The Ethereum Foundation is the non-profit organisation that supports the Ethereum ecosystem, and Devcon is its main stage: a four-day programme of talks, panels, workshops, and informal discussion.

It helps to be clear about what Devcon is not. It is not a trading expo, not a place where coins are launched for investors, and not a seminar promising returns. The audience is mostly people who write code, audit code, research cryptography, design wallets and apps, or run community projects. The sessions are technical by design — but the themes they cover eventually reach every ordinary user, because they decide how the network you use will work in the years ahead.

This year's programme spans nine tracks: Core Protocol, Privacy & Consent, Security, Futures Worth Building, Users, Builders & Agents, Rights, Freedoms & Governance, Applied Cryptography, Permissionless Networks, and Open & Verifiable Stack. In plain language, that means the agenda covers everything from the deepest engine room of Ethereum to the everyday questions of privacy, safety, and how ordinary people actually use decentralised apps.

More than 300 speakers and contributors are featured this year, including Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin, Ethereum Foundation researcher Justin Drake, Polygon co-founder Sandeep Nailwal, Tor Project co-founder Roger Dingledine, and former Taiwan digital affairs minister Audrey Tang. For Indian attendees, the presence of builders such as Nailwal alongside global names underlines how much of Ethereum's story is now written from India as well as from anywhere else.

## Why Mumbai, and Why Now?

Devcon has travelled the world in past years, but it had never come to India before 2026. The choice of Mumbai reflects something Indian beginners should feel good about: India is now one of the largest and most active crypto developer communities in the world, with a deep bench of engineers, students, and startups building on Ethereum and related networks.

The timing is deliberate too. India Blockchain Week ran on 1–2 November, immediately before Devcon, and the months leading up to the conference included Road to Devcon initiatives supporting local projects, academic communities, events, and contributors across India. In other words, Devcon week is not a single event dropped into Mumbai from outside — it is the visible peak of months of India-focused activity, designed to extend participation beyond the four days of the conference itself.

For a beginner, this matters for a practical reason: when a global event of this size comes to your own country, its coverage becomes far easier to follow. Indian media, Indian creators, and Indian developer communities are all reporting on it in real time, in time zones that match yours, with local context that a distant conference would never get. If you have ever felt that crypto news happens somewhere else, Devcon 8 Mumbai is the week that argument stops working.

## What Is on the Agenda?

Three agenda items are drawing the most attention this week, and each one teaches a different lesson about how Ethereum actually changes.

### The Ether issuance debate (4 November)

On Wednesday 4 November, Devcon hosts a roundtable on Ethereum's issuance policy — the rules that decide how much new ETH is created and paid to validators, the network participants who secure the chain. The session will present arguments on both sides of a draft proposal known as EIP-8363, titled "Tapered Issuance Burn", which would burn more validator rewards as the share of ETH staked rises.

This is worth understanding carefully, because it is easy to misread. A roundtable is a discussion, not a decision. EIP-8363 is a draft proposal, its design is unsettled, and the session is explicitly set up to air arguments for and against changing issuance — not to approve a change. Nothing discussed on 4 November automatically becomes a rule of the network. Ethereum changes through a long process of testing, debate, and agreement among developers, node operators, and the wider community, and many proposals never reach the live network at all.

For beginners, the useful takeaway is the concept, not the outcome: issuance policy affects how much new ETH enters circulation and how staking rewards work, which is why even a debate about it attracts attention. Follow the arguments, not the hype, and remember that a proposal and a protocol change are two very different things.

### The Glamsterdam upgrade — delayed, with no new date

The second big talking point is a delay. Ethereum's next major upgrade, known as Glamsterdam, has no new mainnet date after an October notice postponed a testnet activation on the Hoodi testnet. Testnets are practice networks where upgrades are trialled before they touch real user funds, so a postponed testnet step means the whole upgrade timeline moves back.

This is normal, and it is actually a sign of caution working as intended. Ethereum upgrades are not product launches with marketing deadlines; they are changes to a network holding enormous value, and rushing one would be far riskier than delaying it. Beginners should treat "delayed with no new date" as information, not as failure — and should be sceptical of anyone who turns an upgrade delay into a reason to panic or, just as misleadingly, into a buying opportunity.

### Privacy, security, and quantum questions

Beyond the two headline items, the programme gives heavy weight to privacy, security, applied cryptography, and the longer-term question of quantum security for Ethereum's future. These are the quiet topics that matter most to ordinary users over time: how to keep transactions private without breaking the law, how to make wallets and apps harder to attack, and how to prepare a network built on today's cryptography for a future in which far more powerful computers exist.

None of these sessions will produce a headline you can trade on, and that is precisely the point. Devcon's value is in the slow, unglamorous work of making a network safer and more usable — the kind of work that rarely trends on social media but decides whether a technology survives for decades.

## What Devcon 8 Means for Indian Beginners

So what should you actually do with Devcon week? Here is a practical, hype-free way to follow it.

First, follow outcomes, not announcements. When a session ends, ask: was this a proposal, a test result, a debate, or a finished change? Only the last of those affects how Ethereum works today. Most of what you will read this week falls into the first three categories, and that is fine — it is still useful to know what builders are thinking, as long as you do not mistake thinking for shipping.

Second, use the India angle. Indian speakers, Indian projects, and Indian developer communities are a large part of this Devcon. If you are learning, this is a good week to discover Indian builders, follow their talks, and find communities — online and in your own city — where you can keep learning after the conference ends. The Road to Devcon initiatives across India show that this ecosystem existed long before the conference arrived; Devcon just put a spotlight on it.

Third, beware the scams that cluster around big events. Whenever a major crypto event trends, fake accounts appear offering "Devcon airdrops", discounted tickets through unofficial links, or giveaways in the name of well-known speakers. The Ethereum Foundation does not run surprise giveaways, and no genuine Devcon ticket or reward will ever ask you to send crypto first or share your wallet's secret phrase. If an offer arrives through a forwarded message or a suspicious account, ignore it — the same caution applies here as with any trending token or too-good-to-be-true scheme.

## Tickets, Prices, and the India Tax Angle

If you are curious about attending a future Devcon rather than this one, the ticket structure is instructive. The official ticket shop lists prices in US dollars, including 18% GST. For this Mumbai edition, verified Indian residents could buy an India Resident ticket for $149 — roughly ₹14,140 — and approved Indian students for $25, roughly ₹2,373. General admission cost $499 when paid in ETH, or $999 by card, according to published ticket guides for the event.

That price gap carries a tax lesson worth knowing. Paying for something with ETH is not like paying with rupees: under Indian tax law, spending a virtual digital asset counts as a transfer of that asset, so any gain over your purchase cost can be taxed at 30% plus cess, with 1% TDS rules also in play for certain transfers. In other words, the cheaper ETH ticket price could still come with a tax bill that a card payment would not create.

Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

For most beginners, the sensible way to follow Devcon is free: read the coverage, watch published talks afterwards, and join local learning communities. You do not need a ticket to learn from a conference whose whole purpose is open, public discussion.

## How to Follow Devcon Without Attending

If you cannot be in Mumbai this week, you can still follow the event well. Look for summaries from reputable crypto publications and from the Ethereum Foundation's own channels, and prefer reports that distinguish clearly between proposals and confirmed changes. Watch for the outcome of the 4 November issuance roundtable, any new signal on the Glamsterdam testnet timeline, and the security and privacy sessions — those are the threads most likely to matter for the network's future.

It also helps to read Devcon coverage alongside what you already know about Ethereum itself. If terms like staking, validators, or testnets are still fuzzy, this is a perfect week to firm them up, because every Devcon headline will use them. A beginner who understands those three words will understand roughly half of conference week.

Finally, keep perspective. Devcon is a developer conference, not a market event. Its sessions will not tell you what ETH will be worth next week, next month, or next year, and anyone claiming otherwise is selling you something. What Devcon can tell you is what the people building Ethereum are worried about, excited about, and working on — and for a beginner planning to learn this space properly, that is far more valuable than any price call.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is Devcon?

Devcon is the Ethereum Foundation's flagship gathering for the people who build Ethereum and other open-source decentralised systems — developers, researchers, designers, and community contributors. It is a working conference of talks, workshops, and discussions about the technology itself, not a trading event or an investment seminar.

### Do I need to attend Devcon to benefit from it?

No. The talks and discussions from Devcon are widely reported and usually published online afterwards, so a beginner can follow the important outcomes — such as upgrade timelines and security discussions — from home. Attending in person is mainly valuable for builders who want to meet collaborators, ask questions directly, and take part in workshops.

### Will Devcon announcements change the price of ETH?

Nobody can say, and this guide makes no prediction. Most Devcon sessions discuss proposals, research, and work in progress — not finished changes — and even a confirmed upgrade can take months or years to reach the live network. Treat Devcon as a window into what Ethereum's builders are working on, not as a signal to buy or sell.

## The Bottom Line

Devcon 8 Mumbai matters because it brings the world's most important Ethereum conversation to India for the first time — four days, from 3 to 6 November, at the Jio World Centre, with the developers, researchers, and builders who actually shape the network. The week will produce debates, not decisions: an issuance roundtable on 4 November that airs both sides of a draft proposal, a Glamsterdam upgrade still waiting on a new testnet date, and deep work on privacy, security, and the network's long-term future.

For Indian beginners, the opportunity is not a trade — it is a front-row seat, in your own time zone, to how a major blockchain really evolves. Follow the proposals, learn the vocabulary, ignore the scams that ride on the event's name, and remember the golden rule of conference week: a discussion is not a decision, and a proposal is not a protocol change. The builders in Mumbai this week are doing slow, careful work. Learning to tell that work apart from the noise around it is one of the best crypto skills you can build.
