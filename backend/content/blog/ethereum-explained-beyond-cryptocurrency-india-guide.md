Ask most people about Ethereum and they will tell you it is "the second-biggest cryptocurrency after Bitcoin." That is true, but it misses the real story. Ethereum explained properly is not just about a coin called Ether (ETH) that goes up and down in price. Ethereum is a programmable platform — a kind of shared, global computer that anyone can build applications on. That one difference, programmability, is why Ethereum powers decentralised finance (DeFi), non-fungible tokens (NFTs), stablecoins and thousands of apps that have nothing to do with simply sending money.

If you are an Indian beginner trying to understand crypto beyond the headlines, Ethereum is the second concept worth learning properly, right after Bitcoin itself. This guide explains what Ethereum actually is, how it differs from Bitcoin, what smart contracts and gas fees mean in plain language, and what risks you should understand before you ever buy a single rupee's worth of ETH.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This article explains how Ethereum works; it does not recommend that you buy Ether or any other asset.

## Ethereum in One Simple Analogy

Think of Bitcoin as digital cash — or, as many people describe it, digital gold. Its main job is to be a store of value and a way to transfer value without a bank in the middle. It does that one job deliberately and conservatively.

Ethereum is closer to a smartphone. A basic phone makes calls; a smartphone runs apps. Ethereum's blockchain does not just record "A sent money to B." It can run small programs, called smart contracts, that automatically carry out agreements when conditions are met. A vending machine is a helpful comparison: you insert money, press a button, and the machine gives you the item — no shopkeeper needed. A smart contract is like that vending machine, but for digital agreements: "if this happens, then do that," executed by code instead of by a person or a company.

Ether (ETH) is the fuel for this system. Every action on Ethereum — sending ETH, using an app, minting an NFT — costs a small amount of ETH as a fee, called gas. So ETH is both an asset people hold and the currency the network itself runs on.

## A Brief History, Without the Hype

Ethereum was proposed in 2013 by programmer Vitalik Buterin, who argued that Bitcoin's blockchain was too limited because it could not easily run general-purpose programs. A team of co-founders built the network, and it went live in 2015. From the start, the goal was broader than payments: a "world computer" where developers could build decentralised applications, often called dApps.

Two milestones matter for a beginner. First, Ethereum popularised the idea of tokens — new digital assets created on top of an existing blockchain. Many of the stablecoins, DeFi tokens and NFTs you hear about were first built on Ethereum using shared technical standards, which is why they work across so many wallets and apps. Second, in September 2022 Ethereum completed "The Merge," switching from energy-intensive mining (proof-of-work) to proof-of-stake, where validators lock up ETH to secure the network. That change cut the network's energy use dramatically and changed how new ETH enters circulation.

None of this is a promise about price. It simply explains why Ethereum occupies a different category from Bitcoin in most people's mental map of crypto.

## Bitcoin vs Ethereum: The Key Differences

Beginners often ask which one is "better." That is the wrong question — they were built for different purposes. This comparison should help:

| Feature | Bitcoin (BTC) | Ethereum (ETH) |
|---------|---------------|----------------|
| Main purpose | Digital money and store of value | Programmable platform for apps and contracts |
| Launched | 2009 | 2015 |
| What runs on it | Mainly payments and holdings | DeFi, NFTs, stablecoins, games, dApps |
| Supply | Capped at 21 million BTC | No fixed cap; issuance changes with network rules |
| Network security | Proof-of-work (mining) | Proof-of-stake (staking) since 2022 |
| Typical beginner description | Digital gold | Digital oil / app platform |

Bitcoin's fixed supply is central to its "digital gold" story. Ethereum's supply has no hard cap, although its fee system burns — permanently destroys — a portion of every transaction fee, which can reduce supply growth when the network is busy. The practical takeaway is not that one supply model wins, but that the two assets respond to different forces. Bitcoin's value story is mostly about scarcity and adoption as money. Ethereum's value story is tied to how much people actually use the platform.

## What Are Smart Contracts, Really?

Smart contracts are the heart of Ethereum, so let us slow down on them. A smart contract is a program stored on the blockchain that runs exactly as written when triggered. Once deployed, it operates without a company server in the middle, and its rules are visible for anyone to inspect.

A simple real-world style example: imagine two strangers in India agreeing that ₹10,000 will be released to a freelancer only when a website is delivered and approved. Traditionally, you might trust an escrow service or just hope the other side behaves. A smart contract could hold the funds and release them automatically when the agreed digital condition is met. No office, no paperwork chase, no "payment is processing" phone calls.

Scale that idea up and you get the building blocks of DeFi: contracts that lend, borrow, swap tokens and pay out interest according to code. You also get NFTs, where a contract records unique ownership of a digital item, and decentralised autonomous organisations (DAOs), where token holders vote on decisions through contracts.

The strength is also the weakness. Code that runs automatically will also run a mistake automatically. Bugs in smart contracts have caused very large losses in crypto's history, and there is usually no customer support desk to reverse a transaction. That is why experienced users treat new or unaudited apps with caution, and why beginners should not rush into DeFi just because it is built on Ethereum.

## Gas Fees: Why Using Ethereum Costs Money

Every action on Ethereum costs gas, paid in ETH. Think of gas as a postage charge: the network's validators do work to process and secure your transaction, and the fee pays for that work. Simple ETH transfers cost little gas; complex actions, like interacting with a DeFi app, cost more.

The catch is that gas prices rise and fall with demand. When the network is crowded — during a popular NFT launch or a volatile market day, for example — fees can spike sharply, sometimes making small transactions uneconomical. When the network is quiet, fees fall. This is very different from UPI, where a transfer in India is effectively instant and free for the user. On Ethereum, you are bidding for limited space in the next block.

To reduce costs, a whole ecosystem of "Layer 2" networks has grown around Ethereum. These process transactions off the main chain in batches and then settle them back to Ethereum for security, offering lower fees and faster confirmations. Many beginners will encounter these names inside wallet apps. The key point for now: if a fee looks surprisingly high, you may be using the main network at a busy moment, and alternatives may exist — but always verify you are using a legitimate network and app before moving funds.

## How Indians Typically Encounter Ethereum

For most Indian beginners, the first contact is simple: ETH appears as the second option on exchanges like CoinDCX, WazirX and ZebPay, right next to Bitcoin. You can deposit INR via UPI or bank transfer after completing KYC with your PAN and Aadhaar-linked details, and buy a small amount of ETH in exactly the same flow as buying BTC.

Beyond holding ETH, Ethereum is usually the network behind your first steps into wider crypto. If you move crypto to your own wallet, you will likely meet Ethereum addresses (they start with "0x"), choose a network when withdrawing, and discover that sending tokens on the wrong network can mean losing them. That practical layer is where many beginners get hurt — not by price moves, but by copy-paste mistakes, wrong-network withdrawals and fake apps. Go slowly, test with tiny amounts, and double-check addresses.

Tax applies here too, just as it does to Bitcoin. Profits on selling ETH are taxed under India's crypto rules, and TDS can apply on transactions. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. Keep records of every buy and sell from day one; reconstructing them later is miserable.

## The Honest Risks of Ethereum

A balanced Ethereum explainer has to name the risks plainly:

**Price volatility.** ETH has historically seen very large rises and falls, including drawdowns of more than half its value. Prices fluctuate significantly, sometimes within a single day. Any money in ETH must be money you can afford to lose entirely.

**Complexity risk.** Bitcoin mostly asks you to buy, hold and secure a key. Ethereum invites you to *do things* — connect wallets to apps, approve token permissions, bridge between networks. Every extra action is a new way to make a mistake or be scammed.

**Smart contract and app risk.** The base network and the apps built on it are different layers. An app can fail, be hacked or turn out to be a scam even if Ethereum itself keeps running. "It is on Ethereum" is not a safety certificate.

**Regulatory change.** Rules in India and globally continue to evolve for crypto platforms and DeFi. Changes can affect taxes, exchange access and how apps operate. Do not assume today's setup is permanent.

Understanding these risks does not mean Ethereum is "bad." It means the extra power comes with extra responsibility, and a beginner's job is to learn the system before scaling any involvement with it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is Ethereum the same as Ether (ETH)?**
Not quite. Ethereum is the name of the network — the blockchain and platform. Ether, shortened to ETH, is the cryptocurrency that runs on that network. You pay fees and use apps in ETH, just as you use rupees inside India's banking system, but rupees are not the banking system itself.

**Can I buy a small amount of Ethereum in India?**
Yes. Like Bitcoin, Ether is divisible into very small units, so you do not need to buy one full ETH. Indian exchanges such as CoinDCX, WazirX and ZebPay let you start with small amounts, often a few hundred rupees via UPI, after completing KYC. Start small while you are learning how deposits, orders and wallets work.

**Is Ethereum safer or riskier than Bitcoin for a beginner?**
Ethereum is generally considered riskier because it does more. Its value depends not just on people holding ETH, but on demand for apps, smart contracts and DeFi built on the network — and bugs or failures in those apps can affect users. That extra usefulness comes with extra complexity, so beginners should understand both before putting in any money they cannot afford to lose.

## The Bottom Line

Ethereum explained in one line: Bitcoin digitised money; Ethereum digitised agreements and applications. ETH is the fuel and asset of that system, smart contracts are its engine, and gas is the price of using it. For an Indian beginner, you do not need to use DeFi, buy NFTs or run a validator to benefit from understanding this. You just need a clear mental model before hype — or fear — makes decisions for you.
