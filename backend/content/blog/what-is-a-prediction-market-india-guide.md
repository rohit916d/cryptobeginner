Imagine the night before a big cricket final. Millions of fans have an opinion about who will win — but only a few are willing to put money behind it. A prediction market is a place where those opinions become prices. People buy and sell shares tied to the outcome of a future event, and the price of each share tells you what the crowd thinks will happen. A share priced at 62 means the market, taken together, puts the chance of that outcome at roughly 62%.

Prediction markets have grown fast in the last few years, especially crypto-based ones such as Polymarket and regulated ones such as Kalshi. They now cover elections, sports, court cases, central-bank decisions and crypto prices — and their odds are quoted in the news almost as often as opinion polls. This guide explains how they work, why people pay attention to them, what can go wrong, and why they are effectively off-limits for readers in India.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Prediction markets involve risking real money on uncertain outcomes — you can lose your entire stake. This guide explains how they work; it does not recommend using, joining or trading on any platform.

## What Is a Prediction Market?

A prediction market is a marketplace for bets on the future. Instead of buying shares in a company, you buy shares in an outcome. Every market asks a yes-or-no question with a clear resolution date, such as:

- "Will India win the next T20 World Cup?"
- "Will the US Federal Reserve cut interest rates in December?"
- "Will Bitcoin trade above a certain price on 31 December?"

Each market has two sides: **Yes** and **No**. Shares in each side trade between 0 and 100 (or the local-currency equivalent, such as ₹0 to ₹100). If the event happens, Yes shares pay out 100 and No shares pay out nothing. If it does not happen, the reverse is true.

The key idea is that the **price is a probability**. If Yes shares for "India wins the World Cup" trade at 62, the market is saying there is roughly a 62% chance of that happening. If good news arrives — a star player recovers from injury — the price might rise to 70. If bad news arrives, it might fall to 50. The price is simply the crowd's running estimate, updated every second by people putting money behind their views.

## How Prediction Markets Work: Shares, Prices and Payouts

Here is a simple worked example. Suppose a market asks, "Will India win the next T20 World Cup?" and Yes shares are trading at 62.

1. **You buy 10 Yes shares at 62.** You pay ₹620 in total (10 × ₹62).
2. **India wins the tournament.** Each Yes share pays out ₹100, so you receive ₹1,000. Your profit is ₹380.
3. **India loses instead.** Each Yes share pays out nothing. You lose the full ₹620.

That is the whole mechanic: buy low, hope the event resolves in your favour, and collect 100 per share — or lose everything you put in. There is no partial recovery. This all-or-nothing payout is the first thing a beginner must understand, because it makes prediction markets very different from buying an asset that usually keeps some value.

Most platforms take a small fee on trades or on winnings, and some charge for deposits and withdrawals. On crypto-based platforms, you may also pay blockchain network fees to move funds in and out. These costs are small per trade but add up quickly if you trade often.

## How Prices Move — and What They Actually Mean

Prices move because people trade on new information. There are two common ways a market sets prices:

- **Order book:** buyers and sellers post the prices they are willing to trade at, and trades happen when a buyer and seller agree. This is the same system stock exchanges use.
- **Automated market maker (AMM):** a pool of money prices shares automatically using a formula. The more people buy Yes, the more expensive Yes becomes — and the cheaper No gets.

Either way, the price at any moment is the market's best guess. That is why prediction markets are often called forecasting tools: when thousands of people with different information and incentives trade against each other, the resulting price is sometimes more accurate than a single expert's opinion or a poll.

But a price is not a prediction in the fortune-teller sense. A market priced at 62 does not mean the event "will" happen — it means the crowd thinks it will happen about six times out of ten. Four times out of ten, it will not. Treating a 62% price as a certainty is the most common beginner mistake, and it is an expensive one.

## Types of Prediction Markets

Prediction markets cover far more than elections. The main categories are:

| Category | Example questions | Why people watch them |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Politics | Election winners, referendum results, cabinet picks | Real-time alternative to opinion polls |
| Sports | Match winners, tournament champions, player records | Huge, liquid markets with constant news flow |
| Crypto prices | Will Bitcoin close above a level by a date? | Traders hedge or speculate on price moves |
| Economics | Interest-rate decisions, inflation prints, GDP | Faster signal than official forecasts |
| Courts and law | Verdicts, regulatory decisions, approvals | Insiders and lawyers trade on public filings |
| Entertainment | Award winners, box-office results, show outcomes | Fun markets, but often thin and easy to move |

Crypto-based platforms such as Polymarket run on blockchains and settle in stablecoins, which is why they attract crypto users. Regulated platforms such as Kalshi operate under US financial regulation and settle in dollars. The mechanics are the same on both; the legal status and the user base are different.

## Prediction Markets vs Betting vs the Stock Market

Beginners often confuse prediction markets with the other two. The comparison below shows where they overlap and where they differ.

| Feature | Prediction market | Sports betting | Stock market |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What you buy | Shares in an outcome | A bet with a bookmaker | Shares in a company |
| Payout | Fixed 100 per winning share, 0 otherwise | Fixed odds set by the bookmaker | No fixed payout; value rises or falls |
| Price set by | Other traders | The bookmaker | Other traders |
| Can you sell early? | Yes, at the current price | Usually only via cash-out | Yes, at the current price |
| Underlying value | Nothing — the share expires | Nothing — the bet expires | A real business with earnings |
| Typical holding period | Days to months | Hours to days | Months to years |

The most important row is the last-but-one. A share in a company keeps some value even on a bad day. A prediction-market share in the wrong outcome is worth exactly zero when the event resolves. That is why the risk profile is closer to betting than to investing, whatever the platform's marketing says.

## Why Do People Use Prediction Markets?

People use these markets for three reasons, and only one of them is about making money:

- **Forecasting:** journalists, researchers and businesses read the prices as a live estimate of what will happen. During elections, prediction-market odds are now quoted alongside polls.
- **Hedging:** someone exposed to an outcome can offset the risk. A business that loses money if interest rates rise, for example, could buy shares that pay out if rates rise.
- **Speculation:** traders buy shares they believe are mispriced — a 62 that they think should be 75 — and sell when the price corrects.

The forecasting use is the one with genuine social value. The speculation use is where most beginners lose money, because they are competing against traders who follow the news full-time, use automated tools and understand the mechanics better than a casual user does.

## The Risks Beginners Must Understand

Prediction markets look simple, which makes them dangerous. The main risks are:

- **You can lose your entire stake.** A wrong outcome pays zero. There is no "it will come back" as there is with a stock.
- **Thin markets move easily.** In a small market, one large trader can push the price from 40 to 70 in minutes. A price that looks like strong evidence may just be one person's bet.
- **Insider trading is a real problem.** People with non-public information — government officials, employees, people close to an event — have traded on what they knew before the public did. In November 2026, the US House Oversight Committee released a memorandum after a six-month investigation into exactly this behaviour on major platforms, and recommended Congress consider banning officials from trading on them.
- **Resolution can be disputed.** If the outcome is ambiguous — a match abandoned by rain, an election result challenged in court — the platform decides how to settle. That decision can go against you even if you read the question correctly.
- **Scams and fake platforms.** Fake prediction-market sites, fake "insider tips" groups on Telegram and WhatsApp, and cloned apps are common. They promise guaranteed wins and then steal deposits. No legitimate platform guarantees a return.
- **Addiction and overtrading.** Because markets resolve quickly and new markets appear constantly, it is easy to keep trading — and keep losing — far beyond what you planned.
- **No investor protection.** Unlike a bank deposit or a regulated stockbroker account, there is no deposit insurance on a prediction-market platform. If the platform fails, freezes withdrawals or turns out to be fraudulent, your money may simply be gone.

## Prediction Markets and India: What You Need to Know

For readers in India, the position is straightforward: **there is no regulated prediction-market framework in India, and you should treat offshore prediction markets as off-limits.**

Online betting and gambling are largely prohibited under state laws in India, and prediction markets sit squarely in that grey-to-prohibited zone. Sending money abroad to place bets would also breach India's foreign-exchange rules, which restrict how Indian residents can use foreign currency. No FIU-IND registered Indian exchange offers prediction markets, and no Indian regulator licenses them.

That does not mean the topic is irrelevant to you. Prediction-market prices are public information — you can read Polymarket or Kalshi odds in the news without opening an account or spending a rupee. Used that way, they are a useful forecasting signal: one more data point alongside polls, expert analysis and official data. What you should not do is treat them as a loophole, join through a VPN, or send crypto to an offshore platform to trade. The legal risk is yours, and the platform will not protect you if something goes wrong.

Any gains from offshore betting would not escape Indian tax law, and the reporting and foreign-exchange complications are a further reason to stay away.

## How to Read Prediction-Market Odds Without Trading

You can get most of the value of prediction markets for free, as a reader. Here is a practical routine:

1. **Check the price, then check the volume.** A 62% price on a market with thousands of traders means more than the same price on a market with twelve.
2. **Look at how the price moved.** A price that jumped after a news event is reacting to information. A price that drifted slowly may just reflect low activity.
3. **Compare with other sources.** If polls say one thing and the market says another, ask why — the difference is often where the interesting information is.
4. **Remember the base rate.** A 62% price means the event fails about four times in ten. Never read a favourite's price as a guarantee.
5. **Ignore social-media "sure things".** Screenshots of winning trades and "insider" tips are marketing, not evidence. Nobody posts their losing slips.

## 5-Minute Safety Checklist

If you ever encounter a prediction-market platform or a group promoting one, run through this checklist first:

- [ ] Is the platform regulated in any country you can verify — or is it an unregulated offshore site?
- [ ] Does it ask you to deposit crypto to an address you cannot verify?
- [ ] Is someone promising guaranteed returns or "insider" outcomes? (That is a scam.)
- [ ] Would using it from India breach gambling or foreign-exchange rules? (Assume yes unless a qualified professional tells you otherwise.)
- [ ] Could you afford to lose 100% of whatever you put in? (If not, do not put it in.)

If any answer worries you, the correct move is to walk away. There is no prediction-market opportunity so good that it justifies breaking the law or risking money you need.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a prediction market in simple terms?

A prediction market is a platform where people buy and sell shares tied to the outcome of a future event — an election, a cricket match, a court verdict or a crypto price. Each share is priced between 0 and 100, and the price works like a probability: a share trading at 62 means the market collectively puts the chance of that outcome at about 62%. When the event resolves, shares in the correct outcome pay out 100 and the others become worthless.

### Can Indians legally use prediction markets like Polymarket?

There is no regulated prediction-market framework in India, and online betting and gambling are largely prohibited under state laws. Sending money abroad to place bets on offshore platforms would also breach India's foreign exchange rules. Indian users should treat offshore prediction markets as off-limits. You can still read prediction-market odds as public information — the prices are visible without trading — but participating from India is not a legal route.

### Is a prediction market the same as gambling?

It shares the core feature of gambling: you risk money on an uncertain outcome, and you can lose your entire stake. Supporters argue prediction markets also produce useful forecasts, because prices reflect the crowd's best guess about the future. Regulators in many countries still treat them as betting or derivatives, which is why access is restricted in several places, including the United States for some platforms and effectively in India. The safest way to think of them is as a form of betting with an information side-effect — not as investing.

## The Bottom Line

Prediction markets are a clever idea: turn opinions into prices, and the price becomes a live forecast of the future. For journalists and researchers, the odds are a genuinely useful signal. For traders, they are a high-risk, all-or-nothing instrument where the crowd is often smarter than any single participant — and where insiders, thin markets and disputed resolutions can turn a good read into a total loss.

For readers in India, the practical conclusion is simple. Read the odds, learn from them, and leave the trading to people in jurisdictions where it is legal and regulated. The most valuable thing a prediction market can give you costs nothing: a clearer sense of what the world thinks will happen next — and how often the world is wrong.
