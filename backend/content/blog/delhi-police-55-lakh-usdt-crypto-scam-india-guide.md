# Delhi Police Bust ₹55 Lakh USDT Crypto Scam in India

Delhi Police arrested five people on 19 November 2026 for allegedly cheating a Delhi man of ₹55 lakh in a cryptocurrency scam built around USDT, the dollar-pegged stablecoin Tether. The case is a textbook example of a trust-first fraud: the gang completed one real deal to earn the victim's confidence, then took a much larger cash payment and emptied his wallet.

The arrests were made by the Special Staff of Delhi Police's South-East District, which says it broke up a multi-layered syndicate operating across Delhi, Uttar Pradesh and Haryana. The five accused were picked up from different cities, and police recovered 16 mobile phones, 11 SIM cards, a vehicle and ₹3 lakh in cash from them.

This guide explains what happened, how the trap worked step by step, what the police case says, and — most importantly — how Indian crypto users can spot the same pattern before it costs them money.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto transactions are irreversible in most cases, and cash deals with strangers carry serious risk. This article explains a reported fraud case so you can recognise the pattern — it is not investment guidance.

## What Happened: The Case in Brief

The complaint was filed by Rahul, a resident of Mangolpuri in Delhi, at Amar Colony police station on 4 October 2026. According to police, he had been lured through social media advertisements promising substantial returns on cryptocurrency investments, and was put in touch with a man calling himself Krishna Agarwal — a pseudonym police say was used by Ritik Chawla, one of the accused.

The sequence of events, as reported by Delhi Police and carried by PTI and other outlets on 19 November, was:

| Date | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| Early October 2026 | Rahul sees social media ads promising returns on USDT investments and is contacted by "Krishna Agarwal" |
| 3 October 2026 | Rahul pays ₹36 lakh in cash and receives 36,000 USDT in his Trust Wallet — the deal completes normally |
| 4 October 2026 | Rahul pays ₹55 lakh in cash for 55,000 USDT at a meeting in Amar Colony; the USDT is transferred to his wallet |
| 4 October 2026 | The accused withdraw the cryptocurrency from Rahul's wallet shortly after the transfer |
| 4 October 2026 | FIR No. 0372/2026 registered at Amar Colony police station under Sections 318(4) and 3(5) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) |
| 19 November 2026 | Delhi Police announce five arrests and the recovery of phones, SIM cards, a vehicle and ₹3 lakh in cash |

The Times of India added a detail that explains why the first deal felt so convincing: the gang initially handed over the 36,000 USDT at a loss of about ₹1.5 lakh to gain the complainant's confidence. In other words, the fraudsters were willing to lose a little money on the first transaction because they expected to make far more on the second one. That is the economics of a trust-first scam — the first deal is not the business, it is the bait.

It is important to be precise about the legal status here. The five men have been arrested and a case has been registered, but these are allegations at this stage. An arrest is not a conviction, and the charges will be tested in court. This article reports what police have said and what has been published; it does not treat the allegations as proven facts.

## Who Was Arrested

Police identified the five arrested accused as:

- **Omprakash**, a resident of Faridabad, Haryana
- **Aman** (30), from Rohini, Delhi
- **Ritik Chawla** (26), from Ghaziabad, Uttar Pradesh — police say he posed as "Krishna Agarwal" to lure the complainant
- **Gaurav Rana**, from Meerut, Uttar Pradesh — police identify him as the alleged kingpin who coordinated the syndicate
- **Saurabh alias Chhotu** (23), from Muzaffarnagar, Uttar Pradesh

The geography matters. The gang operated across three states — Delhi, Uttar Pradesh and Haryana — which is why police describe it as an interstate syndicate. Multi-state operations make investigation harder: victims, phones, bank accounts and meeting points are spread across different police jurisdictions, and a single district's police force has to coordinate across state lines to make arrests.

The recoveries also tell a story. Sixteen mobile phones and 11 SIM cards suggest the group used many different numbers and devices — consistent with police saying the network used illegally procured SIM cards and international WhatsApp accounts to contact victims. Multiple phones and numbers let a small group pose as many different people, run several conversations at once, and disappear from one number while continuing on another.

## How the Trust-First Trap Worked

The Delhi case follows a pattern that fraud investigators see again and again in India. Understanding the pattern is the best defence against it, because the same structure appears in different cities, with different coins and different promises. Here is how it unfolded, step by step.

**Step 1: The advertisement.** The victim was reached through social media advertisements promising substantial returns from cryptocurrency investment. Ads are cheap, easy to target, and — on some platforms — lightly checked. A fraudster does not need a convincing website or a real company; a polished-looking ad and a WhatsApp number are enough to start conversations with dozens of potential victims.

**Step 2: The friendly contact.** A person using a false name — in this case "Krishna Agarwal" — built a relationship with the victim, explained the supposed opportunity, and proposed a simple deal: cash for USDT. USDT is a stablecoin, meaning its value is designed to track the US dollar, so a victim can be told the deal is low-risk. The pitch is not a promise of spectacular gains but something much more ordinary: "buy USDT from us at a good rate." That ordinariness is part of the trap.

**Step 3: The small, honest deal.** The first transaction — ₹36 lakh for 36,000 USDT — was completed properly. The victim received the cryptocurrency in his own Trust Wallet and could see it there. This is the most important step in the whole fraud. A victim who has been paid once stops worrying about whether the counterparty is honest. Psychologists call this the trust-building phase; fraud investigators call it the hook. Either way, it works because it exploits a reasonable human instinct: if someone kept their word once, they will probably keep it again.

**Step 4: The bigger deal.** With trust established, the victim agreed to a larger transaction the very next day — ₹55 lakh in cash for 55,000 USDT. The meeting took place in Amar Colony, and the USDT was transferred to the victim's wallet, just as before.

**Step 5: The withdrawal.** This is the step that separates a legitimate deal from a fraud. Shortly after the second transfer, the accused withdrew all the cryptocurrency from the victim's wallet. Police have not publicly explained the exact technical method, but there are only a few ways this can happen: the victim's wallet may have been set up with the fraudsters' help in a way that left them with access, the victim may have shared a recovery phrase or private key during the process, or the wallet may have been accessed through a device or app the fraudsters controlled. Whatever the method, the result was the same — the money was gone within hours of the handover.

**Step 6: The disappearance.** Once the funds were withdrawn, the group went quiet. The victim filed his complaint at Amar Colony police station on 4 October, the same day as the second deal. It then took police about six weeks of investigation to identify and arrest the five accused across three states.

The lesson is not that the first deal was fake — it was real. The lesson is that a real first deal proves nothing about the second one. Legitimate sellers do not need to prove themselves with a loss-making trial transaction, and fraudsters are happy to pay for trust when the payoff is several times larger.

## Why Cash-for-Crypto Deals Are So Risky

The Delhi case was a cash deal: the victim handed over physical currency and received cryptocurrency in return. That format is popular with fraudsters for several reasons, and Indian users should understand each one.

**There is almost no paper trail.** A bank transfer or UPI payment leaves a record — an account number, a name, a timestamp. Cash leaves none of that. Once the cash changes hands, the only evidence is the victim's word and whatever messages survive on a phone. That makes both investigation and recovery much harder.

**Crypto transfers are hard to reverse.** When the accused withdrew the USDT from the victim's wallet, there was no bank to call and no chargeback to file. Cryptocurrency transactions are designed to be final. Exchanges can sometimes freeze funds if they arrive quickly enough and the receiving wallet is on their platform, but a transfer to a private wallet is, in practice, gone.

**The wallet is the weak point.** In a cash-for-crypto deal, the buyer must receive the coins in a wallet — and the seller often "helps" set it up. That help is the danger. Anyone who knows your wallet's recovery phrase or private key can empty it from anywhere in the world, at any time. No legitimate seller ever needs your recovery phrase, your private key, or your wallet password. If someone asks for any of these, for any reason, the deal is a fraud — full stop.

**The price is the lure.** Victims in these cases are often offered USDT at a slightly better rate than the market, or promised returns on the investment. In the Delhi case, the first deal was reportedly done at a loss to the sellers. A rate that is too good to be true is not a bargain — it is the cost of acquiring your trust.

The safer route for Indians who want USDT or any other cryptocurrency is a registered Indian exchange or a FIU-IND registered platform, where identity is verified, payments go through traceable channels, and there is a company to complain to. It is slower and less exciting than a cash deal in a parking lot. That is the point.

## The Red Flags, in One Place

If you are ever offered a crypto deal in person, over WhatsApp, or through a social media ad, check it against this list. Any single item is a reason to slow down; two or more is a reason to walk away.

| Red flag | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| The deal is cash-only | No paper trail means no recovery and no accountability |
| A stranger offers a better-than-market rate | The discount is the price of your trust, not a bargain |
| The first small deal is suspiciously smooth | Fraudsters often complete one real deal to earn trust for a bigger one |
| The seller wants to "help" set up your wallet | Wallet setup help is how they get your recovery phrase or private key |
| You are asked for a recovery phrase, private key or password | No legitimate person ever needs these; sharing them hands over your funds |
| Contact is only through WhatsApp or Telegram | No verifiable company, office, or registered platform behind the deal |
| Pressure to act today, or the rate "expires" | Urgency stops you from checking, verifying, or thinking |
| Returns are promised on a stablecoin | USDT is designed to hold its dollar value — promised "returns" on it are a sales pitch, not a feature |
| The person uses a name you cannot verify | A pseudonym plus a new SIM card is all a fraudster needs |
| The meeting is in a random location | Legitimate businesses have offices; fraudsters have meeting points |

The Delhi victim encountered several of these at once: a social media ad, a stranger using a false name, a cash deal, a too-good first transaction, and a wallet that the accused could later empty. Any one of them, noticed in time, could have stopped the loss.

## What the Law Says: BNS Sections in the FIR

The case was registered under Sections 318(4) and 3(5) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), the criminal code that replaced the Indian Penal Code in 2024.

Section 318 deals with cheating. In plain language, it covers dishonestly inducing someone to hand over property — in this case, cash — by deception. Section 3(5) deals with acts done by several people in furtherance of a common intention, which is how the law treats a group that plans and carries out a fraud together rather than as isolated individuals.

These are the sections in the first information report (FIR). An FIR is the start of a criminal case, not its conclusion. The investigation, the filing of a chargesheet, and any trial will decide what the accused are ultimately held responsible for, and every accused person is presumed innocent until proven guilty. That distinction matters, and it is why this article describes the accused as "alleged" throughout.

For victims, the practical point is simpler: a police complaint is the single most useful step after a fraud. It creates an official record, it triggers investigation, and it is usually required before banks, exchanges or cybercrime cells can act on the case. Filing promptly — as the Delhi victim did on the same day — gives investigators the best chance of tracing funds before they are moved through more wallets.

## How This Fits a Bigger Indian Pattern

The Delhi bust is not an isolated case. It is the latest in a series of crypto fraud enforcement stories in India in recent weeks, and the pattern is worth seeing as a whole.

In early November, the Andhra Pradesh CID announced a ₹15 crore crypto fraud case involving a Tenali victim, 27 transactions across Bitcoin, Solana and Dogecoin, and eight arrests spanning Hyderabad, Maharashtra, China, Malaysia and Portugal. In late October, the Enforcement Directorate searched premises in Bengaluru and Madhya Pradesh in a case involving alleged FEMA contraventions of more than ₹4,600 crore. And on 1 November, the Enforcement Directorate's Korvio Coin case — searches in Chandigarh, Mohali and Zirakpur, one arrest, and an estimated ₹500 crore in losses across more than 2.48 lakh users — showed the same trust-building structure at a much larger scale.

The details differ, but the shape repeats: social media contact, a promise of returns, a small success to build confidence, then a large loss. The Delhi case is smaller in money terms than the Korvio or Andhra Pradesh cases, but it is in some ways the most useful warning for ordinary users, because it happened to one person, through one ad, in two meetings. It is the scale at which most Indians will actually encounter crypto fraud — not a ₹500 crore scheme, but a stranger with a good rate and a convincing first deal.

There is also an enforcement pattern worth noting. Police and agencies are increasingly tracing crypto through exchanges and wallet addresses, and making arrests across state and national borders. That is good news for victims in the long run, but it does not change the basic fact: prevention is far more effective than recovery. Once crypto leaves your wallet, getting it back is rare, slow, and often impossible.

## What to Do If You Are Targeted

If you are approached with a crypto investment offer, or you think you have already been cheated, here is the practical sequence:

**Before you pay anything:**
1. Stop and verify the person and the platform independently — search the name, the number and the wallet address online.
2. Never share your recovery phrase, private key or wallet password with anyone, for any reason.
3. Use only FIU-IND registered exchanges and platforms, and pay through traceable channels such as bank transfer or UPI.
4. Treat any cash-for-crypto deal with a stranger as high risk, whatever the rate.
5. If the offer promises returns, ask yourself what the return is actually based on — and remember that USDT is designed to stay near one dollar, so "returns" on it are a sales claim, not a property of the coin.

**If you have already been cheated:**
1. Report immediately on the National Cybercrime Reporting Portal at cybercrime.gov.in, or call the helpline 1930.
2. File a complaint at your local police station with every detail: wallet addresses, transaction IDs, chats, phone numbers and payment proof.
3. Save screenshots of everything before messages or accounts are deleted.
4. If the funds went to an exchange wallet, contact that exchange's support team quickly with the transaction details and your police complaint number.
5. Do not pay anyone who offers to recover your funds for a fee. Recovery-fee offers are themselves one of the most common second-stage scams, and they target people who have already lost money once.

**About tax:** Buying, selling or transferring crypto in India has tax consequences. Gains are taxed at a flat 30% plus a 4% cess, with 1% TDS on covered transfers, and losses cannot be set off against other income. Cash deals do not make the tax disappear — they only make it harder to prove what happened. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.
