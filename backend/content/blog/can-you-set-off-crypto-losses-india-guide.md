# Can You Set Off Crypto Losses in India? Rules Explained

Many Indian beginners ask the same question at tax time: can you set off crypto losses against your crypto gains, the way losses sometimes work elsewhere in tax filing? The short answer is no, and that single rule surprises people more than the 30% tax itself. This guide explains what set off means, how India's rules for virtual digital assets treat losses, and why a year that felt like a net loss overall can still produce a tax bill.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. Tax rules change — consider consulting a qualified tax professional for your own filing.

Nothing here is personal tax advice or a filing service. It is a plain-language explainer so you can organise your exchange records, understand what the return is doing, and have a better conversation with a chartered accountant if you use one.

## What Does Set Off Actually Mean?

Set off means using a loss from one transaction to reduce a gain from another before tax is calculated. In many parts of tax law, this is allowed within limits. If you gain on one item and lose on another in the same category, the loss can shrink the taxable gain, so you pay tax only on the net result.

Carry forward is the close cousin of set off. If a loss cannot be used in the current year — because there is not enough gain to absorb it, for example — some types of losses can be declared and carried forward to reduce gains in a later year, usually for a limited number of years and only if the return was filed on time.

Beginners often arrive at crypto tax with this familiar mental model. They add up the year in their head, see a small profit or even a small loss overall, and assume the tax will follow that net figure. For virtual digital assets in India, it does not. The law deliberately removes both set off and carry forward for these assets, and understanding that early prevents a nasty surprise in July.

## The Rule for Crypto Losses in India

Transfers of virtual digital assets — the Income-tax Act's term for cryptocurrencies and similar assets, including NFTs — are taxed under a special section, commonly referred to as Section 115BBH. Gains from a transfer are taxed at a flat 30%, plus applicable surcharge and cess. Only the cost of acquisition, meaning what you paid to buy that asset, can be deducted when working out the gain on that transfer. No other expense is allowed as a deduction.

The same section states the loss rule in plain terms. If a transfer results in a loss, that loss cannot be set off against income from any other transfer of a virtual digital asset, or against income under any other head. It also cannot be carried forward to the next year. In practical language: the loss is recorded, but it gives you no tax benefit anywhere, in any year.

A second rule runs alongside this one. On most sales on Indian exchanges such as CoinDCX, WazirX, and ZebPay, 1% TDS is deducted on the sale consideration under Section 194S and later appears as a credit in your Form 26AS and Annual Information Statement. TDS is not the final tax — it is an advance collection that is adjusted against your total tax liability when you file.

Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

## A Worked Rupee Example

Numbers make the rule easier to see. Imagine two separate transfers in one financial year. These are rounded teaching examples, not records of any real asset or price.

| Transfer | You bought for | You sold for | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Asset A | ₹50,000 | ₹80,000 | Gain of ₹30,000 |
| Asset B | ₹60,000 | ₹40,000 | Loss of ₹20,000 |

If set off were allowed, you would net the two results: ₹30,000 minus ₹20,000 leaves a ₹10,000 net gain, and tax would be calculated on that smaller figure. That is not how crypto is treated. Instead, the gain on Asset A stands on its own. Tax at 30% on ₹30,000 is ₹9,000, before surcharge and cess. The ₹20,000 loss on Asset B reduces nothing. Even though your combined result for the year was only ₹10,000 ahead, the tax is worked out on the full ₹30,000 gain.

Notice also what the 1% TDS does here. TDS would have been deducted on the sale amounts of both transfers, including the one that ended in a loss, because TDS is charged on consideration, not on profit. Those TDS amounts are credits you claim in the return — they are not lost — but claiming them correctly depends on matching every credit to your PAN records.

## Crypto Losses Compared With Shares

The contrast with shares helps explain why the crypto rule catches people out. The table below is a simplified comparison of the general approach, not a complete statement of either tax regime.

| Question | Listed shares (general) | Virtual digital assets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can a loss offset a gain in the same year? | Often yes, within the same head and rules | No |
| Can an unused loss be carried forward? | Often yes, if declared on time | No |
| Is the tax rate a special flat rate? | Depends on holding period and type | Flat 30% on gains |
| What can be deducted from a gain? | Cost plus permitted expenses | Only cost of acquisition |

The lesson is not that one regime is better or worse overall. It is that habits learned from equity filing do not transfer to crypto filing. A beginner who files both needs to keep the two rulebooks separate and let Schedule VDA handle crypto transfers on their own terms.

## Which Events Count as a Transfer?

Set off questions often appear alongside confusion about what counts as a transfer in the first place. In general, a transfer happens when you sell a virtual digital asset for rupees, exchange one crypto asset for another, or spend crypto to pay for goods or services. Each of those events closes the story of that holding: compare what you received with what that asset cost you, and the result is a gain or a loss for that transfer alone.

A simple purchase with INR through UPI or a bank transfer is not itself a taxable transfer — it is the start of a holding, and its cost and date become the records you will need later. Moving your own crypto between your own wallets or your own exchange accounts is also different in character from selling it, but you should still keep clear records of those movements, because your cost history must remain traceable when a real transfer eventually happens.

Gifts and inherited assets have their own specific treatments, and high-value or unusual cases deserve individual professional advice rather than assumptions copied from a general guide.

## Why You Should Still Report Losses

If a loss gives no benefit, some beginners are tempted to leave loss-making transfers out of the return entirely. That is a mistake for three reasons.

First, Schedule VDA asks for transfers, not just profitable ones. Reporting each transfer with its acquisition date, transfer date, head of income, cost, and consideration keeps your return internally consistent with the schedule's design. Second, exchanges report transaction information, and TDS credits appear in your tax records whether or not a transfer was profitable. A return that omits transfers can mismatch the data the tax department already holds. Third, complete records protect you later: if a notice or a question ever asks how a balance was built or sold, a full transaction history in rupees is far easier to explain than a partial one.

Keep, for every transfer, the exchange statement, the date and INR value of both purchase and sale, any transfer fees shown separately, and the TDS certificate or statement entry. A simple spreadsheet updated through the year is enough for most beginners and is far easier than rebuilding a year of history from an app at filing time.

## Common Beginner Mistakes With Crypto Losses

The first mistake is netting off in your head and budgeting for tax on the net figure, then discovering the bill is larger. The second is assuming a loss can be carried forward like a capital loss on shares and planning next year's trades around a benefit that does not exist. The third is trying to manufacture a deduction the law does not allow — for example, adding exchange fees, withdrawal charges, or interest costs on top of the purchase cost. For virtual digital assets, only the cost of acquisition is deductible.

A fourth mistake is ignoring small transfers. Swaps between two crypto assets on a DEX or an overseas platform are still transfers, even though no rupees touched your bank account. The absence of an Indian exchange or of 1% TDS on a particular platform does not remove the Indian tax resident's duty to report the gain correctly.

## What This Means for How You Learn and Plan

This guide is education, not a recommendation to trade more or less. The practical takeaway is narrower and more useful: judge every transfer on its own after-tax reality. A gain is taxed even in a year when other holdings fell. Frequent small trades create frequent small records, each with its own result, and the loss-making ones will not soften the tax on the winning ones. Keeping that picture clear before you act is part of basic crypto literacy in India, alongside wallet safety, scam awareness, and record keeping.

If your situation involves large amounts, business income questions, foreign exchanges, or notices from the tax department, treat a qualified tax professional's fee as part of doing this correctly, not as an optional extra.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a loss on one coin reduce tax on a gain from another coin?

No. Each transfer of a virtual digital asset is calculated separately. A loss on one asset cannot reduce a gain on another asset, in the same year or any later year.

### What if all my crypto transfers in a year were losses?

Then there is no crypto gain to tax for that year, but the transfers should still be reported correctly, and any 1% TDS credits should still be claimed and matched in the return. The losses themselves cannot be carried forward to shelter a future gain.

### Does 1% TDS still apply when I sell at a loss?

Yes, in general TDS under Section 194S is deducted on the consideration received, not on whether you made a profit. That is why a loss-making sale can still show a TDS credit in Form 26AS, which you then claim in your return.

## Conclusion

In India, crypto losses cannot be set off against crypto gains and cannot be carried forward — each gain is taxed at 30% on its own, with only its purchase cost deductible, and 1% TDS applies on sale consideration. Once you accept that simple, strict rule, filing becomes easier to organise: report every transfer in Schedule VDA, keep rupee records for each one, match your TDS credits, and verify the current rules before you file.
