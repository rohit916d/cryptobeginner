# WazirX Binance ED CBI Probe: What Indian Users Must Know

Thousands of Indian crypto investors have approached the Enforcement Directorate and the Central Bureau of Investigation over alleged losses linked to WazirX and Binance. The complaints, reported by news agency IANS on 21 November 2026 and carried the same day by the Free Press Journal, the Morung Express, Punjab Kesari and other outlets, reopen one of the oldest unresolved questions in Indian crypto: when an exchange fails its users, who is actually responsible? This guide explains what the WazirX Binance ED CBI complaints say, the ownership dispute and the July 2024 hack behind them, and what Indian users should know — without taking sides, because the complaints are allegations, not findings.

## What Happened: Thousands of Investors Approach the ED and CBI

On 21 November 2026, IANS reported from New Delhi that thousands of cryptocurrency investors had approached central investigative agencies — the Enforcement Directorate (ED) and the Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) — alleging that crypto exchanges WazirX and Binance caused significant financial losses to them. The report, syndicated widely the same day, said investor groups and individual users had submitted complaints reporting alleged unauthorised access to accounts and loss of crypto assets.

The complaints do not make a single claim. They raise a cluster of connected issues, and the reports describe them in roughly the same terms across outlets:

- **Unauthorised access and lost assets.** Complainants reported alleged unauthorised access to their accounts and the loss of crypto assets, and asked the agencies to examine how customer funds were handled.
- **A disputed ownership relationship.** The complaints question the relationship between WazirX and Binance, and argue that uncertainty over who owned and controlled what made it hard to establish accountability for customer assets.
- **The aftermath of the July 2024 cyberattack.** Investors allege that after the major attack on WazirX in July 2024, the exchange proposed measures that effectively spread the losses across both affected and unaffected users.
- **A restructuring routed through Singapore.** The complaints question WazirX's decision to seek court approval for a restructuring scheme through Zettai Pte Ltd, its Singapore-based entity, arguing that overseas legal proceedings increased the financial and procedural burden on Indian investors seeking remedies.

Two things the reports are careful to note. First, regulatory authorities have been unable to take decisive action, the reports say, because India has no comprehensive legal framework governing cryptocurrency-related frauds. Second, neither WazirX nor Binance had issued a statement about the complaints at the time of the reports. No agency has publicly announced a formal investigation based on these complaints, and no court has made any finding on the allegations. What has happened, so far, is that complaints have been filed — and that, in itself, is the news.

## The Ownership Question at the Heart of the Complaints

To understand why investors are asking the ED and CBI to look at ownership at all, you need the short history of the WazirX–Binance relationship — because it is genuinely confusing, and that confusion is the point of the complaints.

WazirX launched in India in 2018 and grew into one of the country's largest crypto exchanges, claiming around 16 million registered users — 1.6 crore — and marketing itself as one of India's largest and most secure platforms. In 2019, Binance announced that it was acquiring WazirX. For years afterwards, the two platforms operated as visibly connected businesses: WazirX users' funds were held in wallets that, according to WazirX's own user agreement, were provided by Binance as a wallet service provider.

Then, in 2022, the relationship broke down in public. Binance's then-chief executive said publicly that Binance did not own WazirX, and that the 2019 acquisition had never been completed. WazirX's founder disputed that account. The two sides have disagreed about the ownership ever since, and the disagreement has never been resolved by a court or a regulator.

For ordinary users, the practical effect of that dispute was simple and serious: when something went wrong, it was not clear which company was responsible for their money. The complainants say exactly that. According to the reports, they contend that Binance's statement that it did not own WazirX, set against a user agreement that still named Binance as the wallet service provider, created confusion among customers about who was safeguarding their funds.

This is not a technical footnote. In traditional finance, the question "who holds my money?" has a clear, documented answer, and a regulator can enforce it. In crypto, where an exchange can be a website, a company in one country, a wallet provider in another and a brand that changes hands in a press release, the answer can be genuinely unclear — and that lack of clarity is what the complainants are asking the agencies to examine.

## The July 2024 Hack and the Loss-Sharing Restructuring

The second strand of the complaints goes back to July 2024, when WazirX suffered a major cyberattack. Reports at the time put the loss at roughly $235 million — about ₹1,950 crore at the exchange rates then — and the exchange restricted withdrawals and trading for a prolonged period while it dealt with the aftermath.

What the complainants object to is not only the hack itself, but what happened next. According to the reports, after the attack the exchange proposed measures that effectively distributed the losses across both affected and unaffected users — in other words, customers whose own crypto had not been stolen were asked to share the cost of the shortfall. This kind of loss-sharing is sometimes called "socialising" losses, and it is one of the most contentious things an exchange can do after a hack, because it turns a security failure into a bill paid by everyone on the platform.

WazirX then pursued a restructuring scheme through Zettai Pte Ltd, its Singapore-based entity, seeking court approval in Singapore. The complainants argue that routing the process through an overseas court increased the financial and procedural burden on Indian investors — who had to follow a foreign legal process, in a foreign jurisdiction, to pursue claims about money they had deposited with an exchange that served them in India.

Whether that restructuring was the right or wrong path is a matter for the courts and the creditors involved, and this guide does not take a position on it. But the complaint itself is easy to understand from a user's point of view: you signed up with an Indian-facing exchange, your money was held through an arrangement involving a global exchange, and when the money was gone, the process for getting any of it back ran through a court in another country.

## Why Regulators Have Found It Hard to Act

The reports carry one more point that matters for every Indian crypto user, not just WazirX customers: according to the reports, regulatory authorities have been unable to take decisive action because India has no comprehensive legal framework governing cryptocurrency-related frauds.

That is worth unpacking, because it explains why the complainants went to the ED and the CBI rather than to a single crypto regulator — there is no single crypto regulator in India to go to. Instead, different agencies cover different pieces of the picture:

- **The Enforcement Directorate (ED)** investigates suspected violations of the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA) and money-laundering offences. It has been active in crypto: it searched premises linked to CoinDCX and Delta Exchange on 6 November 2026 in connection with alleged FEMA violations, and it conducted searches on 27 October 2026 in an earlier crypto probe.
- **The Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI)** is India's federal investigative agency for serious criminal cases, including large-scale fraud. Investors approach it when they believe a matter involves criminal wrongdoing at a scale that state police cannot easily handle.
- **The Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU-IND)** registers crypto exchanges operating in India and receives reports of suspicious transactions. Registration is a compliance obligation, not a guarantee of an exchange's solvency or security.
- **The Reserve Bank of India (RBI)** regulates banks and payment systems, which is why banking-partner problems have repeatedly disrupted rupee withdrawals at Indian exchanges in November 2026.

None of these agencies is a crypto consumer-protection authority. India taxes crypto — a flat 30% on gains plus cess, 1% TDS on transfers above a threshold, and no set-off of losses against other income — but it has not passed a comprehensive law that defines what a crypto exchange owes its customers, how customer funds must be held, or what happens when an exchange fails. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

That gap is the reason the same story keeps repeating in different forms: a hack or a failure, a restructuring or a recovery plan, and users discovering that the protections they assumed existed were never written down anywhere. The Bharat Web3 Association, the industry body, submitted recommendations to the Finance Ministry on 21 November 2026 — the same day these complaints were reported — asking for clearer tax rules and clearer obligations for offshore platforms serving Indian users. The two stories are different, but they point at the same gap.

## What This Means for Indian Crypto Users

The complaints are about WazirX and Binance, but the lessons apply to anyone who keeps crypto on an exchange in India. The single most important question is the one the complainants are asking: who actually holds your money, and under what legal arrangement?

**Know who holds your funds.** Before you deposit with any exchange, check whether customer funds are held by the exchange itself, by a third-party custodian, or by a wallet provider in another country. If the exchange's own terms name another company as the wallet provider, that company — not the brand on the app — is the one holding your assets. This is not a reason to avoid an exchange on its own, but it is information you should have before you deposit, not after something goes wrong.

**Understand that "registered" is not "guaranteed."** An exchange registered with FIU-IND has met a compliance requirement. That is not the same as a bank deposit guarantee, and it does not mean the government will make you whole if the exchange is hacked or fails. In India, there is no deposit insurance for crypto held on an exchange.

**Keep your own records.** Exchanges can restrict access, go into restructuring, or simply become hard to reach. Keep independent records of your deposits, trades, withdrawals and balances — screenshots, downloaded statements and transaction IDs — outside the exchange's app. If you ever need to file a complaint, those records are your evidence, and you may not be able to retrieve them later.

**Do not trust recovery promises.** After every major exchange failure, scammers contact victims offering to recover their funds for a fee, or posing as exchange staff, lawyers or officials. No genuine recovery process asks you to pay an upfront fee to a stranger, share a seed phrase, or transfer funds to a "safe" wallet. Report such approaches to the National Cyber Crime Helpline on 1930 or through cybercrime.gov.in.

**If you believe you are a victim, report it.** Suspected fraud in India should be reported to the National Cyber Crime Helpline on 1930 or through the cybercrime portal at cybercrime.gov.in. Keep a copy of your complaint reference number. For legal options specific to your situation — including whether to approach a consumer forum, a civil court or an investigative agency — consult a qualified lawyer. ## The Bigger Picture: Accountability Is the Story of 2026

The WazirX complaints are the latest in a year in which Indian users have repeatedly learned how much depends on arrangements they cannot see. In the same month, the ED searched CoinDCX and Delta Exchange, CERT-In warned that an AI-assisted actor was targeting crypto exchanges, Unocoin disclosed a hot-wallet theft, and CoinDCX and ZebPay both told users that breaches at messaging vendors had exposed customer contact data. Each incident was different, but they share a shape: the risk sat somewhere the user could not see — a vendor, a banking partner, a wallet provider, a foreign court process — and the user found out only after the fact.

That is why the ownership question in these complaints matters beyond WazirX. If it is genuinely unclear who holds customer funds, then it is unclear who is accountable when those funds are lost — and unclear which regulator, which court and which country's law applies. Investors are asking the ED and the CBI to examine exactly that chain of responsibility. Whether the agencies act, and what they find, will take time to become clear, and this site will cover developments as they are reported.

For now, the practical takeaway is the same one this site has given after every incident this year: treat any exchange as a counterparty, not a vault. Keep only what you are actively trading on an exchange, keep your own records, use two-factor authentication and unique passwords, and understand — before you deposit — who actually holds your funds and under whose law. No exchange's marketing can substitute for that knowledge.

## The Bottom Line

On 21 November 2026, IANS reported that thousands of Indian crypto investors had approached the ED and the CBI, alleging that WazirX and Binance caused them significant financial losses through unauthorised account access, a disputed ownership arrangement, loss-sharing after the July 2024 cyberattack, and a restructuring routed through Singapore. Neither exchange had responded to the reports at the time, no agency has announced a formal probe, and the allegations have not been tested in any court — so they should be read as complaints, not findings. But they put a clear question on the table for every Indian crypto user: who holds your money, and who is accountable if it disappears? Until India has a comprehensive framework that answers that question in law, the answer is something each user has to find out for themselves — before they deposit, not after.
