On 12 November 2026, Germany's lower house of parliament did something no major European country has done before: it voted to let customs officers freeze cryptocurrency worth more than €100,000 when its lawful origin looks doubtful — without first opening a criminal case. The Germany crypto seizure law is not yet in force, and it still needs the approval of the upper house, but the vote itself is the news. It is the clearest sign yet that European governments are moving from regulating crypto exchanges to giving state agencies direct powers over the assets themselves.

## What Germany Just Did: The Facts at a Glance

The law is called the Customs Financial Justice Act — in German, the Zollfinanzgerechtigkeitsgesetz. The Bundestag, Germany's lower house, passed it on Thursday 12 November 2026. It is a customs reform, not a crypto law: it covers cash, property, vehicles and other high-value assets, and cryptocurrency is included expressly through the definition in the European Union's Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) regulation. Crypto is covered, in other words, but it is not singled out.

The facts, as reported on 15–16 November 2026 by CoinDesk, Decrypt, Bitcoin Magazine, Ethnews, Coindaro and ChainAIWire, are:

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| What passed | The Customs Financial Justice Act (Zollfinanzgerechtigkeitsgesetz) |
| When | Bundestag vote, 12 November 2026; Finance Committee recommended passage 11 November |
| Threshold | Assets worth more than €100,000 (about $116,000, roughly ₹1.09 crore at current rates) |
| What customs can do | Freeze assets for up to 30 days without opening a criminal case |
| Extension | A finance court may extend the freeze once, by up to 150 days (180 days total) |
| Permanent confiscation | Still requires a finance-court judgment that the origin is unlawful |
| Status | Needs Bundesrat (upper house) consent; planned start 1 January 2027 |
| Votes | CDU/CSU, SPD and AfD in favour; The Left opposed; the Greens abstained |

The government introduced the bill on 30 September 2026. The federal cabinet had adopted it on 12 August. It is part of a wider overhaul of Germany's customs administration and its response to money laundering and organised financial crime, and it partly carries out the federal government's action plan against organised crime from February 2026, along with recommendations from the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) and the Egmont Group of financial intelligence units.

## What the Law Actually Lets Customs Do

The core of the law is a new administrative route for dealing with assets of doubtful origin. Until now, seizing assets generally meant a criminal investigation first: the police or prosecutors opened a case, and the seizure followed from it. The new law breaks that sequence.

Under the new provisions, customs officers may secure an asset if there are factual indications that it is not of lawful origin. They do not need to open a criminal case first, and they do not need to tie the asset to a specific crime. The freeze lasts up to 30 days while customs investigates where the asset came from. A finance court may then extend the hold once, by up to 150 days, taking the maximum to 180 days. During that period, customs can ask the owner — or the beneficial owner, if the asset is held through someone else — for documents and information about where it came from.

Permanent confiscation is a different step, and it still needs a court. A finance-court panel must be convinced that the origin is unlawful before assets can be taken for good. The freeze is administrative; the confiscation is judicial. That distinction is the most important thing to understand about the law, because most early headlines collapsed the two into one word: "seizure".

The law also does other things beyond the freeze power. It reorganises Germany's customs administration, strengthens its Financial Intelligence Unit, and gives customs a legal basis to use technologies such as artificial intelligence. The German government expects the package to bring in about €308 million (around $342 million) in extra revenue, according to Decrypt's reporting — though that figure covers the whole customs reform, not crypto alone.

## Why €100,000 Is Not an Automatic Seizure

The threshold has caused the most confusion, so it is worth being precise about it. The law treats an asset as "significant" when its value exceeds €100,000. That figure is a rule of thumb for which assets the new procedure can reach, not a trigger that makes seizure automatic.

Customs must still have factual indications that the asset is not of lawful origin. The bill sets out the factors that weigh in that assessment: whether the owner's income and financial position are consistent with the value of the asset, whether the source of funds or the transaction the owner describes can actually be traced, and whether the owner or beneficial owner is transparent about who they are. The law does not say that an unexplained transaction, an old wallet or a high Bitcoin price automatically makes the assets criminal proceeds. Value alone is not enough; the doubt has to be grounded in facts.

That matters for ordinary holders. A person who bought Bitcoin on a regulated exchange, paid from a bank account in their own name, and declared the holding in their tax return has a traceable story to tell. The law is aimed at the opposite situation: assets whose owner cannot explain them, whose stated source does not check out, or whose ownership is hidden behind layers that serve no visible purpose. Whether customs applies that distinction fairly in practice is exactly what the law's critics are worried about — and it is a question that will only be answered when the power is first used.

## What Still Has to Happen Before It Takes Effect

The Bundestag's vote is not the end of the process. The law was drafted as one that needs the consent of the Bundesrat, the upper house that represents Germany's federal states. The Bundesrat has not yet voted, and no date for a vote has been announced. Its next plenary session is on 20 November, but according to Coindaro's reporting, the agenda as last updated on 13 November does not list the customs law — though agendas can still be supplemented.

The government's planned start date is 1 January 2027, with some parts coming later. That timetable depends on the Bundesrat approving the measure and on formal promulgation. If the upper house delays or amends it, the start date slips. For now, the law is passed by one house and pending in the other, and nothing has been frozen under it.

That pending status is worth keeping in mind when reading coverage. Headlines that say Germany "can now seize crypto" are describing what the Bundestag approved, not what is currently in force. The accurate statement is narrower: Germany's lower house has approved a law that would let customs freeze crypto over €100,000 without a criminal case, subject to upper-house approval and a planned 1 January 2027 start.

## Why Germany Did This

Germany's move did not come out of nowhere. It sits inside a wider European shift toward treating crypto as part of the financial-crime landscape rather than as a separate technology to be regulated on its own terms.

The European Union's MiCA regulation already gives crypto a legal definition, and Germany's law uses that definition to bring crypto inside the customs framework. The EU has also been building a sanctions apparatus around crypto: the European Commission announced a dedicated Crypto Sanctions Task Force in early November 2026, aimed at the A7A5 ruble stablecoin and the platforms used to evade sanctions. The United States, for its part, used the FinCEN process to cut off the MEXC exchange in November over alleged illicit flows of at least $16 billion. The pattern across all three is the same: governments are no longer asking only whether crypto businesses are licensed — they are asking what happens to the assets themselves when their origin cannot be explained.

Germany has a specific domestic driver too. The bill is part of the federal government's action plan against organised crime, adopted in February 2026, and it follows recommendations from FATF and the Egmont Group. Customs is the agency that meets cross-border value in physical form — at borders, in parcels, in cash — and crypto has become another form of value that crosses borders without passing through a bank. The law gives customs a tool for the cases where the value is visible but the story behind it is not.

## The Criticism: Who This Catches, and Who It Might Not

The law has drawn sharp criticism from Germany's legal profession, and the criticism is worth reporting in full because it is the other half of the story.

The Federal Bar Association (BRAK) opposed the measure. German legal associations have warned that it gives customs authorities overly broad powers, and that it risks treating missing ownership records as evidence of illegal activity. The concern is practical: many people, including entirely lawful crypto holders, have incomplete records — an old wallet from 2017, an exchange that has since closed, a gift from a relative, a peer-to-peer trade with no paperwork. If the absence of a clean paper trail is treated as a factual indication of unlawful origin, the law could catch the disorganised rather than the criminal.

Opposition lawmakers made a related point in the Bundestag debate: the powers are more likely to catch cash couriers than sophisticated launderers. A professional money-laundering operation splits value across many wallets, many people and many jurisdictions, staying below any single threshold. A person carrying a single large holding — or holding it in one wallet — is the one the €100,000 figure reaches most easily. The government's revenue estimate of about €308 million suggests it expects the powers to be used, but it does not say against whom.

There is also a structural concern. The freeze is administrative, which means it happens before a court has looked at the case. The court only enters the picture when customs asks for an extension, or when confiscation is sought. For up to 30 days, the owner bears the burden of producing documents to explain assets that the state has already frozen. Supporters of the law say that is the point: the freeze preserves the assets while the explanation is checked. Critics say it reverses the normal order, in which the state must justify taking something before it takes it.

None of this criticism has stopped the law so far — it passed with the votes of the governing CDU/CSU and SPD, plus the AfD — but it shapes what happens next. The Bundesrat, the courts and the first real cases will all test whether the safeguards in the bill work as written.

## What It Means for Indians in Germany

For the large Indian community in Germany — students, engineers, researchers and their families — the law is relevant in a practical, everyday way, even though it is not aimed at them.

First, the threshold is not hard to cross. At current rates, €100,000 is roughly ₹1.09 crore. A person who bought Bitcoin or Ethereum over several years, held it through the market's rises, and now lives in Germany can hold more than that without ever having done anything unusual. The law does not care how you got there; it cares whether you can show how you got there.

Second, the records that answer that question are ordinary ones. Exchange statements showing your purchases, bank statements showing the transfers that funded them, and tax returns in which the holdings were declared. If you bought crypto in India and later moved to Germany, the Indian records matter too: the exchange you used, the bank account you paid from, and the Indian tax filings that reported the gains. Keep those records in one place, and keep them for years, not months.

Third, do not improvise. Moving crypto between wallets, splitting it across accounts, or transferring it to a relative to get below the threshold after the law takes effect would look exactly like the behaviour the law is designed to catch. If your holdings are documented and lawful, the correct response to a law like this is to keep the documents, not to rearrange the assets.

Fourth, the timeline gives you room. The law still needs Bundesrat approval, and the planned start is 1 January 2027. That is time to gather records, not time to panic. If you are unsure about your own documentation, a conversation with a tax adviser who understands both German and Indian crypto rules is worth more than any article, including this one.

## How India Does It Differently

The contrast with India is instructive, because India already seizes crypto — but through a different route.

In India, the Enforcement Directorate attaches crypto assets under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA), and it does so after an investigation. The cases our readers will recognise follow that pattern: the ED's searches at CoinDCX and Delta Exchange in November 2026 over alleged FEMA violations and offshore USDT routing; the ₹400 crore Chennai cyber-fraud arrests the same month; the ₹112.91 crore HashPe Ponzi case; the ₹500 crore Korvio Coin case. In each, the attachment followed searches, statements and a formal process, with an adjudicating authority confirming the attachment and courts deciding the outcome. The ED cannot simply freeze an asset because its origin looks unclear; it must build a case first.

Germany's new law reverses that order for the freeze itself: customs can secure the asset first, on factual indications of unlawful origin, and investigate afterwards. The court still decides confiscation, but the freeze happens before the court is involved. India has no equivalent administrative freeze for crypto. Its closest analogues — the ED's provisional attachment, or the income-tax department's powers — both sit inside an investigation or assessment that is already under way.

That difference cuts both ways. India's route is slower and more court-bound, which protects owners but can let assets move while the process runs. Germany's route is faster, which preserves assets but puts the burden of explanation on the owner sooner. Neither is obviously better in the abstract; the question is which risk a country worries about more — assets disappearing, or owners being wrongly frozen. Germany has now answered that question one way. India, so far, has answered it the other.

For Indian readers, the practical upshot is simple: your rules have not changed. Gains on crypto transfers are still taxed at a flat 30% plus a 4% cess, sales still attract 1% TDS, exchanges must still register with FIU-IND, and cross-border movement is still governed by FEMA. What has changed is the picture of where global regulation is heading: toward direct powers over assets, not just licences for businesses.

## What to Watch Next

Three things will determine how this story develops, and all three are worth watching over the coming weeks.

The first is the Bundesrat. If the upper house approves the law, the 1 January 2027 start date becomes real. If it delays, amends or blocks it, the law changes shape or stalls. The 20 November plenary is the next date to watch, though the law is not currently on its agenda.

The second is the first use. Laws like this are defined by their first cases. The first time customs freezes a crypto wallet under the new power — and the first time a finance court is asked to extend a freeze or order a confiscation — will show whether the safeguards work as written, and whether the legal profession's warnings were justified. Watch for the first reported case, and for how the courts handle it.

The third is whether other countries follow. Germany is the first major European economy to give customs this power over crypto, but the pressures behind it — organised crime, sanctions evasion, the visibility of large holdings — are not unique to Germany. If the law survives the Bundesrat and the courts, expect other European governments to look at it closely. The EU's Crypto Sanctions Task Force, announced in the same month, suggests the direction of travel is already set.

## The Bottom Line

On 12 November 2026, the Bundestag passed the Customs Financial Justice Act, a customs reform that lets German customs freeze crypto and other assets worth more than €100,000 — about $116,000, or roughly ₹1.09 crore — when there are factual indications their origin is not lawful, without first opening a criminal case. The freeze lasts up to 30 days, extendable once by a finance court to 180 days in total, and permanent confiscation still requires a court judgment. The law still needs the Bundesrat's approval, and its planned start date is 1 January 2027. Germany's legal associations oppose it, warning that it gives customs overly broad powers and risks treating missing records as evidence of wrongdoing.

For Indian readers, nothing in Indian law has changed. But the direction is now visible: governments are moving from licensing crypto businesses to claiming direct powers over crypto assets, and Germany has just written the clearest version of that power into law. If you hold crypto in Germany, or you are an Indian with assets there, the advice is unglamorous and free — keep your purchase records, your bank statements and your tax filings together, and be able to show where every rupee and every euro came from.
