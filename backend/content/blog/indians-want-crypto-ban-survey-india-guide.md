A nationwide survey has put a number on something many Indian crypto users have felt for months: trust in private cryptocurrencies has fallen sharply. According to a LocalCircles survey reported on 9 November 2026, 59% of Indians who answered the question favour a complete ban on cryptocurrencies, and 82% of respondents said they have little or no trust in digital currencies. The survey collected more than 43,000 responses from citizens across 311 districts, making it one of the largest Indian public-opinion snapshots on crypto since the 2021 survey it compares itself against. This guide explains what the survey actually found, why trust has fallen, what a ban would and would not mean, and what Indian crypto users should take away from it.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a published survey and the current Indian rules; it does not recommend any exchange, token or investment, and it does not predict what the government will do.

## The Headline Numbers: 59% for a Ban, 82% With Little or No Trust

The survey's two headline findings are stark, and they are worth stating exactly as reported, because the details matter more than the headline.

First, on regulation: among the 10,776 respondents who answered the question about how the government should handle crypto, 59% supported a complete ban on cryptocurrencies. A further 16% favoured taxing crypto as a digital asset held overseas, without granting it legal status in India. Another 19% supported legalising and taxing cryptocurrencies domestically. Six per cent were undecided.

Second, on trust: 82% of respondents said they have little or no trust in digital currencies. Within that, 66% reported zero trust — up from 49% in the comparable 2021 survey. The share reporting low confidence fell from 27% to 16%, and those reporting average confidence fell from 12% to 6%. Only 4% said they had a high level of trust in cryptocurrencies, while 8% remained undecided. One small counter-current is worth noting: the share of people described as strongly attracted to digital currencies rose from 1% in 2021 to 4% — a tiny base, but the only figure in the survey that moved in crypto's favour.

Put together, the picture is not a country that is indifferent to crypto. It is a country that has formed a strong opinion, and the opinion is negative. The 59% ban figure is the number that travelled furthest in the coverage, but the trust figures are arguably more important: a ban is a policy preference, while zero trust is a description of how people feel about the asset class itself.

## Trust Has Fallen Steeply Since 2021

The most useful part of the survey is the comparison with LocalCircles' 2021 survey, because it turns a single snapshot into a trend. In 2021, distrust was already high — 71% of respondents had low or zero trust, and 49% had zero trust. Five years later, those numbers have moved decisively in the wrong direction for the crypto industry.

| Measure | 2021 | 2026 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zero trust in crypto | 49% | 66% | Up 17 points |
| Low confidence | 27% | 16% | Down 11 points |
| Average confidence | 12% | 6% | Down 6 points |
| High trust | Not reported separately | 4% | — |
| Little or no trust (total) | 71% | 82% | Up 11 points |
| Strongly attracted to crypto | 1% | 4% | Up 3 points |

The pattern is clear: people have not drifted from "low trust" to "average trust" — they have moved from "low trust" to "zero trust". The middle has emptied out. That is what a confidence collapse looks like in survey data: not a gradual cooling, but a hardening of opinion, with the undecided and the mildly sceptical moving into outright distrust.

The comparison also matters because the 2021 survey was conducted in a very different moment. In November 2021, India was debating a bill that would have banned private cryptocurrencies, Bitcoin was near its then all-time high, and crypto advertising was everywhere during the cricket season. Even then, a majority of respondents did not want crypto legalised. The 2026 survey shows that five years of hacks, fraud cases, a punishing tax regime and a long price decline have not softened that scepticism — they have deepened it.

## Most Indian Households Still Stay Away From Crypto

The survey also asked a simpler, more personal question: does anyone in your household trade or invest in cryptocurrencies? Of the 10,819 respondents who answered, 76% said nobody in their household was involved. Another 16% said one family member participated in crypto transactions or investments, while 5% reported involvement by multiple family members. The remaining 3% did not give a clear response.

That 76% figure is important context for the ban number. Most of the people who want crypto banned are not people who lost money in crypto — they are people who never touched it and, increasingly, do not trust it. That does not make their opinion less real, but it does shape how it should be interpreted. A survey of non-participants will naturally weigh the risks (hacks, scams, fraud) more heavily than the benefits (returns, access, innovation), because the respondents have experienced the risks only through the news and have not experienced the benefits at all.

It also fits with what other data shows about Indian crypto adoption: a large, curious, but still minority user base, with most households watching from the sidelines. For a beginner reading this site, the practical takeaway is not that "everyone hates crypto" — it is that the Indian public conversation about crypto is dominated by people who do not use it, and that the industry's reputation problem is now a mainstream public-opinion problem, not just a niche one.

## Why Trust Fell: A Year of Hacks, Starting With Binance

The survey was conducted against the backdrop of the largest crypto theft in history, and the coverage of the survey draws the connection explicitly. On 29 October 2026, Binance — the world's largest crypto exchange — suffered a breach in which about $5.9 billion was stolen according to blockchain analytics firm Chainalysis, and about $6.3 billion according to Binance's own estimate of the total impact. Binance said nearly $3 billion had been frozen on-chain and that it would cover user losses through its Secure Asset Fund for Users (SAFU) and company funds.

But the Binance hack was not an isolated event. The survey coverage lists a chain of incidents that Indian readers will recognise:

- **Bitget, 24 September 2026:** an attack on the global exchange resulted in losses of approximately $387.5 million from its hot and warm wallets.
- **CoinDCX, October 2026:** the Indian exchange confirmed that approximately $16 million had been drained from wallets it had previously retired. The exchange said it had absorbed the loss and that customer funds remained safe.
- **WazirX, July 2024:** approximately $230 million was stolen in India's most prominent exchange hack. Users recovered 85% of their funds when the exchange resumed operations in October 2025 following a court-approved restructuring.

On top of the exchange hacks, Indian enforcement agencies have spent the same period making high-profile crypto arrests and searches — the Enforcement Directorate's searches at CoinDCX and Delta Exchange over alleged foreign-exchange violations, the AP CID's ₹15 crore fraud bust, and the ED's Chennai arrests in a ₹400 crore cyber-fraud case. Each of these stories reached the mainstream news, not just the crypto press, and each of them reinforced the same message in the public mind: crypto is where money disappears.

None of this proves that crypto itself is unsafe as a technology. It does explain why a survey of ordinary Indians, most of whom do not use crypto, would record 66% zero trust. When the only crypto stories that reach a general audience are about theft, fraud and enforcement, trust is the first casualty.

## The Digital Rupee Contrast: 56% Would Use the RBI's Currency

The most interesting finding in the survey is not about private crypto at all. It is about the digital rupee — the Reserve Bank of India's central bank digital currency (CBDC), launched in 2022. Of the 10,906 citizens who answered the question on potential CBDC usage, 56% said they would be willing to use the digital rupee for business-to-consumer and consumer-to-consumer transactions once it becomes widely available through banks. Another 21% said they were not interested, while 23% remained undecided.

That is a striking contrast with the private-crypto numbers: 56% willing to use the digital rupee, against 4% with high trust in private cryptocurrencies. The difference is the issuer. The digital rupee is issued and regulated by the central bank, and it represents a digital form of the Indian rupee itself — one digital rupee is always worth one rupee, because it is a rupee. It carries none of the price volatility, none of the exchange-counterparty risk, and none of the "will this company still exist next year" anxiety that comes with a private token or a private exchange.

For beginners, this contrast is the single most useful lesson in the survey. The Indian public is not rejecting digital money. It is rejecting unregulated, volatile, privately issued digital money — and it is open to digital money issued by an institution it already trusts. That distinction is likely to shape Indian crypto policy for years: a government that sees 56% willingness to use its own digital currency and 59% support for banning private crypto has little political incentive to liberalise private crypto, and every incentive to push its own CBDC instead.

## Who Was Surveyed — and What the Numbers Can and Cannot Say

A survey is only as good as its sample, so it is worth understanding who actually answered. The survey received more than 43,000 responses from citizens across 311 districts. Of the respondents, 68% were men and 32% were women. Participants from tier-1 cities accounted for 45% of responses, followed by tier-2 cities at 37%, with the remaining 18% from tier-3, tier-4 and rural districts.

Those demographics tell a careful reader two things. First, the survey is large and geographically broad, which is a genuine strength — 311 districts is a wide net, and 43,000 responses is a large sample. Second, it is a survey of LocalCircles' community — people who chose to answer an online survey about crypto — not a random sample of the Indian population. The 68% male skew and the 82% tier-1 and tier-2 skew mean the results describe the views of engaged, urban, mostly male internet users more than they describe a village in Bihar. That does not invalidate the findings, but it does mean the headline should be read as "59% of the people who answered this survey" rather than "59% of all Indians" in the strict statistical sense.

The survey also reports different respondent counts for different questions — 10,776 answered the ban question, 10,819 the household question, 10,906 the digital-rupee question — which is normal for an online survey where respondents skip questions. It is good practice, and it is why this guide quotes the per-question counts rather than the 43,000 total for any single figure.

There is one more caveat worth stating plainly. A survey measures opinion at a moment in time, and opinion after a $6 billion hack is not the same as opinion in a quiet month. If the survey had been conducted in a month with no major hack, the numbers would probably have been softer. That is not a reason to dismiss them — it is a reason to understand them as a reading taken at a moment of maximum public alarm, and to expect them to move if the news cycle changes.

## What a Ban Would Actually Mean — and What the Law Says Now

The most common misunderstanding about a survey like this is to read it as a forecast. A survey does not make law. As of November 2026, cryptocurrencies are not banned in India. Buying, holding and selling crypto on an FIU-IND-registered exchange is legal, and the government taxes it rather than prohibiting it.

The current Indian rules, in brief:

- **Gains are taxed at a flat 30%** plus a 4% cess — 31.2% in total — with no set-off of losses against gains.
- **Sales attract 1% TDS** under Section 194S of the Income Tax Act, deducted by the exchange or buyer.
- **Exchanges must register with FIU-IND** (the Financial Intelligence Unit) and follow anti-money-laundering rules, including KYC.
- **Crypto is not legal tender.** It cannot be used to settle debts in the way rupees can, and the Supreme Court's 2020 decision that struck down the RBI's 2018 banking ban did not make crypto a currency — it restored banking access to crypto businesses.

Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

If the government ever did move towards a ban, it would need Parliament to pass a law. A bill to prohibit private cryptocurrencies was listed for introduction in 2021 and never passed. Since then, the government's approach has been taxation and regulation through FIU-IND, not prohibition — and the 59% figure, while politically significant, does not by itself change that approach. What it does change is the politics: a government considering liberalisation now knows that a majority of surveyed citizens would prefer the opposite, and a government considering tougher rules knows it has public support for them.

For Indian crypto users, the practical implication is not "sell everything before a ban" — that would be a prediction, and this guide does not make predictions. The implication is simpler: keep your records clean, use FIU-IND-registered exchanges, pay the tax that is due, and do not assume that today's rules are permanent. The rules have changed before, and public opinion is now pointing in a stricter direction.

## What Indian Crypto Users Should Do With This Information

The survey is a public-opinion story, not a market signal, so the right response is practical rather than financial. None of the following is investment advice — it is basic hygiene for anyone who holds crypto in India.

**1. Keep your tax records in order.** The 30% tax and 1% TDS apply regardless of what public opinion says, and enforcement agencies are actively investigating exchanges and users. Keep a record of every buy, sell and transfer, including dates, amounts in INR, and the exchange used. If you are unsure how to file, speak to a chartered accountant who understands crypto taxation.

**2. Use FIU-IND-registered exchanges.** The survey's trust numbers are a reminder that the exchange you choose matters. An FIU-IND-registered exchange in India is subject to Indian KYC and reporting rules, which gives you a legal framework and a point of contact if something goes wrong. An unregistered offshore platform offers neither.

**3. Do not keep large amounts on an exchange.** The Binance, WazirX and CoinDCX incidents all involved exchange wallets. For holdings you do not plan to trade soon, a self-custody wallet — with the seed phrase written on paper and stored offline — removes the exchange from the risk equation. If you use a hardware wallet, buy it from the manufacturer, not from a reseller.

**4. Treat survey headlines as mood, not fact.** "59% want a ban" is a real survey result, but it is a statement about the people who answered, on the day they answered, after the news they had just read. It is not a law, not a court order, and not a prediction. The useful question is not "will crypto be banned?" but "what would I do if the rules changed?" — and the answer to that question is to have clean records, registered exchanges and self-custody in place before you ever need them.

**5. Report fraud promptly.** If you are a victim of crypto fraud in India, report it on the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal at cybercrime.gov.in or call the 1930 helpline. Enforcement action in the cases the survey cites began with user complaints, and the ED's recent arrests show that complaints do lead to action.

## What to Watch Next

Three things will determine whether this survey is remembered as a turning point or as a momentary spike in public anger.

The first is whether the government responds. The survey's findings have been shared with government leadership, the RBI and Members of Parliament, according to LocalCircles. A formal response — a statement, a parliamentary question, or a policy proposal — would be the first sign that the survey is moving from public opinion into policy. So far, there has been no such response.

The second is whether the trust numbers recover. Trust is a lagging indicator: it falls fast after a hack and recovers slowly, if at all. If the next LocalCircles survey, or any comparable poll, shows zero trust falling back below 60%, that would suggest the Binance shock is fading. If it stays at 66% or rises, the damage is structural.

The third is the digital rupee. The 56% willingness figure is the government's strongest card in this debate. If the RBI expands digital-rupee availability through banks and it becomes genuinely easy to use for everyday payments, the argument that "Indians want digital money, just not private crypto" gets much stronger — and the case for liberalising private crypto gets correspondingly weaker.

## The Bottom Line

A LocalCircles survey of more than 43,000 Indians across 311 districts, reported on 9 November 2026, found that 59% of those who answered the regulation question favour a complete ban on cryptocurrencies, and 82% of respondents have little or no trust in digital currencies. Zero trust has risen from 49% in 2021 to 66% today, while most households — 76% — have no involvement with crypto at all. The backdrop is a year of major exchange hacks, led by the Binance breach of 29 October 2026, and the contrast is the digital rupee, which 56% of respondents said they would be willing to use.

For Indian crypto users, the survey is not a prediction and not a law. It is a clear, well-measured statement of public mood: the Indian public is sceptical of private crypto, supportive of a ban, and open to the central bank's own digital currency instead. The rules have not changed — crypto remains legal, taxed at 30% plus cess with 1% TDS, and exchanges must be FIU-IND-registered — but the politics around those rules have become harder for the industry. The sensible response is the unglamorous one: clean records, registered exchanges, self-custody for long-term holdings, and no decisions made on the basis of a headline.
