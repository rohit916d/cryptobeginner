# ZebPay MoEngage Data Breach: What Indian Users Must Know

ZebPay, one of India's oldest crypto exchanges, warned some of its customers on 21 November 2026 that their personal details had been exposed — not because ZebPay itself was hacked, but because a company it hires to send its messages was. The breach happened at MoEngage, a customer-engagement platform, and the attacker reached ZebPay user data through compromised credentials on MoEngage's side. ZebPay says its own systems were untouched and customer funds are safe. The real danger now is not a drained wallet — it is a phone call, text or email that sounds exactly like ZebPay, because the caller knows details only ZebPay should have. This guide explains what happened in the ZebPay MoEngage data breach, what was exposed and what was not, and what Indian users should do next.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a reported security incident; it does not recommend any token, investment or action, and it does not predict any price movement.

## What Happened: A Breach at the Vendor, Not the Exchange

On 21 November 2026, ZebPay sent a notice to affected customers saying that an unauthorised party had used compromised credentials to access the cloud infrastructure of MoEngage and had taken information relating to some of its users, including ZebPay users. The disclosure was first reported by crypto.news and was corroborated the same day by Moneycontrol, ET Now, BitcoinWorld and other outlets, all carrying the same core facts.

MoEngage is the platform ZebPay uses to communicate with customers — to send notifications, run campaigns and manage how it reaches users across email, app and web. When you sign up with an exchange, your contact details do not stay only with the exchange. They flow into the tools the exchange uses to talk to you, and those tools become part of the exchange's security perimeter whether or not the exchange treats them that way.

MoEngage confirmed the breach on 21 November as well. The company said the incident took place in late October 2026 and originated through a compromised third-party vendor account — one of MoEngage's own service providers — which allowed unauthorised access to a limited part of its cloud infrastructure. MoEngage said it contained the breach quickly, that it affected fewer than 1 per cent of its customer accounts, and that all affected customers were notified directly. It also said there was no disruption to its services as a result of the incident, and it did not disclose further details, including what additional security measures it has put in place.

The timeline, as reported, runs like this:

| Date | What happened |
|---|---|
| Late October 2026 | Breach at MoEngage through a compromised third-party vendor account |
| ~13–19 November 2026 | Some MoEngage customers lose access to the platform for nearly a week, per The Economic Times |
| 19 November 2026 | Some affected customers regain access to MoEngage, per The Economic Times |
| 20 November 2026 | MoEngage sends breach-notification emails to its clients |
| 21 November 2026 | ZebPay warns affected users; MoEngage confirms the breach publicly |

ZebPay has not said how many of its own users were affected, and MoEngage has not named the other clients whose data was involved. The public numbers are only the ones MoEngage gave: fewer than 1 per cent of its customer accounts. What is clear is that the exposure was real enough for ZebPay to warn users individually and to report the incident to the appropriate authorities in India.

## What Data Was Exposed — and What Was Not

ZebPay's notice listed the categories of information involved rather than itemising individual records, so no affected user was told exactly which of their fields were taken. The categories, as described in the notice and in the reporting of it, were:

- **Basic identifiers** — the kind of details that identify an account holder
- **Contact details** — email addresses and phone numbers used for communication
- **Online and device data** — information about how and from where a user accesses services
- **App settings and event data** — the preferences and activity records MoEngage holds to group audiences and send messages
- **Recent account activity** — a user's latest activity on the platform

What was not exposed matters just as much. ZebPay says its own servers, databases, wallets and trading systems were not accessed or affected, and that customer funds and crypto holdings were not at risk. The exchange also said it had found no unauthorised activity in the account of the customer receiving the notice. There is no report of passwords, wallet seed phrases or private keys being taken, and nothing in the reporting suggests the attacker could move funds or log into ZebPay accounts directly.

That distinction is important, but it is also easy to misunderstand. A breach that takes no money can still be dangerous, because the data it takes is exactly the raw material of impersonation fraud. A scammer who knows your name, your email, your phone number, the device you use and what you did on an exchange recently does not need to break into your account. They only need to sound like someone who already knows you.

## How the Breach Happened: Compromised Vendor Credentials

The mechanism, as far as it has been disclosed, was straightforward: compromised credentials. An unauthorised party used stolen login details to get into MoEngage's cloud infrastructure. MoEngage traced the incident back to a compromise at one of its own third-party service providers, which makes this a supply-chain incident — a breach that reaches a company through a firm it works with, rather than through a direct attack on its own systems.

Supply-chain attacks have become a recurring theme in crypto security in 2026, and Indian users have now seen the pattern twice in one week. On 17 November, CoinDCX told customers that an unauthorised party had accessed Plotline, a third-party service it uses for in-app messages, in two windows — 24–26 October and 30–31 October — and that a small number of customers' names, email addresses and linked bank names had been accessed. That incident, covered here on 18 November, was the same shape as this one: a messaging vendor, compromised credentials, customer contact data exposed, and the exchange's own trading systems and funds unaffected.

The ZebPay case adds one more layer. The compromised credentials belonged not to MoEngage itself but to one of its vendors — a third party of a third party. Each link in that chain is a place where security can fail, and the exchange at the end of the chain has no direct control over any of them. That is the uncomfortable reality of how modern apps are built: your data is only as safe as the weakest vendor in a chain you never see.

It is worth noting what this incident is not. It is separate from two other ZebPay service issues reported in the same month. On 11 November, the exchange said a technical problem at a banking partner had slowed Indian rupee withdrawals. Separately, ZebPay paused crypto withdrawals during platform upgrades while keeping crypto deposits open, with a warning that deposits could take longer to process. Those were operational issues — a banking partner and a maintenance window — not security incidents, and crypto.news explicitly distinguished them from the MoEngage breach. Mixing them up would give the wrong picture: the breach is about data and phishing risk, not about whether withdrawals work.

## Why This Matters More Than It Looks: The Phishing Risk

ZebPay's customer warning centred on fraud risk rather than account safety, and that framing is deliberate. The exchange warned that a caller, texter or emailer may cite personal or transaction details to sound like a genuine ZebPay representative. When a scammer can open a call with your name, your email, the device you trade on and your recent activity, the usual warning signs — "how would they know that?" — stop working.

This is how the next phase of an incident like this usually plays out. The stolen data gets sorted, packaged and sold or shared. Then the messages start: a call from "ZebPay support" about suspicious activity on your account, a text with a link to "verify" your details, an email warning that your account will be locked unless you act now. Each one is designed to create urgency and to borrow the credibility of the details the attacker already holds.

Indian crypto users are a particularly attractive target for this kind of fraud. The country's crypto rules — a flat 30% tax on gains, 1% TDS on transfers, no set-off of losses — mean many users are already anxious about compliance and account status, and a message that mentions tax, verification or account security lands on ready-made worry. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. Add to that the fact that Indian users are used to receiving genuine calls and messages from exchanges about KYC, banking partners and withdrawals, and the line between a real message and a fake one gets very thin.

The good news is that the defence is simple and does not depend on knowing whether your own data was taken. It depends on one rule: never trust an incoming message on its own say-so, no matter how much it knows about you. Verify through a channel you open yourself — the app, the official website, or a number you already have — and never through a link or number the message itself provides.

## ZebPay vs CoinDCX Plotline: A Pattern, Not a Coincidence

The two vendor breaches of November 2026 are worth comparing, because together they show Indian users something important about where their risk actually sits.

| | ZebPay / MoEngage | CoinDCX / Plotline |
|---|---|---|
| Vendor | MoEngage (customer engagement) | Plotline (in-app messaging) |
| How the attacker got in | Compromised credentials, via a MoEngage vendor | Unauthorised access to the vendor's systems |
| When it happened | Late October 2026 | 24–26 October and 30–31 October 2026 |
| When users were told | 21 November 2026 | 17 November 2026 |
| Data exposed | Basic identifiers, contact details, online and device data, app settings and event data, recent account activity | Names, email addresses, linked bank names (small number of customers) |
| Exchange systems affected? | No — servers, databases, wallets and trading systems unaffected | No — trading systems and customer funds unaffected |
| Funds at risk? | No, per ZebPay | No, per CoinDCX |

Neither incident drained a wallet. Both exposed the contact data that makes phishing work. And both were disclosed weeks after the underlying access happened — which is normal for breach investigations, but means the data may already have been circulating before users were warned.

For Indian users, the lesson is not to pick one exchange over another based on these two incidents. It is to understand that the exchanges you use share the same kind of vendors, and that a breach at a vendor you have never heard of can put your data in a stranger's hands. The question to ask any exchange is not "have you been hacked?" but "who holds my data besides you, and what do you do when one of them is breached?"

## What Indian ZebPay Users Should Do Now

If you use ZebPay — or if you are not sure whether you were among the users notified — here is the practical checklist, in order of importance.

**Do not act on unexpected contact claiming to be ZebPay.** If you receive a call, text or email about your account, do not follow its instructions, click its links or call its numbers. Open the ZebPay app yourself, go to the Help section and start a chat through the Support Centre. That is the channel ZebPay itself points users to for verification. When submitting a support ticket, use the email address registered to your ZebPay account.

**Never share a password, OTP, two-factor code or seed phrase.** ZebPay's notice was explicit: its staff will never ask you to share a password, a one-time password, a two-factor authentication code or a wallet seed phrase. They will never ask you to install a remote-access app, and they will never ask you to transfer funds to a wallet or account. Any message that asks for any of these is a scam, no matter how official it looks or how much it knows about you.

**Report suspicious messages.** ZebPay's security guidance lists a dedicated address for reporting suspicious emails: phishing@zebpay.com. In India, suspected fraud should be reported to the National Cyber Crime Helpline on 1930 or through the cybercrime portal, cybercrime.gov.in. Reporting matters twice over: it can stop a scam in progress, and it helps authorities track which stolen datasets are being used.

**Turn on two-factor authentication everywhere it is offered.** If you have not already enabled two-factor authentication on your ZebPay account and on the email account linked to it, do it now. An attacker who knows your contact details still cannot log in without the second factor — and your email account is the key to password resets on every service you use, so it deserves the same protection.

**Use a unique password for each exchange.** If the password on your ZebPay account is the same one you use elsewhere, change it, and use a different one for every exchange and every important account. A breach at a vendor does not expose your exchange password, but password reuse turns any future leak into a skeleton key.

**Watch for the slow burn.** Phishing built on stolen data does not always arrive the next day. It can arrive weeks or months later, when you have forgotten the breach happened. Stay sceptical of unexpected account-related messages for the foreseeable future, and be especially careful around messages that mention your recent activity or device — those are the details this breach exposed.

## How to Spot a Fake ZebPay Message

Scammers succeed by making the fake feel routine. Here are the signs that should make you stop and verify through the app instead:

- **Urgency.** "Your account will be locked in 24 hours", "verify now or lose access", "suspicious activity detected — act immediately". Real support messages do not rush you into decisions.
- **A request for a code or a secret.** Any ask for a password, OTP, two-factor code or seed phrase is a scam. Full stop.
- **A request to install something.** No exchange will ask you to install a remote-access app to "secure" your account.
- **A request to move funds.** No exchange will ask you to transfer crypto or rupees to a "safe" wallet or account.
- **A link or number in the message itself.** Even if the link looks right, do not use it. Open the app and navigate to support from inside it.
- **Details used as proof.** A caller who knows your email, your device or your recent activity is not proving they are ZebPay. Those are the details this breach put into circulation.

## The Bigger Lesson: Your Data Lives in More Places Than Your Exchange

The ZebPay MoEngage breach is a small incident in money terms — no funds were taken, and the number of affected accounts is, by MoEngage's account, under 1 per cent of its customer base. But it is a useful incident, because it makes visible something that is usually invisible: the chain of companies that handle your data when you use a crypto exchange.

MoEngage is a Bengaluru and San Francisco-based customer-engagement platform, founded in 2014, that provides AI-enabled tools for marketing and product teams across mobile, email, web and other channels. It is exactly the kind of company most users never hear of — until it appears in a breach notice. Your exchange is the name on the app, but your data also lives with the firms that send its emails, run its notifications, analyse its users and support its operations. Each of those firms has its own vendors, and each vendor is a potential point of failure.

That does not mean Indian users should stop using exchanges. It means they should stop assuming that an exchange's security is the only security that matters. The practical defences are the same ones this site has recommended after every incident this year: two-factor authentication, unique passwords, scepticism toward unexpected contact, and verification through channels you open yourself. Those defences work against vendor breaches, exchange breaches and ordinary phishing alike, because they do not depend on knowing which company failed — only on refusing to hand over the keys.

## The Bottom Line

On 21 November 2026, ZebPay warned affected customers that a breach at its communications provider MoEngage had exposed their contact details, device data and recent account activity. The breach, in late October, came through compromised credentials at one of MoEngage's own vendors and affected fewer than 1 per cent of MoEngage's customer accounts. ZebPay says its own servers, wallets and trading systems were not touched and customer funds are safe — and the incident is separate from the exchange's recent INR withdrawal delays and crypto withdrawal pause, which were operational issues, not security incidents. The defence is simple and it is in your hands — verify through the app, never share a password, OTP, two-factor code or seed phrase, and report anything suspicious to phishing@zebpay.com and to the National Cyber Crime Helpline on 1930.
