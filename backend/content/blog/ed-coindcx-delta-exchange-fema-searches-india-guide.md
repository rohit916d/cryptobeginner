On 6 November 2026, India's Enforcement Directorate (ED) announced that it had searched the Bengaluru premises of two of the country's best-known crypto platforms — CoinDCX and Delta Exchange — over alleged violations of the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), 1999. The searches, carried out on 4 and 5 November at three premises, are the second major ED action against Indian crypto exchanges in the space of ten days, and they put a spotlight on a question many Indian users have never had to think about: when you deposit rupees on an Indian exchange and trade crypto, where does your money actually go?

The agency's allegation, in short, is that customer rupee deposits were converted into the stablecoin USDT and routed through overseas exchanges to trade products that Indian platforms are not authorised to offer — crypto derivatives and tokenised shares of foreign companies — with volumes running into thousands of crores of rupees. This guide explains what the ED said, what FEMA actually is, why the routing matters, and what Indian crypto users should take away from it. Nothing here is proven yet: the investigation is ongoing, and the allegations have not been tested in court.

## What Happened: The Searches of 4–5 November

According to the ED's press release of 6 November 2026, reported the same day by NDTV Profit, Moneycontrol, The Hindu BusinessLine, Coinpedia and NewsBytes, the agency's Bengaluru Zonal Office searched three premises linked to two companies: Neblio Technologies Pvt. Ltd., which operates CoinDCX, and Excelium Technologies Pvt. Ltd., which operates Delta Exchange India. The searches were carried out under Section 37 of FEMA, 1999 — the provision that lets the ED search premises and seize documents during a foreign-exchange investigation.

The case, the ED said, began with information alleging large-scale FEMA violations by the two companies, centred on unauthorised cross-border financial transactions involving crypto token derivatives and Real World Asset (RWA) tokens. The agency said the volume of these transactions runs into thousands of crores of rupees in each product line — spot trades on one side, and futures and options on the other.

CoinDCX responded to the reports by describing the visit in measured terms. The Hindu BusinessLine quoted the exchange as saying: "Officials from the Enforcement Directorate visited our Bengaluru office as a part of a routine enquiry." At the time the first reports were published, no response from Delta Exchange had been reported. The investigation is continuing, and the ED's findings so far are allegations at the search stage — no charges have been filed, and the allegations have not been proven.

## What the ED Alleged: The USDT Routing Chain

The heart of the allegation is a chain of money movement that, if proven, would show Indian customer deposits leaving the country through crypto rails instead of the banking system. Here is the chain as the ED described it, step by step:

**Step 1: Rupees in.** Indian customers deposited rupees on the platforms, as normal users do — through UPI or bank transfer — expecting to trade crypto.

**Step 2: Rupees converted to USDT.** According to the ED, CoinDCX converted those customer rupee deposits into USDT, the dollar-pegged stablecoin, through an account held in the name of a Costa Rica-based entity. In other words, the conversion step happened through an overseas paper entity rather than through an Indian banking channel.

**Step 3: USDT routed offshore.** The USDT was then moved to overseas crypto exchanges — the ED named Binance, KuCoin, Bybit, Gate.io and Hyperliquid — where the actual trading happened.

**Step 4: Products traded offshore.** On those foreign exchanges, the funds were used for spot crypto trades, crypto futures and options, and trades in tokenised stocks of foreign companies — products that the ED says the Indian platforms were not authorised to offer.

**Step 5: Fees paid offshore.** The ED further alleged that CoinDCX paid hundreds of crores of rupees in transaction fees to the foreign exchanges through USDT — again, the agency says, bypassing the prescribed foreign-exchange framework.

The ED also said both platforms offered crypto futures and options and openly advertised trading in tokenised stocks of foreign companies, despite allegedly not being authorised to do so. And it added a legal conclusion of its own: the products offered appear, prima facie, to be contrary to the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956, the SEBI Act, 1992 and the RBI Act, 1934.

Two things are worth noting about the scale. First, "thousands of crores" is the ED's own characterisation of the volumes — no precise figure has been published. Second, the allegation covers each product line separately: spot trades running into thousands of crores, and derivatives running into thousands of crores as well. The case is therefore not about a single transaction or a small accounting error; it is about the business model itself, as the ED sees it.

## What Is FEMA, and Why Does It Apply to Crypto?

For most Indian crypto users, FEMA is a law they have never read — and that is exactly why this case matters. The Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 is the law that governs how money crosses India's borders. Its basic principle is simple: any payment to or from a person outside India must go through authorised channels, and certain kinds of transactions need permission.

Here is how that connects to crypto:

**Rupees cannot simply be sent abroad for any purpose.** Under FEMA, moving money out of India for investments, trading or payments generally has to happen through an authorised dealer — normally a bank — under the Reserve Bank of India's Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS), which currently allows resident Indians to remit up to $250,000 per financial year for permitted purposes. Buying crypto on a foreign exchange or trading offshore derivatives is not a permitted use of that route in the way the rules are currently read.

**Stablecoins do not change the law.** USDT is a token that tracks the US dollar, and moving it across borders feels like sending an email — fast, cheap, and borderless. But Indian regulators treat the underlying transaction, not the technology, as the thing that matters. If rupees are converted into USDT and the USDT is sent to a foreign exchange to trade, the economic substance is a cross-border transfer of value — and FEMA's rules on cross-border transfers apply regardless of whether the value travelled by bank wire or by blockchain.

**Derivatives and tokenised stocks are regulated products in India.** Futures and options on securities are governed by the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act and SEBI; tokenised shares of foreign companies raise the same questions as buying foreign shares directly, which is regulated by the RBI and SEBI. An Indian platform offering these products to Indian users without authorisation is, on the ED's reading, offering products outside the legal framework — which is why the agency cited three different Acts, not just FEMA.

**FEMA is a civil law with serious penalties.** Unlike the older FERA it replaced, FEMA is primarily civil rather than criminal. But its penalty provision allows fines of up to three times the sum involved where the amount is quantifiable — which is why cases described in "thousands of crores" carry such large potential consequences. Adjudication happens through FEMA's own process, and the ED's searches are the evidence-gathering stage, not the verdict.

## What Are Crypto Derivatives and Tokenised Stocks?

Two of the products at the centre of this case are unfamiliar to many beginners, so it helps to explain them plainly.

**Crypto derivatives** are contracts whose value follows the price of a cryptocurrency, without the buyer necessarily owning the coin. The most common are futures (an agreement to buy or sell at a set price later) and options (the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell at a set price). They are popular with professional traders because they allow leverage — controlling a large position with a small amount of money — but leverage also magnifies losses, and a small price move can wipe out the whole position. Our guide to crypto leverage explains why beginners are generally advised to avoid these products.

**RWA tokens and tokenised stocks** are blockchain tokens that represent ownership of a real-world asset. A tokenised share of a foreign company, for example, is a token that is supposed to track the price of that company's stock — Apple, Tesla or Nvidia, say — and give the holder economic exposure to it. For Indian users, this is attractive on paper: it looks like a way to invest in foreign stocks without a foreign brokerage account. But buying foreign shares from India is governed by the LRS and by securities law, and a token that mimics a share does not escape those rules simply because it lives on a blockchain.

The ED's allegation is that both platforms were offering these products to Indian users from India, while the actual trading was happening offshore — combining two regulatory problems at once: unauthorised products, and unauthorised cross-border money movement.

## How This Case Differs From the ED's 27 October Searches

This is not the first time the ED has searched Indian crypto platforms, and readers who follow the news may feel a sense of déjà vu. But this is a different case from the one reported ten days earlier, and the distinction matters.

On 29 October, we covered ED searches carried out on 27 October in Bengaluru and Madhya Pradesh against a different set of platforms — Mudrex, Saber.money, Fluid and Instadapp were named in the reporting — over alleged FEMA contraventions involving unauthorised foreign-platform balances. That case centred on different companies, different dates and different alleged conduct.

The CoinDCX and Delta Exchange case is a separate enforcement action: different exchanges, searches on 4 and 5 November, and a different alleged mechanism — the USDT routing chain through a Costa Rica entity to named overseas exchanges, plus the offering of derivatives and tokenised foreign stocks. The two cases together suggest the ED is conducting a broader examination of how Indian crypto platforms handle cross-border flows, but they are not the same investigation, and neither has reached a conclusion.

There is also a separate CoinDCX story that readers should not confuse with this one. In late October, CoinDCX confirmed a deposit-wallet incident that led to losses of about $16 million — a security event, covered in our explainer at the time. The ED case is not about that incident, and no link between the two has been alleged. Keeping the two stories separate is important, because they raise different questions: one about exchange security, the other about foreign-exchange compliance.

## What This Means for Indian Crypto Users

Enforcement news about a platform you use is unsettling, even when the allegations are unproven. Here is a practical reading of what this case does and does not change for an ordinary Indian user.

**Your funds on an exchange are a separate question from the exchange's compliance.** The ED case alleges unauthorised cross-border routing of transactions. It does not allege that customer funds were stolen or lost. Even so, the general safety rules still apply: keep only the money you are actively trading on an exchange, move long-term holdings to self-custody in a wallet you control, enable two-factor authentication, and never share your seed phrase or OTP with anyone.

**Check whether your exchange is FIU-registered.** India's Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU-IND) registration is the basic compliance marker for crypto exchanges serving Indian users. Our guide explains how to check whether an exchange is FIU-registered — it takes two minutes, and it is the single most useful check a beginner can do before depositing rupees anywhere.

**Be cautious about offshore derivatives and tokenised stocks.** If a platform offers you crypto futures, options or tokenised foreign shares while you are in India, pause before using them. These products are not part of the standard authorised offering for Indian retail users, and as this case shows, regulators are actively examining them. The potential upside of leverage is real, but so is the risk of losing your entire position on a small price move — and, as the ED alleges here, the regulatory risk as well.

**Keep records of everything.** If you trade crypto in India, keep a simple record of every deposit, trade and withdrawal — dates, amounts in rupees, and the platform used. This matters for tax filing, and it matters even more if a platform you use ever faces an investigation: clear records are the fastest way to show your own transactions were ordinary.

**Remember the Indian tax picture.** None of this changes how crypto is taxed in India. Gains on crypto are taxed at a flat 30%, plus a 1% TDS on transfers above the threshold, and losses cannot be set off against other income. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

## What Happens Next

The honest answer is that nobody outside the ED knows yet. Here is the sequence that typically follows a search under FEMA, and what to watch for:

**The investigation continues.** After searches, the ED examines seized documents and data, records statements, and may issue summonses. This stage can take months. Further developments — additional searches, a show-cause notice, or a closure of the inquiry — would each be reported in their own right.

**The exchanges' responses matter.** CoinDCX has described the visit as a routine enquiry. If either company issues a fuller statement, or if the ED publishes further findings, that will shape how the case is understood. Watch for statements from the companies themselves rather than relying on social-media commentary.

**Adjudication, not arrest, is the usual FEMA route.** FEMA cases are generally adjudicated by an adjudicating authority, which can impose penalties after giving the parties a hearing. The outcome could range from no violation found, to a penalty, to directions about how the platforms must operate. Nothing about that outcome can be predicted from a press release.

**The broader regulatory direction is the real story.** Two ED actions in ten days, both about cross-border crypto flows, suggest that offshore routing is now a sustained enforcement focus — alongside the BWA self-regulatory body's voluntary guidelines and the FIU registration regime. For Indian users, the practical direction of travel is clear: the compliant path is rupee spot trading on FIU-registered Indian exchanges, with records kept and taxes filed. Anything that routes your money offshore through stablecoins deserves a hard second look.

## The Bottom Line

The ED searched three Bengaluru premises linked to CoinDCX and Delta Exchange on 4 and 5 November 2026, and announced on 6 November that it alleges unauthorised cross-border transactions worth thousands of crores of rupees — rupee deposits converted into USDT, routed through a Costa Rica-based entity to overseas exchanges, and used to trade crypto derivatives and tokenised foreign stocks that the agency says the platforms were not authorised to offer. CoinDCX called the visit a routine enquiry; the investigation is ongoing and the allegations are unproven.

For Indian users, the case is a useful lesson in a rule that is easy to forget: crypto may be borderless, but money is not. The technology makes it simple to move value across borders in seconds — and the law still asks where the value came from, where it went, and whether the route was authorised. Understanding that distinction is more valuable than any single enforcement headline, because it is the rule that will decide which platforms and products are safe to use in India in the years ahead.
