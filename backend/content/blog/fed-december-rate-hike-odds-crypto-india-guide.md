The Federal Reserve raising interest rates in December is now priced by markets as a near-certainty — and that single shift in expectations is the force behind much of the crypto sell-off Indian readers have watched over the past week. On Saturday 14 November 2026, the CME FedWatch tool assigned an 88.5% probability to a 25-basis-point rate hike at the Fed's 8–9 December meeting, with 11.5% for a larger 50-basis-point move and nothing at all for rates staying unchanged or being cut. Prediction markets agreed: Kalshi traders priced a 25-basis-point hike at 89%, and Polymarket at 88%. This guide explains what those odds actually are, how they flipped so fast, why a US rate hike matters for Bitcoin and crypto in India, and what to watch before the December meeting — without predicting what the Fed will do or where any price will go.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a market event; it does not recommend buying, selling or holding any asset.

## What Happened: The Facts at a Glance

The facts, as reported on 13–15 November 2026 by Bitcoin.com News, CoinStats, CoinLab, Summa Money and Coin24 News, are:

- **A December hike is now priced at close to 100%.** According to the CME FedWatch tool, as reviewed by Bitcoin.com News on Saturday morning, futures traders assigned an 88.5% probability to the Federal Reserve lifting its benchmark rate by 25 basis points at the 8–9 December meeting, and 11.5% to a 50-basis-point increase. No probability was assigned to unchanged rates or to a cut. A separate market roundup put the total probability of any hike at 100% as of the 13 November close, with about 87% for a 25-basis-point move and about 13% for a 50-basis-point move.
- **Prediction markets agree.** Kalshi traders priced a 25-basis-point hike at 89%, compared with 10% for a larger increase, according to Bitcoin.com News. On Polymarket, the corresponding probabilities stood at 88% for 25 basis points and approximately 9% for 50 basis points, with cumulative volume exceeding $33.7 million.
- **The shift happened in about a week.** On 6 November, CME FedWatch assigned a 23.7% probability to rates staying unchanged and 76.3% to a quarter-point increase. By 14 November, the "unchanged" probability had gone to zero. A week earlier, the odds of a 50-basis-point hike had been 0%; by 13 November they were about 11–13%, according to CNBC data cited in the week's coverage.
- **Hot inflation data was the trigger.** October's US consumer price index, released on 10 November, rose 0.4% month-on-month against a forecast of 0.3%, and core CPI — which excludes food and energy — rose 0.3% against a forecast of 0.2%. Bank of America told clients the data "all but lock in" a December hike and opened the door to a 50-basis-point move, while the bank itself still forecasts 25 basis points.
- **A Fed governor floated the bigger move.** Fed Governor Christopher Waller said in a speech before the CPI release that he leans toward a 25-basis-point increase in December, but that if core CPI came in around 0.3% or higher, "a 0.5% rate hike should be considered", according to Reuters. Core CPI then printed exactly 0.3%.
- **Bond yields and the dollar moved with the odds.** The US 10-year Treasury yield rose nearly 10 basis points to 4.95% after the CPI data, according to CNBC, and by one weekly measure it rose 29.5 basis points to 5.154%, its largest weekly increase since April 2025. The Dollar Index rose to 103.41, its highest level in about 19 months.
- **Bitcoin fell as the odds climbed.** Bitcoin touched $65,020 on Bitstamp on Saturday morning as inflation concerns and tighter-policy expectations weighed on risk assets, according to Bitcoin.com News, and then fell further to $63,320 later on Saturday — the move this site covered on 15 November in its post on Bitcoin below $65,000 and $1.9 billion in liquidations. US spot Bitcoin ETFs lost about $1.07 billion in the week to 13 November, the second straight week above $1 billion in outflows, and almost $3 billion from 30 October to 13 November.

## What FedWatch and the Prediction Markets Actually Measure

For a beginner, the most confusing thing about "88.5% probability of a hike" is what the number is. Nobody at the Federal Reserve voted, and nobody polled traders. The number comes from prices.

The CME FedWatch tool reads the prices of federal funds futures — contracts traded on the Chicago Mercantile Exchange in which traders bet on where the Fed's benchmark interest rate will be after a given meeting. If the contracts are priced as though a 25-basis-point hike is almost certain, the tool converts that pricing into a probability. The logic is simple: traders put real money behind their views, so the market price is a summary of what thousands of traders, collectively, think is most likely. The tool is widely watched precisely because it is a market price, not an opinion poll.

Kalshi and Polymarket work differently but measure the same thing. They are prediction markets where people buy and sell contracts that pay out if a stated event happens — in this case, "the Fed raises rates by 25 basis points in December". The price of the contract is the market's probability. On 14 November, Kalshi's contract for a 25-basis-point hike traded at a price implying 89%, and Polymarket's at 88%. The fact that three different markets — futures, Kalshi and Polymarket — all landed within a point of each other is what made the reading so striking. Three independent pricing mechanisms agreed.

Two caveats matter, and the coverage on 14 November stated both of them. First, these figures represent market expectations, not confirmed policy. The Federal Open Market Committee has not announced its December decision, and it will not do so until the meeting ends on 9 December. Second, the three platforms use different pricing methodologies and contract specifications, so the numbers are not identical and should not be treated as a single precise forecast. What they are is a consensus: the market has moved from "probably a hike" to "almost certainly a hike", and from "no chance of 50 basis points" to "a real, if small, chance".

## How the Odds Flipped in a Week

The speed of the flip is the story. Just a week earlier, the picture looked different in two ways. On 6 November, CME FedWatch still gave a 23.7% chance that rates would stay unchanged in December — a meaningful minority betting on no move at all. And the probability of a 50-basis-point hike was 0%, according to CNBC data: nobody in the futures market was pricing the bigger step.

Then came the inflation data. October's CPI, released on Tuesday 10 November, beat forecasts on both the headline and the core measures: 0.4% month-on-month against 0.3% expected, and 0.3% core against 0.2% expected. Inflation running hotter than expected is the single most important input to the Fed's decision, because the Fed's job is to keep inflation under control, and raising rates is its main tool for doing so. When inflation comes in hot, the market's first reaction is to price in more tightening, and that is exactly what happened: the odds of a hold collapsed to zero, and the odds of a 50-basis-point hike appeared from nowhere.

Fed Governor Waller's speech sharpened the move. Speaking in London before the CPI release, he said he leans toward a 25-basis-point increase in December, but that if core CPI came in around 0.3% or higher, "a 0.5% rate hike should be considered". Core CPI then printed exactly 0.3% — the threshold Waller had named. That sequence — a named threshold, then the data landing on it — is why Bank of America told clients the CPI data "all but locks in" a December hike. The bank itself still forecasts 25 basis points, not 50, but the fact that a major bank is even discussing a 50-basis-point move shows how far the debate has moved in a week.

The bond market confirmed the repricing. The 10-year US Treasury yield rose to around 4.95–5.15% during the week, and the Dollar Index climbed to 103.41, its highest in about 19 months. When yields rise, holding cash and bonds pays more; when the dollar rises, assets priced in dollars get more expensive for buyers using other currencies. Both moves are the market's way of saying the same thing the FedWatch odds say: money is expected to get tighter, and to stay tighter for longer.

## Why a US Rate Hike Moves Crypto

The link between US interest rates and crypto prices is one of the most consistent patterns in the market, and it works through three channels that are easy to understand.

**First, rates set the price of safety.** When the Fed raises rates, US government bonds pay more interest. A 10-year Treasury yield near 5% means an investor can earn about 5% a year from an asset backed by the US government. Crypto pays no interest at all. That does not make crypto worthless — people hold it for other reasons — but it raises the bar: money that might have gone into riskier assets has a safer alternative paying a real return. When rates were near zero, that alternative barely existed. At 5%, it does, and the flow of money reflects it: US spot Bitcoin ETFs lost about $1.07 billion in the week to 13 November, the second straight week above $1 billion.

**Second, higher rates strengthen the dollar.** When US rates rise relative to other countries, global investors buy dollars to earn the higher return, and the dollar rises. The Dollar Index at 103.41 is the visible result. Bitcoin is priced in dollars worldwide, so a stronger dollar tends to push the dollar price of Bitcoin down, and it makes Bitcoin more expensive in local currency for buyers outside the US — including in India, where the rupee has been trading around ₹94 to the dollar. This site covered the strong dollar's role in the same sell-off on 13 November, in its post on Bitcoin below $70,000.

**Third, rate expectations move faster than rates themselves.** The Fed has not actually raised rates yet — the meeting is on 8–9 December, and the current target range is still 3.75% to 4%. But markets trade on expectations, not on the announcement. Bitcoin fell to $65,020 on Saturday morning and then to $63,320 as the hike odds climbed, before any rate decision had been made. That is why the FedWatch odds matter even though they are only a probability: the market reprices assets the moment expectations change, and the price move happens in advance of the event itself.

It is worth adding that the link is not a law. Crypto has risen during periods of rising rates before, and it has fallen during periods of cuts. Rates are one of several forces — the week's coverage also named technology-stock weakness, the fallout from the Binance hack, and ETF outflows as pressures on the market. But in the week to 14 November, the rate story was the one that changed the most, the fastest, and it is the one the coverage most consistently tied to Bitcoin's fall.

## What This Means for India

For Indian readers, the Fed's December decision matters through the rupee, through the price of Bitcoin in rupees, and through the tax rules that do not change with the market.

The arithmetic is straightforward. Bitcoin is priced in dollars globally, and Indians buy it with rupees. When the dollar strengthens — as it has, to a 19-month high against major currencies — one Bitcoin costs more rupees even if the dollar price does not move. And when the dollar price falls at the same time, as it did to $63,320 on Saturday, the rupee price falls with it: one Bitcoin was worth roughly ₹60 lakh on 15 November, down from about ₹71–75 lakh when Bitcoin was near $75,000 in early October. The two forces pull in opposite directions, but in the past week the dollar-price fall has been much bigger than the rupee effect, so Indian holders have seen the rupee value of their holdings fall sharply.

Three India-specific points follow:

- **The tax does not change with the Fed.** Gains on virtual digital assets are taxed at a flat 30% plus a 4% cess, with 1% TDS on transfers, and those rates apply whether the market is rising or falling, and whether the Fed hikes or not. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. A fall does not create a tax refund, and losses on crypto cannot generally be set off against other income. The Fed's decision changes the market; it does not change the Indian tax treatment.
- **The premium Indians pay is part of the same story.** Indian buyers have been paying several percent more for Bitcoin than the global price for weeks — the India premium was about 6.68% at the 6 November daily fix, and live readings on 15 November were higher still. The premium exists because of capital controls, the 1% TDS that drove professional traders offshore, and limited local liquidity, and it tends to widen when markets are stressed. A US rate hike that tightens global liquidity can make that premium wider, not narrower, because it reduces the flow of dollars into Indian exchanges. The premium is a cost Indian buyers pay on top of the global price, whatever the Fed does.
- **The RBI is watching the same data, from the other side.** The Reserve Bank of India has been cautious about crypto banking all year — in June, RBI deputy governors said banks and financial institutions should stay away from crypto-related businesses — and the same week brought fresh evidence of how fragile the banking link is, with Coinbase halting INR withdrawals in India and Unocoin suspending services. A Fed hike that strengthens the dollar and tightens global liquidity does not make the RBI more likely to open the banking door to crypto. Indian readers should not expect the Fed's decision to change anything about how easily rupees move in and out of exchanges.

## What to Watch Before 8–9 December

The hike odds are a snapshot, not a verdict, and three scheduled releases before the meeting can move them. The coverage of the week listed them, and they are worth marking on a calendar rather than a price chart.

- **18 November — the Fed's meeting minutes.** The minutes of the October meeting, at which the Fed held rates with two dissents in favour of a hike, will show how many officials were already leaning toward tightening. If the minutes show a hawkish tilt, the odds of a 50-basis-point move could rise; if they show caution, the odds could ease back toward 25 basis points.
- **4 December — the US jobs report.** A strong jobs report supports the case for a hike, because a hot labour market feeds inflation; a weak one weakens it. The jobs data is the biggest single input between now and the meeting.
- **10 December — the next CPI print.** November's inflation figures arrive the day after the Fed meeting ends — too late to change the December decision, but they will shape expectations for the January meeting, and the market will trade on them immediately.

Beyond the data, the other thing to watch is whether the odds themselves move. If the 88.5% figure drifts down toward 70% or 60%, that is the market telling you the hike is less certain than it looked on Saturday. If the 50-basis-point probability climbs from 11.5% toward 25% or 30%, that is the market telling you the bigger move is gaining support. The odds are a live read on expectations, and they change every day the market is open.

## What This Does Not Mean

Three things are worth stating plainly, because a "near-certain" number is easy to over-read:

- **This is not a prediction that the Fed will hike.** An 88.5% probability is not a decision. The Federal Open Market Committee has not announced anything, and the members who will vote on 8–9 December have not voted. Markets have been wrong about the Fed before — the odds of a hold were 23.7% on 6 November and zero a week later, which shows how fast the consensus can move in either direction. A single soft jobs report or a dovish speech could move the odds back.
- **This is not a prediction that Bitcoin will keep falling.** Bitcoin fell as the hike odds rose, and the coverage tied the two together, but correlation is not a forecast. The same reports that named the hike odds also named technology-stock weakness, ETF outflows and the Binance hack fallout as pressures, and analysts are split on how much each one mattered. A market can fall on rate fears and then recover on the same fears if the actual decision turns out milder than expected — the classic "buy the rumour, sell the news" pattern works in reverse too.
