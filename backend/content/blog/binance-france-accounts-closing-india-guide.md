Binance is closing customer accounts in France. On 6–7 November 2026, the world's largest crypto exchange began emailing customers of its French entity to say their accounts will close on **31 December 2026**, and that from **29 November** any balance left behind will start attracting a monthly fee of 0.025% — or 10 USDC, roughly ₹940, whichever is higher. Similar notices went to users in Spain, Italy and Poland. The reason is not a hack and not a fraud allegation: Binance failed to win a licence under the European Union's Markets in Crypto-Assets regulation, known as **MiCA**, and without that licence it cannot legally keep serving customers in the EU.

This guide explains exactly what the closure covers, what the fee clock looks like, why MiCA forced Binance's hand, and what it means for Indian users — including the large number of Indians who live, study or work in Europe. Every figure is attributed to its source, and nothing here is a prediction or a recommendation about any coin or exchange.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a reported regulatory action; it does not recommend any coin, exchange or trade.

## What Binance Told Its Users

The notices went out by email on 6–7 November 2026, first reported by French crypto media and then picked up by CoinDesk, Bitcoin.com News, TradingView, Coinpedia and several other outlets on 7 November. The message to customers of Binance France was direct: deposits are no longer possible, and every account held with the French entity will be closed with effect from 31 December 2026.

A Binance spokesperson confirmed the plan to CoinDesk, saying: "Affected accounts will be closed with effect from 31 December 2026," and describing the wind-down as "a carefully managed process." The spokesperson added that "our commitment to Europe remains unchanged, and we are actively progressing our authorization pathway" — in other words, Binance says it still wants to operate in Europe, but it does not yet have the licence it needs.

The closure is not limited to France. CoinDesk reported on 7 November that users in Spain and Poland had received similar notices, and other outlets reported the same for Italy. Binance had already contacted users in France, Spain, Italy and Poland back in June 2026, urging them to withdraw their funds — the November emails are the final step in a wind-down that began months ago.

The scale is large. Around 2 million users in France are estimated to be affected, according to TradingView's news coverage, and Bitcoin.com News reported that about 2 million French users had already lost access to trading on 1 July 2026, when Binance limited its EU services. These are not small numbers: for many of those users, Binance was their main — sometimes only — crypto exchange.

Three practical points in the notices matter most to anyone holding funds there.

**First, deposits are already blocked.** Customers can no longer add fiat money or cryptocurrency to the affected accounts. Withdrawals, however, are still open — a Binance spokesperson told CoinDesk that customers can still withdraw their crypto and fiat balances. That is the window the whole notice is built around: take your money out while you still can.

**Second, the closure is orderly, not sudden.** Users have until 31 December 2026 — roughly seven weeks from the notice — before the accounts are closed. That is a long runway compared with, for example, an exchange freezing withdrawals overnight. But the fee clock starts much sooner, on 29 November, which is why the practical deadline for most users is the end of November, not the end of December.

**Third, this is a wind-down of specific local entities, not a shutdown of Binance.** The closure applies to accounts held with Binance France and the other affected local entities. It does not mean Binance is shutting down its global platform, and it does not affect accounts held with Binance entities outside the EU. That distinction matters a great deal for Indian users, as explained below — but it also matters for European users, because some of them may hold accounts with a different Binance entity and never receive a notice at all.

## The Fee Clock: What Happens to Money Left Behind

The most concrete part of the notice is the fee schedule, and it is designed to push users to withdraw. From 29 November 2026, any account that still holds assets will be charged a monthly management fee equal to **0.025% of the remaining balance, or 10 USDC, whichever is higher**.

| Date | What happens |
|---|---|
| 29 November 2026 | Monthly fee starts: 0.025% of balance or 10 USDC (~₹940), whichever is higher |
| Within 3 days of 29 November | First fee deduction from the account |
| 24 December 2026 | Second fee deduction |
| 31 December 2026 | All affected accounts close |

A few details of the fee are worth spelling out. The fee is charged per month, so a balance left in place through December would be charged twice — once around the end of November and once on 24 December. The 10 USDC minimum means the fee bites hardest on small balances: on a balance of, say, 100 USDC, the 0.025% calculation would be just 0.025 USDC, but the minimum of 10 USDC applies instead — a tenth of the balance in a single month. On larger balances the percentage applies: on 10,000 USDC the fee would be 2.50 USDC, below the minimum, so the 10 USDC charge still applies; only above 40,000 USDC does the 0.025% calculation exceed the 10 USDC floor.

The fee is deducted from USDC balances first. If there is not enough USDC in the account, it is taken from other cryptocurrencies held in the account, according to TradingView's coverage of the notice. And accounts that withdraw all their assets before 29 November will not be charged at all — the fee only applies to balances still sitting in the account when the clock starts.

What happens to assets still in the account after 31 December? According to reporting by Cointime and Bitcoin.com News, assets not withdrawn by then may be treated as belonging to inactive accounts and could be converted, transferred or otherwise handled by the exchange. Binance has not published a detailed public plan for unclaimed balances, so users should not assume their funds will simply wait for them. The safe reading of the notice is that 29 November is the deadline to avoid fees, and 31 December is the deadline to have the money out at all.

## Why MiCA Forced This: The Licence Binance Could Not Get

To understand why the world's largest exchange is closing accounts in one of Europe's biggest markets, you need to understand MiCA — the Markets in Crypto-Assets regulation.

MiCA is the European Union's rulebook for crypto. Before it existed, each EU country had its own, often very different, rules for crypto exchanges: a licence in France did not automatically let an exchange serve customers in Germany or Spain. MiCA replaced that patchwork with a single framework. Once an exchange is authorised in one EU member state, it can "passport" that authorisation across the whole bloc and serve customers in all 27 member states. The trade-off is that the requirements are demanding: capital, governance, custody, consumer protection and stablecoin rules that must all be met before authorisation is granted.

The key date is **30 June 2026**. That is when MiCA became mandatory for crypto-asset service providers and the transition period ended. From that date, an exchange needed a MiCA authorisation to serve customers anywhere in the EU. Binance did not have one — and still does not, as of 7 November 2026.

The sequence of events, as reported by CoinDesk and Bitcoin.com News, runs like this:

- **May 2022:** Binance France obtains registration with France's financial regulator, the Autorité des marchés financiers (AMF).
- **June 2026:** Binance withdraws its application for a Greek MiCA licence, saying it planned to seek authorisation in another EU member state — which it has yet to name publicly.
- **June 2026:** Binance first contacts users in France, Spain, Italy and Poland, urging them to withdraw their funds.
- **30 June 2026:** The MiCA transition period ends; MiCA authorisation becomes mandatory for crypto service providers in the EU.
- **1 July 2026:** Binance limits its EU services; about 2 million French users lose access to trading.
- **2 July 2026:** Binance France's AMF registration lapses automatically.
- **6–7 November 2026:** Binance emails customers of its French entity that accounts will close on 31 December 2026, with monthly fees from 29 November.
- **7 November 2026:** CoinDesk, Bitcoin.com News and others report that users in Spain and Poland have received similar notices.

The story, then, is not that a regulator ordered Binance to close. It is that Binance could not obtain the licence the law now requires, and rather than operate without one, it is winding down the affected entities in an orderly way. That is a meaningful distinction: it is a compliance failure, not an enforcement action. But from the user's point of view the result is the same — the account closes, and the money has to move.

It is also worth noting that Binance is not alone in finding MiCA hard. Several large exchanges have struggled with the authorisation process, and the regulation has already reshaped which platforms can operate in Europe. MiCA's supporters say that is the point: a single, serious licensing regime keeps out operators who cannot meet its standards. Its critics say the bar is so high that it pushes activity to exchanges outside the EU. Either way, the Binance France closure is the most visible consequence of MiCA so far — the largest exchange in the world, closing accounts for an estimated 2 million users in France alone, because it could not get the licence.

## What Is MiCA, in Plain Language?

For readers who have not followed European regulation, MiCA can be summed up in three ideas.

**One licence, 27 countries.** Under MiCA, a crypto exchange authorised in any one EU member state can serve customers across the entire bloc. Before MiCA, exchanges needed separate approvals in each country, or operated in a grey area. The passport is valuable — which is why losing access to it matters so much to Binance.

**Authorisation is not a formality.** To get a MiCA licence, an exchange must show it has enough capital, proper governance, secure custody of customer assets, clear rules on conflicts of interest, and systems to detect market abuse. The regulator in the member state reviews the application and can refuse it. Binance's experience shows that even the biggest exchange in the world can fail to clear that bar on its first attempts.

**Stablecoins get special treatment.** MiCA has detailed rules for stablecoins, including reserve and redemption requirements. That part of the regulation has already forced changes across the European crypto market, including the delisting or restriction of some stablecoins on EU platforms. It is one reason the regulation is watched closely by Indian readers too: Europe is effectively writing the template that other jurisdictions study when they design their own crypto rules.

India has no equivalent of MiCA. There is no comprehensive licensing regime for crypto exchanges in India, and no passport system. Instead, Indian exchanges must register with the Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU-IND) and follow know-your-customer and anti-money-laundering rules, while crypto gains are taxed at a flat 30% with 1% TDS on sales. The European model and the Indian model are very different — but the Binance France closure shows Indian users what a licensing deadline looks like in practice, and it is a reminder that registration and licensing are not paperwork: they decide whether an exchange is allowed to keep your account open.

## What It Means for Indian Users

The Binance France closure does not change the law for anyone living in India, and it does not close any account held in India. But it matters for Indian users in four practical ways.

**1. Indians living in Europe are directly affected.** Tens of thousands of Indians live, study or work in France, Spain, Italy and Poland — the countries named in the notices. If you are an Indian resident in one of those countries and your Binance account sits with the French entity or one of the other affected local entities, your account is covered by the closure. Check the email Binance sent you: it names the entity that holds your account. If it is Binance France or another affected local entity, withdraw your funds before 29 November to avoid the monthly fee, and before 31 December at the latest. Do not assume that because you are an Indian citizen, your account is held in India — what matters is which legal entity holds the account, not your passport.

**2. Indian Binance accounts are a different entity.** Binance's India-facing service is separate from its French entity. In January 2024, India's Financial Intelligence Unit blocked Binance's website and app in India for operating without registration; Binance subsequently registered with FIU-IND, and its India service continued. That registration is what allows Binance to serve Indian users today — and it is the Indian parallel to the MiCA licence Binance could not obtain in Europe. The France closure is a reminder of how much depends on that registration: if an exchange loses its right to operate in a jurisdiction, its users there lose their accounts, however large the exchange is.

**3. Your tax obligations do not depend on the platform.** Whether you trade on an Indian exchange, a European one or an offshore one, Indian tax law follows you as an Indian resident. Gains on crypto are taxed at a flat 30% plus a 4% cess — 31.2% in total — with no set-off of losses, and every sale attracts 1% TDS. If you withdraw funds from a Binance France account and later sell or convert crypto, those rules still apply to you, and you still need to report the income when you file your return.

Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

**4. Exchange risk is not only about hacks.** Indian readers have seen a run of exchange stories in the last two weeks: the Binance security incident and its $6 billion-plus aftermath, the ED searches at CoinDCX and Delta Exchange over alleged offshore routing of Indian users' trades, the CERT-In warning about AI-assisted attacks on crypto exchanges, and the US Treasury's order cutting off MEXC from the American financial system. Hacks get the headlines, but regulatory action can change what an exchange can do just as suddenly — closing accounts, charging fees on leftover balances, or cutting off banking access. No exchange, Indian or offshore, can guarantee that its regulatory position will never change.

## The Wider Pattern: Regulators Are Closing In

The Binance France closure is the third major regulatory deadline to land on crypto users in a single week, and the pattern is worth naming.

On 6 November, the US Treasury's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) ordered American financial institutions to stop moving money to or from MEXC Global, alleging at least $16 billion in transactions linked to sanctions evasion, cybercrime and other illicit activity between May 2021 and May 2026. That order takes effect 30 days after it is published in the Federal Register. In the same week, India's Enforcement Directorate searched three premises in Bengaluru linked to CoinDCX and Delta Exchange, alleging that Indian users' rupee deposits were converted into USDT and routed to offshore exchanges in breach of FEMA. The allegations in both cases are unproven and the companies dispute them — but the direction is unmistakable.

These three stories are different in law, in country and in allegation: a US funds-transmittal ban against MEXC, an Indian FEMA investigation into CoinDCX and Delta Exchange, and a European licensing failure at Binance France. What they share is the consequence for ordinary account holders. In each case, a regulatory deadline arrived and the user's options narrowed: withdraw by a date, pay a fee, or lose access. The Binance France case is the clearest example of the three, because the dates and the fees are published in advance and there is still time to act.

For Indian users, the lesson is the same one this site has repeated through the whole Binance saga: use exchanges that are registered where you live, keep your own records of deposits, trades and withdrawals, and do not leave on an exchange more than you are prepared to have tied up while a dispute or an investigation runs its course.

## A Checklist Before 29 November

If you hold a Binance account and you are not sure whether the closure affects you, work through these steps in order.

**1. Find the entity that holds your account.** Open the email Binance sent you, or check your account settings and terms of service. The closure covers accounts held with Binance France and the other affected local entities. If your account is held with a different Binance entity, the notice does not apply to you — but confirm that from the notice itself, not from a guess.

**2. Withdraw before 29 November.** If your account is affected, move your funds out before the fee clock starts. Withdrawing earlier is better than later: withdrawals are still open, but the exchange has warned that processing may slow as the deadline approaches, and the first fee deduction happens within three days of 29 November.

**3. Check your USDC balance first.** The monthly fee is deducted from USDC before other cryptocurrencies. If you hold USDC in the account, that is what the fee will eat first. Withdrawing everything — including small USDC balances — is the only way to be sure nothing is charged.

**4. Keep records of the withdrawal.** Save screenshots or transaction records showing the amounts withdrawn, the destination wallet or bank account, and the dates. If you are an Indian resident, those records are what you will need when you report the transactions for tax purposes — and the 30% tax and 1% TDS rules apply regardless of which exchange the funds came from.

**5. Do not move funds to another unregistered platform.** The point of withdrawing is to get your money somewhere safe and legal for you to use it. Moving from one exchange that is losing its licence to another that has no registration at all does not solve the problem. If you are in India, use an FIU-IND-registered exchange; if you are in Europe, check whether the platform you move to holds a MiCA authorisation.

## What to Watch Next

Four things matter from here. **Whether Binance wins authorisation elsewhere in the EU** is the first: the exchange says it is "actively progressing" its authorisation pathway, and if it secures a MiCA licence in another member state it could return to serving European customers — but that would be a new authorisation, not a reopening of the closed accounts. **What happens to unclaimed balances after 31 December** is the second: reporting suggests they may be treated as inactive and converted or transferred, but Binance has not published a detailed plan, so users should not rely on recovering funds easily after the deadline. **Whether other exchanges face the same deadline** is the third: Binance is the biggest name to be caught by MiCA so far, and the question now is whether other platforms without authorisation follow the same path. **India's own regulatory direction** is the fourth and most important for Indian readers: whether the government moves towards a comprehensive licensing regime of its own, and what that would mean for the exchanges Indian users rely on.
