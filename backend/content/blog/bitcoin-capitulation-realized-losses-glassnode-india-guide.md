Bitcoin capitulation is back in the headlines. In its Week On-Chain report published on 17 November 2026, titled "Capitulation Meets Support", on-chain analytics firm Glassnode said that Bitcoin's realized losses have climbed to levels previously recorded only near the major market lows of 2018, 2020, 2022 and early 2023. At the same time, buyers absorbed roughly 66,000 BTC in the $62,000 to $66,000 price band during the two heaviest days of selling. The report, covered on 17–18 November by CoinCodex, Crypto Briefing, Bitcoinist, Coinotag, GazeWire and others, is the clearest on-chain account yet of what the November sell-off actually did to holders — and it is worth understanding on its own terms, because the word "capitulation" gets used loosely, and the report itself is careful about what it does and does not claim.

## What Happened: The Facts at a Glance

The story, as reported on 17–18 November 2026 from Glassnode's own report, is:

- **Realized losses hit cycle-low levels.** Glassnode said realized losses — the losses investors actually lock in when they sell below their purchase price — have reached levels previously seen near the major lows of 2018, 2020, 2022 and early 2023. The reading is measured against the profits realized over the past year, so it compares the scale of loss-taking with the scale of profit-taking, not just raw dollar amounts.
- **The selling came mostly from recent buyers.** According to Glassnode, most of the selling came from investors who bought their Bitcoin within the past six months — the group analysts call short-term holders. Many in this group had taken profits during the September–October rally, then bought back in, and are now selling again as prices fall.
- **The fall itself is now two weeks old.** Bitcoin fell to about $62,000 on 15 November 2026, its lowest since July, before recovering to around $64,970 on 17 November. At Indian prices, that is roughly ₹58.1 lakh at the low and about ₹60.9 lakh at the recovery, at an exchange rate of about ₹93.75 to the dollar.
- **Buyers stepped in at $62,000–$66,000.** During the two heaviest days of selling, buyers accumulated approximately 66,000 BTC in that price band. Glassnode says the band holds roughly one-fifth of all the Bitcoin owned by short-term holders, and it was built up around the August low.
- **Leverage unwound at record pace.** Glassnode found that the long liquidations on 13 and 14 November were among the largest relative to open interest since 2023, and more than $2 billion in leveraged positions were liquidated over the weekend.
- **Demand is still thin.** Glassnode reported that US spot Bitcoin ETFs recorded about $2.2 billion in net outflows during November, across eight straight sessions of redemptions, and that the total stablecoin supply has contracted by 4.2% over the past 90 days. Miners have also been selling: MARA and Riot sold roughly 28,000 BTC, according to CoinCodex's account of the report.

None of this is a forecast. Glassnode's own conclusion, repeated by every outlet that covered the report, is that the data does not confirm a bottom. That caution is part of the story, not a footnote to it.

## What "Realized Losses" Actually Measure

To understand the report, it helps to start with the metric itself. "Realized losses" sounds technical, but the idea is simple. Every Bitcoin has a last price at which it moved on the blockchain — in practice, the price its current owner paid for it. When that coin is sold, the difference between the sale price and the price it last moved at is either a realized profit or a realized loss. Add up all the realized losses across the network over a period, and you get a measure of how much pain holders are actually locking in, rather than how much pain they are merely sitting on.

That distinction matters. A coin held at a loss has not been sold. Its owner may be worried, may be waiting, or may not even be watching the price. A realized loss, by contrast, is a decision: someone chose to sell below what they paid, accepting the loss rather than waiting for a recovery. When realized losses spike, it means a lot of people made that decision at the same time. Analysts call that capitulation — the point at which the discipline of holding breaks and selling at a loss feels better than waiting for more of it.

Glassnode's specific reading adds a comparison that makes the number meaningful. It measures realized losses against the profits realized over the past year. In a healthy market, profits dominate: most sellers are selling above what they paid. When losses climb to the level of a year's worth of profits, the balance has flipped — selling at a loss has become as large a force as selling at a profit. The readings that mark that flip have, in the past, appeared near the major market lows of 2018, 2020, 2022 and early 2023. That is the historical parallel the report draws, and it is the reason the finding is being reported so widely.

There is a second, related number in the same report. Glassnode said the share of Bitcoin's circulating supply held in profit fell from 60.45% to 55.3% in a week — its lowest level since August — which means close to half of all Bitcoin in circulation is now underwater, held at a loss relative to its last purchase price. The two numbers describe the same event from different angles: one counts the coins sitting on losses, the other counts the losses actually being locked in by selling.

## Who Is Selling: The Short-Term Holders

The report is specific about who is doing the selling, and that detail changes how the story should be read. Glassnode attributes the selling wave chiefly to short-term holders — wallets that bought their Bitcoin within the past six months. These are the holders who react fastest when the price turns against them, because their purchase prices are recent and their losses are fresh.

The twist in this cycle is that many of these holders had already been through the same experience once this year. They had taken losses earlier in 2026, recovered some of their positions during the September–October rally, and then bought back in — only to face a second round of losses in November. That pattern matters because it explains why the selling is so concentrated: it is not the whole market giving up, it is a specific group of recent buyers being squeezed for the second time in a few months.

Long-term holders, by contrast, barely feature in the report's account of the selling. Their coins were mostly bought at much lower prices, so even after a fall from around $126,000 in early October to about $62,000 in mid-November, many of them are still in profit. That does not mean they are buying — the report is clear that overall demand is weak — but it does mean the capitulation is a story about recent buyers, not about the entire holder base.

This is also why the report's numbers should not be read as "half of all Bitcoin investors are panicking". On-chain data counts coins, not people. One person can own many wallets, one wallet can belong to an exchange holding coins for thousands of customers, and a coin that last moved at a high price may belong to someone who bought it years ago at a far lower price and simply moved it between their own wallets. Glassnode's cohort estimates are careful about this, excluding exchange-held coins and using entity-level analysis, but the headline figures are still measures of coins and transactions, not a census of investors.

## The $62,000–$66,000 Zone: Where Buyers Stepped In

The second half of the report's title — "Meets Support" — refers to what happened while the selling was at its worst. During the two days of heaviest selling, buyers accumulated approximately 66,000 BTC in the $62,000 to $66,000 band. Glassnode says that price range now holds about one-fifth of all the Bitcoin owned by short-term holders, and that the cluster was built up around the August low, when Bitcoin last traded in this area.

In plain terms, a cost-basis cluster is a price range where a lot of coins were bought. When the price returns to that range, two things tend to happen. Some holders who bought there are back at break-even and may sell, and some buyers who missed the first opportunity step in. In this case, the second effect dominated during the heaviest selling: 66,000 BTC changed hands into the hands of buyers willing to purchase in that band. On the major exchanges, resting buy orders on Binance and Coinbase also exceeded sell orders by about 2.3 times within 5% of the market price, according to CoinCodex's account of the report.

Glassnode identifies $62,000 as the critical level to watch. Its report warns that a daily close below $62,000 could expose Bitcoin to a further decline towards the June low of about $58,500 — roughly ₹54.8 lakh — and then to the realized price of about $53,700 — roughly ₹50.3 lakh — which is the average price at which all circulating Bitcoin last moved. For a stronger recovery signal, the report says Bitcoin would need to reclaim $74,100 — roughly ₹69.5 lakh — the average purchase price of short-term holders. These are Glassnode's stated markers, not predictions from this site, and the report is explicit that none of them has been tested yet.

## The Leverage Side: Liquidations at Record Pace

Capitulation on the spot market did not happen in isolation. It arrived together with one of the largest leverage unwinds of the year. Glassnode found that the long liquidations on 13 and 14 November were among the largest relative to open interest since 2023, and more than $2 billion in leveraged positions were liquidated over the weekend as fears of prolonged interest-rate hikes dampened appetite for speculative assets.

The mechanics are worth spelling out, because they explain why the fall was so fast. A leveraged long is a bet that the price will rise, made with borrowed money. When the price falls far enough, the exchange closes the position automatically and sells the collateral — which pushes the price down further, triggering the next set of liquidations. That chain reaction is what turned a steady decline into a sharp one, and it is the same mechanism this site has explained in earlier posts about liquidation cascades. The difference this time is the scale relative to the size of the market: the liquidations were large not just in dollars, but in proportion to the open interest behind them, which is why Glassnode flags them as historically significant.

The ETF side of the story points the same way. US spot Bitcoin ETFs recorded about $2.2 billion in net outflows during November, across eight straight sessions of redemptions, and about $2.6 billion over the ten trading sessions ending 13 November. The ETF MVRV ratio — which compares the market value of the Bitcoin held by the funds with its estimated cost — fell from 1.28 to 1.06 in a week, meaning the funds' holdings are now only barely above their average purchase price. And the stablecoin supply, the dry powder that buyers use to enter the market, has contracted by 4.2% over 90 days. Put together, the report's picture is of a market where sellers are exhausted and buyers are scarce at the same time.

## What This Is Not: A Bottom Call

The most important part of the Glassnode report is the part that gets the least attention in the headlines. Several of the articles covering it carry titles about the bottom being near — but the report itself does not say that, and Glassnode explicitly warns that broader buying demand remains too weak to confirm a sustained recovery.

History explains why that caution is warranted. Similar realized-loss readings in 2018, 2020 and early 2023 did appear near what later proved to be major lows. But they also appeared during falls that continued for weeks or months afterwards, and a reading that marks a local low in one cycle can mark only a pause in another. The metric measures the intensity of selling, not the timing of a turn. A market can be capitulating and still fall further, because the buyers who absorbed 66,000 BTC in the $62,000–$66,000 band may not be there in the same size at $58,500, and because the demand-side numbers — ETF outflows, shrinking stablecoin supply, miner selling — have not yet turned.

There is also a simpler reason to be careful with the word "bottom". A bottom is only visible in hindsight. At the time, a capitulation reading and a genuine low look identical: heavy selling, extreme fear, and a price that has fallen a long way. The difference only becomes clear weeks later, when the price either holds the level or breaks through it. Glassnode's own framing reflects that: $62,000 is a line in the sand, not a floor, and the report treats a daily close below it as the event that would invalidate the support story.

For readers, the practical takeaway is narrower than the headlines suggest. Capitulation tells you that the market is under severe stress, that recent buyers are selling at a loss in large numbers, and that a specific price band absorbed a large amount of selling. It does not tell you what happens next, and anyone presenting it as a buy signal is adding a conclusion the data does not support.

## What It Means for Indian Users

For Indian readers, the report's findings land in a market that has had its own disruptions in the same fortnight. Coinbase India only partially resumed INR withdrawals on 17 November after a nine-day outage, Unocoin's services have been suspended since 9 November following a security breach, and ZebPay has warned of INR delays. Those are separate stories, but they share a practical lesson with the Glassnode report: in a stressed market, the ability to move money matters as much as the price. Before acting on any market view, it is worth checking that your own withdrawal route actually works — a point this site made in the Coinbase resumption guide.

The tax angle is also specific to India, and it cuts against a common assumption. In some countries, selling at a loss creates a tax benefit, because the loss can be set off against gains. In India it cannot: losses on crypto transfers cannot be set off against other income, and gains are taxed at a flat 30% with 1% TDS on covered transfers. Selling at a loss therefore locks in the loss without creating any offset, which is one more reason to distinguish carefully between an unrealised loss — the value of your holding on screen — and a realized one.

Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

There is also a measurement point worth making for Indian readers. The figures in the Glassnode report are in dollars, and the rupee has weakened against the dollar in recent weeks — the rate used in this guide is about ₹93.75 to the dollar. That means the same dollar fall looks smaller in rupees than it does in dollars: a Bitcoin bought at $74,100 cost about ₹69.5 lakh, and at $62,000 it is worth about ₹58.1 lakh, a fall of roughly 16% in rupee terms against a 16% fall in dollars at a fixed rate — but if the rupee moves, the two numbers diverge. When you see a dollar figure in a global report, convert it at the current rate before judging what it means for your own holding.

Finally, a word on how to read this kind of report. Glassnode's Week On-Chain is a research publication, not a news wire, and its value is in the measurement, not the headline. The useful questions to ask of any on-chain report are the ones this guide has tried to answer: what exactly is being measured, who is doing the selling, where did buyers step in, and what does the publisher itself say about the limits of the signal. If a summary of the report cannot answer those four questions, it is probably selling a prediction rather than explaining the data.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is Bitcoin capitulation in simple words?
