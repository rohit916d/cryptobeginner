# What Is a Bid-Ask Spread? The Hidden Cost in Every Trade

Imagine you walk into a market to buy a used phone. The shopkeeper will sell it to you for ₹10,000 — but if you try to sell the same phone back to him a minute later, he will only offer ₹9,500. That ₹500 gap is not a fee anyone charged you. It is simply the difference between the buying price and the selling price. In crypto, that gap has a name: the **bid-ask spread**. It exists in every single trade you make, whether you notice it or not, and for beginners it is often the most underestimated cost of trading.

This guide explains what the bid-ask spread is in plain language, why it exists, what makes it wide or narrow, and how it quietly affects your money — with Indian examples throughout.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains how trading costs work; it does not recommend any coin, exchange, or trading strategy.

## Bid, Ask, and Spread — The Three Words You Need

Every crypto market is really a meeting place for two groups of people: buyers and sellers. Each side states the price it is willing to accept.

- **Bid:** the highest price a buyer is currently willing to pay for a coin. If you want to sell right now, this is roughly the price you will get.
- **Ask (also called the offer):** the lowest price a seller is currently willing to accept. If you want to buy right now, this is roughly the price you will pay.
- **Spread:** the difference between the ask and the bid. It is the gap between what sellers want and what buyers will pay.

Here is a simple illustration. Suppose the order book for a coin on an Indian exchange looks like this:

| Side | Price | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Best bid | ₹100.00 | The most a buyer will pay right now |
| Best ask | ₹100.80 | The least a seller will accept right now |
| Spread | ₹0.80 | The gap — about 0.8% of the price |

Notice something important: there is no single "price" of the coin at any moment. There are two prices — one for buying and one for selling — and the number shown on charts is usually the price of the last trade, which sits somewhere between them. The moment you buy, you pay the ask. The moment you sell, you receive the bid. That round trip is where the spread bites.

## A Worked Example: What the Spread Actually Costs You

Let us walk through an illustrative example with round numbers. These are made-up figures to show the maths, not real market prices.

Suppose you buy a coin when the best bid is ₹500 and the best ask is ₹505. You buy at ₹505. A minute later, nothing about the coin has changed — no news, no price movement — and you decide to sell. The best bid is still ₹500, so you sell at ₹500. You have lost ₹5 per coin even though "the price" never moved.

Now scale that up. If you bought 20 coins at ₹505 (₹10,100) and sold them at ₹500 (₹10,000), you are ₹100 down before any trading fee is counted. That is the spread doing its work: buying high and selling low, by design, on every round trip.

Expressed as a percentage, the spread in this example is ₹5 ÷ ₹500, or about 1%. On a busy, liquid pair the spread might be 0.1% or less — barely noticeable. On a thinly traded coin it can be several percent, which means you start every trade several percent in the red. That is why the spread matters far more than most beginners realise: unlike a fee, it is not shown as a separate line item, so it is easy to ignore until it has quietly eaten into your returns over many trades.

## Why Does a Spread Exist at All?

The spread is not a trick invented by exchanges. It is the payment that market participants earn for providing liquidity — that is, for standing ready to buy or sell at a moment's notice.

Think of a small-town vegetable seller. She buys tomatoes in the morning not knowing whether customers will come, and she risks being left with stock that spoils. To make that risk worthwhile, she sells at a higher price than she paid. Crypto market makers work the same way: they constantly quote buy and sell prices, they take on the risk of holding coins whose price can swing sharply, and the spread is their compensation for that risk and effort.

When many market makers compete, they undercut each other, and the spread shrinks. When few are willing to quote prices — because a coin is obscure, the market is panicking, or trading is thin — the ones who remain demand a wider spread to cover their higher risk. The spread, in other words, is a live measure of how easy and safe it is to trade a coin at that moment.

## What Makes Spreads Wide or Narrow

Several factors decide how wide the spread is at any given time. Beginners who understand them can avoid trading in the worst conditions.

| Factor | Narrow spread | Wide spread |
|---|---|---|
| Trading activity | Many buyers and sellers, high volume | Few participants, low volume |
| Coin popularity | Large, well-known coins | Small or newly listed coins |
| Market calm | Steady prices, normal trading | Sharp volatility, breaking news |
| Exchange size | Large exchanges with deep order books | Small exchanges with thin order books |
| Time of day | Busy trading hours | Quiet hours with few orders |
| Pair type | Popular pairs like BTC/INR | Exotic pairs with few traders |

A few of these deserve a closer look for Indian readers.

**Popular INR pairs are usually cheapest.** On Indian exchanges, pairs like BTC/INR or ETH/INR typically have the tightest spreads, because that is where most Indian trading happens. A coin paired only against an obscure token, or listed on a small platform, can have a spread many times wider.

**Volatility widens spreads fast.** When prices are swinging sharply, market makers widen their quotes to protect themselves from being caught on the wrong side of a sudden move. Trading during a panic or a hype spike usually means paying a wider spread than trading on a calm day.

**New listings are spread traps.** A freshly listed coin often has very few buyers and sellers, so its spread can be enormous in the first days. Beginners drawn in by listing excitement frequently buy at a wide ask and discover they can only sell at a much lower bid.

## Spread vs Fee: Two Different Costs

Beginners often confuse the spread with the trading fee, but they are separate costs that stack on top of each other.

- **Trading fee:** a percentage the exchange charges for matching your order, usually shown clearly on the order screen (for example, 0.2% or 0.3% per trade).
- **Spread:** the gap between the buy and sell price, never shown as a line item, but paid on every trade nonetheless.
- **Total cost:** fee plus spread, on the buy side and again on the sell side.

Here is how they combine in an illustrative example. You buy ₹10,000 worth of a coin. The spread costs you roughly 0.5% (₹50) and the trading fee costs 0.25% (₹25), so your true entry cost is about ₹75. When you sell later, you pay a similar combination again. A trade that "cost 0.25%" on paper actually cost closer to 1.5% round trip. Over dozens of trades, that difference compounds — and it is one reason frequent trading is far more expensive than it looks.

Instant-buy screens deserve special attention here. Many Indian exchanges offer a simple one-tap buy button that hides the order book entirely. The price shown already includes a spread — sometimes a wider one than the exchange's advanced trading screen would give you. Convenience is being priced in. If you plan to trade more than occasionally, learning to read the order book on the advanced screen is worth the small effort.

## How the Spread Affects Beginners in Practice

The spread does not hit everyone equally. It hurts most in exactly the situations beginners find themselves in:

**Small trades feel it more.** A ₹500 trade and a ₹50,000 trade on the same pair face the same percentage spread, but beginners often trade small amounts on illiquid coins where spreads are widest — and the fixed withdrawal or network fees on top make the total cost even heavier in percentage terms.

**Market orders pay the full spread.** A market order says "buy immediately at whatever the best price is," which means you accept the ask as quoted. A limit order says "buy only at this price or better," which lets you set your own bid — but your order may not fill if the market never reaches it. Neither is free; the choice is between certainty of execution and control over price.

**Frequent trading multiplies the cost.** Every round trip pays the spread twice — once buying, once selling. Someone who trades ten times a month pays ten spreads; someone who buys and holds pays one. This is a purely mechanical reason why constant in-and-out trading tends to underperform, even before price moves are considered.

**"The price" you see is not the price you get.** Price alerts, charts, and portfolio apps usually show the last traded price, which sits between the bid and the ask. If you check your portfolio, see a coin at ₹505, and assume you can sell for ₹505, you may be disappointed to receive the ₹500 bid instead. Always check both sides of the order book before deciding a trade is profitable.

## Five Habits That Keep Spread Costs Down

You cannot remove the spread — it is part of how markets work. But you can avoid paying the worst of it.

1. **Check the order book first.** Before any trade, look at the best bid, the best ask, and the spread between them. If the spread looks wide compared with the coin's price, pause and ask whether the trade is worth it.
2. **Prefer liquid pairs.** Trading popular coins on popular INR pairs usually gives you the tightest spreads. The more exotic the pair, the more you should expect to pay.
3. **Use limit orders when you are not in a hurry.** Setting your own price gives you control, and your order may even earn a better fill than a market order. Accept that it might not fill at all — that is the trade-off.
4. **Avoid trading in storms.** During extreme volatility, breaking news, or the first hours of a new listing, spreads widen sharply. Waiting for calmer conditions usually costs nothing and saves on spread.
5. **Compare before you commit.** Spreads differ between exchanges. A quick look at the order book on two FIU-IND registered Indian exchanges can reveal a meaningful difference on the same coin — though always weigh the spread against fees, withdrawal costs, and the exchange's trustworthiness, not the spread alone.

One more habit matters: keep records. Every trade's buy price, sell price, fee, and date feed into your tax reporting. In India, gains from selling crypto are taxed at a flat 30%, with 1% TDS usually applying on sales through Indian exchanges. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. Good records turn tax season from a scramble into a routine.

## The Bottom Line

The bid-ask spread is not a scam, a fee, or a flaw. It is the basic cost of having someone ready to trade with you at a moment's notice. But because it never appears as a line item, beginners routinely underestimate it — and it is widest precisely where beginners are most tempted to trade: small coins, new listings, volatile moments, and one-tap instant-buy screens.

The fix is simple awareness. Before every trade, glance at the bid, the ask, and the gap between them. Add that gap to the trading fee in your head. If the total cost still makes sense for what you are trying to do, trade with your eyes open. If it does not, wait for a more liquid moment. In crypto, as in any market, the cheapest trade is often the one you choose not to rush.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the bid-ask spread in crypto?

The bid-ask spread is the gap between the highest price a buyer is willing to pay (the bid) and the lowest price a seller is willing to accept (the ask). If the best bid is ₹100 and the best ask is ₹101, the spread is ₹1. You buy at the ask and sell at the bid, so the spread acts as a built-in cost on every trade, in addition to any exchange fee.

### Why is the spread wider on some coins than others?

Spreads are narrow where trading is busy and competitive, because many buyers and sellers constantly update their orders. They widen on less-traded coins, during volatile moments, and on smaller exchanges, because fewer participants are willing to quote a price — and those who do demand a bigger cushion for the risk of holding the coin. Popular INR pairs on large Indian exchanges usually have tighter spreads than small or newly listed coins.

### Can beginners reduce the cost of the bid-ask spread?

Yes, partly. Checking the order book before trading, preferring liquid pairs, using limit orders instead of market orders, and avoiding trades during wild price swings all help keep spread costs down. You cannot eliminate the spread entirely — it exists in every market — but you can avoid the situations where it is widest, and you should always count it alongside the trading fee when judging the true cost of a trade.
