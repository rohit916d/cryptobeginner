If you are a Non-Resident Indian (NRI) living in Dubai, Singapore, London, Toronto or anywhere else outside India, crypto can feel confusing. You may have an Indian bank account, an Indian PAN card and family back home — but does that mean you can simply open an Indian crypto account and start trading? The honest answer is: it is complicated, and the rules that apply to you are not the same as the rules for a resident Indian. This guide explains **NRI crypto rules in India** in plain language: exchange accounts, tax and FEMA basics, and the mistakes NRIs most often make.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. Tax and foreign exchange rules are complex and change over time; this guide explains the basics only and is not a substitute for advice from a qualified tax or legal professional.

## First, Are You Actually an NRI for These Rules?

The word "NRI" is used loosely in everyday conversation, but for tax and foreign exchange purposes, what matters is your residential status in a particular financial year. In simple terms, a person who spends fewer than a set number of days in India during a year is generally treated as a non-resident for that year, while someone who spends most of the year in India is treated as a resident.

This matters for two reasons. First, your residential status can change from year to year — you might be an NRI one year and a resident the next if you move back. Second, different rules look at residence differently: income tax law and FEMA each have their own tests, and they do not always give the same answer. So the first step for any NRI thinking about crypto is not choosing a coin or an exchange — it is being clear about your own residential status for the year in question.

If you are unsure, a chartered accountant can confirm your status quickly from your travel dates. Getting this wrong can mean filing in the wrong category or missing a reporting requirement, so it is worth sorting out before you make any transaction.

## Can NRIs Open Accounts on Indian Crypto Exchanges?

This is the question NRIs ask most often, and the answer is frustratingly platform-specific. Indian crypto exchanges are required to follow anti-money-laundering rules, which means they must verify every customer's identity through KYC. In practice, most Indian exchanges have built their onboarding around resident Indian customers: an Indian PAN card, Aadhaar-based verification, an Indian mobile number for OTPs and an Indian bank account for deposits and withdrawals.

Many NRIs run into one or more of these problems:

- **PAN and KYC:** You may hold a PAN card from before you moved abroad, but the exchange may still ask for documents or a verification step designed for residents, such as a live video KYC from an Indian address.
- **Bank account:** Deposits and withdrawals usually have to come from a bank account in your own name. If your Indian account is an NRE or NRO account, the exchange may or may not accept it — policies differ, and they change.
- **Mobile and address:** Some platforms require an Indian mobile number or proof of an Indian address, which an NRI living abroad may not have.
- **Outright refusal:** Some exchanges simply do not onboard NRI customers at all, and say so in their terms of service.

The important point is that there is no single "NRI crypto account" product in India. You have to check each exchange's current eligibility terms yourself, and accept that the answer may be no. What you should never do is work around a refusal by opening an account in a parent's, sibling's or spouse's name. The crypto would legally belong to that person, their PAN would be linked to the tax reporting, and recovering the funds later — especially if relationships change or the account holder passes away — can become a painful legal mess.

## NRE vs NRO Accounts: Why the Account Type Matters

If you do find an exchange that accepts NRI customers, the type of Indian bank account you use still matters. NRIs typically hold two kinds of accounts in India:

| Account type | What it is used for | Why it matters for crypto |
| --- | --- | --- |
| NRE account | Money earned abroad, held in rupees | Funds are generally repatriable, meaning they can be moved back out of India under banking rules |
| NRO account | Income earned in India, such as rent or pension | Funds have more restrictions on moving them abroad |

Crypto purchases on Indian exchanges are usually funded in rupees from an Indian bank account. Which account type an exchange accepts, and what happens when you later withdraw rupees, depends on that exchange's banking arrangements and current policy. Do not assume that because you can deposit from an account, you can freely withdraw to it or repatriate the proceeds. Ask the exchange's support team, in writing, before you deposit anything: which account types are accepted, what withdrawal limits apply, and what documents are needed for withdrawals.

Also keep in mind that your bank itself may have its own view. Some Indian banks are cautious about transfers to and from crypto exchanges. A transfer being blocked or questioned by your bank is not unusual, and it is not a reason to route money through a third person's account or an informal channel — that only creates bigger compliance problems.

## Crypto Tax for NRIs: The 30% Rule and 1% TDS

India taxes gains from virtual digital assets (VDAs), which include cryptocurrencies, at a flat rate of 30%, plus applicable surcharge and cess. A 1% tax deducted at source (TDS) applies to transfers of VDAs above the threshold. Losses from crypto cannot be set off against other income or against other crypto gains, and they cannot be carried forward to future years.

Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

For NRIs, three extra complications apply:

1. **Where is the income taxed?** Whether a particular crypto gain is taxable in India depends on factors including your residential status and where the transaction is considered to take place. A transaction on an Indian exchange and a transaction on a foreign exchange may be treated differently, and the country you live in may also want to tax the same income.
2. **Double taxation:** India has tax treaties with many countries, but crypto is a new asset class and treaty treatment is not always clear-cut. You may need to check whether you can claim relief in your country of residence, and what paperwork that requires.
3. **Reporting:** Even if no tax is due in a particular year, you may still need to report crypto holdings or transactions in your Indian tax return, and possibly in your country of residence as well. Some countries require residents to disclose foreign financial accounts and foreign crypto holdings, and penalties for non-disclosure can be severe.

The practical lesson is simple: keep records of everything. For every transaction, note the date, the platform, the asset, the quantity, the price in rupees, any fees and the TDS deducted. Indian exchanges provide transaction statements, but foreign exchanges may not provide India-friendly reports, so download and save your own records regularly. Good records are what make a tax filing possible — and what protect you if a tax authority ever asks questions.

## FEMA Basics: Why Foreign Exchange Rules Matter for Crypto

FEMA, the Foreign Exchange Management Act, is the law that governs how money moves in and out of India. It applies to foreign exchange transactions, overseas investments and repatriation of funds. Crypto creates a puzzle for FEMA because it does not fit neatly into any existing category: it is not Indian currency, it is not a foreign currency, and it is not a security in the traditional sense.

There is no simple, dedicated FEMA rulebook for crypto, and that uncertainty is itself the risk. For an NRI, the questions FEMA raises include:

- **Can you freely move crypto-related funds across borders?** Moving rupees out of India is regulated, and converting crypto proceeds into foreign currency and sending them abroad is not automatically permitted in the same way as, say, repatriating NRE deposits.
- **Does the payment channel matter?** Using legitimate banking channels with a clear paper trail is very different from using informal transfer routes, peer-to-peer arrangements or third-party accounts to move money. Informal routes can breach FEMA and anti-money-laundering rules even if the underlying crypto transaction seems harmless.
- **Are foreign exchange platforms a problem?** Holding crypto on a foreign exchange is not, by itself, the same as breaking FEMA — but funding that account from India, or bringing proceeds back into India, can raise FEMA questions depending on how it is done.

None of this means NRIs cannot touch crypto at all. It means the cross-border part needs care. Before moving significant amounts, speak to a professional who understands both FEMA and crypto, and keep every bank statement, exchange statement and transaction receipt that shows the money trail.

## Common Mistakes NRIs Make With Crypto

**Using a relative's account.** As discussed above, this is the single most common and most damaging mistake. It mixes up ownership, tax reporting and recovery rights, and can look like an attempt to hide the real owner of the funds.

**Assuming resident rules apply.** Just because your cousin in Mumbai can do something on an exchange does not mean you can. Onboarding, tax and reporting rules may all differ for you.

**Ignoring the other country's rules.** Your country of residence has its own crypto tax and reporting rules. Filing correctly in India but ignoring your home country's requirements — or the reverse — leaves you exposed in one place or the other.

**Trusting "NRI crypto agents."** Scammers actively target NRIs with promises of special NRI crypto accounts, guaranteed returns or help moving money abroad through crypto. No legitimate exchange needs an agent to open your account, and anyone promising assured profits or a secret route around banking rules is running a scam. Report such approaches and do not share your documents, OTPs or wallet recovery phrases with anyone.

**Losing access while abroad.** If your Indian mobile number lapses, your exchange account can become very hard to recover. Keep your registered mobile number active, store backup codes safely, and make sure your recovery email is one you can still access from abroad.

## A Practical Checklist Before You Start

If you are an NRI considering crypto, work through this checklist in order:

1. Confirm your residential status for the current financial year.
2. Read the eligibility terms of any Indian exchange you are considering — check specifically whether NRI customers are accepted.
3. Ask the exchange, in writing, which bank account types it accepts and what withdrawal rules apply.
4. Check the tax and reporting rules in both India and your country of residence.
5. Set up your own record-keeping system before your first transaction, not after.
6. Start small, with a test deposit and withdrawal, so you discover any banking or KYC problem before significant money is involved.
7. Never share your account credentials, OTPs or recovery phrases with anyone, including family members "helping" you manage the account.

## The Bottom Line

NRI crypto rules in India are not a single clear permission or prohibition — they are a patchwork of exchange policies, tax rules and foreign exchange regulations that are still evolving. Indian exchanges may or may not accept you, the 30% tax and 1% TDS framework applies to Indian crypto gains with complications around residence and double taxation, and FEMA adds a layer of caution to any cross-border movement of funds.

That complexity is not a reason to panic, but it is a reason to be methodical: confirm your status, read the terms, use your own accounts and legitimate banking channels, keep thorough records, and get professional advice before large transactions. Crypto rewards patience and good record-keeping far more than speed — and for NRIs, that is doubly true, because the paperwork is what keeps you safe in two countries at once.
