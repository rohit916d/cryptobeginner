## South Korea Reopens ICOs After 9-Year Ban: India Guide

South Korea is preparing to end its nine-year ban on initial coin offerings. On 24 November 2026, the country's Financial Services Commission (FSC) unveiled draft legislation — the Digital Asset Basic Act, formally the Act on Innovation in the Digital Asset Industry and Protection of Users — that would allow companies to sell newly created tokens to the public inside South Korea for the first time since September 2017. The draft was reported the same day by Bitcoin.com, the Cryptonomist, Unlock Blockchain, Altcoin Daily and other outlets, drawing on the FSC's own release and Korean reporting from Chosun Biz, Businesskorea, Yonhap and Maeil Business Newspaper.

This guide explains what the draft bill actually says, why South Korea banned ICOs in the first place, the disclosure rules and penalties it proposes, the two other big changes bundled into the same bill — bank-led won stablecoins and a cap on exchange ownership — and what, if anything, it means for Indian crypto users. It is an explainer, not investment advice, and it makes no prediction about prices or markets.

## What Happened: The Draft Bill at a Glance

The facts, as reported on 24–27 November 2026 by Bitcoin.com, the Cryptonomist, Unlock Blockchain, Altcoin Daily, Cropty and the iPhoneWired analysis of the FSC release, are:

- **The FSC presented the draft Digital Asset Basic Act on 24 November 2026**, at a session of its Virtual Asset Committee, an advisory body set up under the existing Virtual Asset User Protection Act.
- **It would scrap the blanket ICO ban imposed in September 2017**, after a wave of fraudulent token sales and speculative losses. Domestic token sales would become legal again — but only under mandatory disclosure rules and with real penalties for misleading buyers.
- **Token issuers would have to file a disclosure document with the FSC before approaching buyers.** The filing would cover four areas: the project itself, how the funds raised will be spent, the total number of tokens to be issued, and the risks buyers face.
- **A waiting period would follow the filing** — roughly seven days, according to Chosun Biz's reading of the proposal — before the company may actually sell tokens to the public.
- **False statements would carry civil and criminal liability.** The FSC reviews the filing, can demand corrections, and accepted filings would be published through an integrated disclosure system.
- **The same bill would restrict the first issuance of won-pegged stablecoins to consortiums in which banks collectively hold a majority stake**, and would cap any single major shareholder's stake in a crypto exchange at 20%, with exceptions up to 34% subject to regulatory approval.
- **The bill still needs the National Assembly's approval.** It was expected to reach parliament as early as 25 November, sponsored under Representative Yoo Dong-su, who chairs the Political Affairs Committee. The FSC has said it will support the bill through the legislative process.
- **Some details are not yet fixed.** The binding numbers for stablecoin-issuer shareholding and the exact mechanics of the 34% exception are left to an enforcement decree that has not been written yet.

## Why South Korea Banned ICOs in 2017

To understand why this reversal matters, it helps to remember what the ban was responding to. In September 2017, at the height of the first ICO boom, South Korea banned domestic initial coin offerings outright. Regulators cited fraud, speculation and investor losses: anyone could publish a white paper, collect money from the public, and disappear — and many did. The ban was part of a wider crackdown that also covered margin trading and anonymous accounts, and it stayed in place for nine years.

The ban did not stop Koreans from buying tokens. It pushed the activity offshore. Korean founders set up entities in Singapore and Hong Kong, raised money abroad, and then brought their tokens back to list on domestic exchanges. That is exactly the pattern the FSC's own case file describes: in May 2025, a platform company set up a separate foundation in a third country, issued its token there, and circulated it through domestic exchanges — beyond the reach of Korean disclosure rules. The draft bill's answer is not to ban harder but to make the paper trail enforceable: if the sale happens under Korean rules, the issuer can be held to account for what it said.

The scale of the decline is striking. One data provider cited in the coverage tracked public token fundraising falling from about $849 million in the first quarter of 2025 to an estimated $40 million in the second quarter of 2026 — a contraction of roughly 95%. That figure is a reported estimate, not an official statistic, but it captures the direction the FSC is reacting to: the fundraising market has shrunk, and the activity that remains has moved outside Korean jurisdiction.

## The Disclosure Rules: What Issuers Would Have to Do

The heart of the bill is the disclosure regime. Under the proposal, the process for a domestic token sale would look like this:

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1. Prepare the filing** | The issuer writes a disclosure document — a white paper plus a short prospectus — covering the project, the use of proceeds, the offering size and the risks |
| **2. File with the FSC** | The document is submitted to the Financial Services Commission before any buyer is approached |
| **3. Regulator review** | The FSC reviews the filing and can demand corrections |
| **4. Public disclosure** | Accepted filings go public through an integrated disclosure system, so anyone can read them |
| **5. Waiting period** | Roughly seven days pass after filing before the issuer may approach buyers, per reports of the proposal |
| **6. Ongoing duties** | Exchanges carry a continuing duty to keep listing disclosures updated after a token lists, rather than treating the initial listing as the only moment that matters |

The teeth are in the liability. False statements in the disclosure would expose issuers to civil and criminal liability — the point being that a white paper would stop being marketing copy and become a legal document. That is the model securities regulation has used for a century: you may sell to the public, but what you say in the prospectus can be used against you.

It is worth being precise about what the bill does not do. It does not guarantee that any token is a good investment, it does not vet the quality of projects, and it does not protect buyers from losing money on a token whose price falls. Disclosure tells you what the issuer claims; it does not tell you whether the claims are true, and it does not stop a project from failing. The protection it offers is narrower and more specific: a paper trail that regulators and courts can use after the fact.

## The Other Two Pillars: Stablecoins and Exchange Ownership

The ICO change is only one of three things the draft does. The other two matter because they show the shape of the regulatory system Korea is building.

**Won-pegged stablecoins, bank-led.** The bill would initially restrict the issuance of Korean won-pegged stablecoins to consortiums in which banks collectively hold more than 50% of the ownership. In plain terms, the first won stablecoins would be issued by groups dominated by banks, not by crypto companies. The FSC's logic, as reported, is that stablecoins are close enough to money that their issuers should look like regulated financial institutions. The binding shareholding numbers are left to the enforcement decree, so the final shape could still change.

**A 20% cap on exchange ownership.** The bill would cap any single major shareholder's stake in a crypto exchange at 20%, with the limit extendable to 34% with FSC approval under specific conditions. The aim is to prevent a single large owner — a chaebol, a foreign investor, or a dominant shareholder — from controlling an exchange. Existing large stakes would presumably need to be adjusted over time, though the draft's transition arrangements were not spelled out in the coverage.

**One unified statute.** The bill would replace the current dual-law system — which separates anti-money-laundering duties from user-asset protections — with a single unified statute covering the industry, the market and users. That matters because the existing framework, the Virtual Asset User Protection Act of 2024, was built mainly around fraud prevention. The FSC's own diagnosis, quoted in the coverage, is blunt: a rulebook built around fraud prevention left the market with rules that pushed activity offshore instead of bringing it under supervision.

## Why Now: The Korean Context

The timing is not random. South Korea has spent November 2026 in the middle of a wider exchange-security crackdown. Gopax halted deposits and trading on 6 November after a hack threat, the FSC moved to let exchanges freeze withdrawals on mere suspicion of a hack, and Bithumb disclosed in late November that a breach of its internal systems had exposed far more customer data than first reported. The FSC cited the $6.3 billion Binance hack as proof that fast blocking is decisive. In that climate, a bill that pairs reopening ICOs with stricter disclosure, bank-led stablecoins and ownership caps reads as a package: more activity, but inside a tighter frame.

There is also a political calendar. The draft reaches the National Assembly as early as 25 November, and the FSC has said it intends to support the bill through the legislative process so it can become law only after what it describes as sufficient discussion. Separately, some outlets reported that South Korea also plans to delay its 22% tax on cryptocurrency gains by another two years, moving the planned implementation date to 2029 — that is a separate proposal, not part of this bill, and it was not in the FSC's main announcement, so treat it as reported rather than confirmed.

None of these changes take effect unless the National Assembly passes the bill. Exchanges and issuers are left watching the legislative calendar, and the earliest realistic outcome is a law some time in 2027, not this year.

## What It Means for Indians

For most Indian crypto users, this draft changes nothing directly. It is South Korean law, and it would govern token sales inside South Korea. An Indian resident buying crypto on an Indian exchange is governed by Indian law, not by a Korean bill.

But there are three groups of Indians who should pay attention, and one lesson that applies to everyone.

**1. Indians living or working in South Korea.** If you live in Korea, or you hold an account on a Korean exchange, the new rules would reach you once they become law. A domestic ICO sold under the new disclosure regime would be a regulated Korean offering — which does not make it safe, but does mean the issuer has filed a public document you can read. The practical habit is the same one this site has recommended for every jurisdiction: know which legal entity holds your account, keep your KYC current, and keep records of where your funds came from.

**2. Indians who follow ICOs and token sales.** India has no ICO framework of its own. Token sales to Indian residents sit in a grey zone, and Indian regulators have repeatedly warned about ICO-linked fraud. The Indian cases this site has covered — the Korvio scheme, the HashPe Ponzi, the AP CID Tenali fraud — all show the same pattern: tokens created at almost no cost, given artificial values, and sold with promises of daily returns. Korea's answer is disclosure plus liability. India's answer, so far, has been enforcement after the fact. If you are ever offered a token sale, the Korean checklist is a useful filter wherever you live: is there a public disclosure document, does it say what the money is for, and can anyone be held responsible for what it claims? If the answer to any of those is no, that is the warning sign.

**3. Anyone watching how India might respond.** India already taxes crypto gains at a flat 30% with a 4% cess and no set-off of losses, and sales attract 1% TDS. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. At the same time, India's own self-regulatory body, the Bharat Web3 Association, has asked the government in its Budget 2027 recommendations to reduce the 1% TDS, allow loss set-off and review the 30% flat tax. Korea is moving in the opposite direction from a ban — towards a regulated market. Whether India follows with a framework of its own is a question for lawmakers, but the Korean example is now on the table as a working model of what disclosure-based regulation looks like.

The lesson for everyone is simpler. A ban does not remove demand; it moves the activity somewhere the regulator cannot see it. Korea tried the ban for nine years, watched its founders raise money in Singapore and Hong Kong, and has now decided that a public paper trail with legal consequences is a better tool than prohibition. That is a policy judgement, not an investment signal — and it says nothing about whether any individual token is worth buying.

## How to Judge Any Token Sale: A Beginner's Checklist

Because token sales will now be in the news again, here is a practical checklist for evaluating one — as an educational exercise, not as advice to buy anything:

- **Read the disclosure document first.** Is there a public white paper or prospectus? Does it explain the project, the use of funds, the total token supply and the risks in plain language?
- **Check who is behind it.** Are the founders named, with verifiable backgrounds? Anonymous teams are a red flag, not a feature.
- **Look for the money trail.** Does the document say exactly how the funds raised will be spent, and is there any mechanism — escrow, milestones, vesting — that stops the team from walking away with everything?
- **Watch for return promises.** Any promise of fixed daily returns, guaranteed profits or "up to 3x" is a classic Ponzi marker. Legitimate disclosures describe risks; scams describe returns.
- **Check the exchange.** Is the token listed on a regulated exchange that has verified the disclosure, or on an unregulated platform with no checks? Korea's draft puts a continuing disclosure duty on exchanges for exactly this reason.
- **Remember the tax.** In India, gains on crypto are taxed at a flat 30% with no set-off of losses, and 1% TDS applies to transfers. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.
- **Never invest money you cannot afford to lose.** This is the one rule that does not depend on any country's law.

## Conclusion

South Korea's draft Digital Asset Basic Act is the most significant attempt yet to reopen token fundraising in the country since the 2017 ban — but it is a draft, not a law. It would let companies sell tokens domestically again, under a disclosure regime with a waiting period and civil and criminal liability for false statements, while putting banks at the centre of won stablecoins and capping exchange ownership at 20%. The National Assembly still has to pass it, and the enforcement decree still has to be written.

For Indian users, the direct impact is small and the lesson is large. Nine years of prohibition pushed Korean token sales offshore; Korea has now decided that a public paper trail is a better answer than a ban. India has no ICO framework of its own, and its investors have repeatedly been hurt by fake-token schemes. Whatever New Delhi decides, the Korean draft gives Indian readers a concrete example of what a disclosure-based system looks like — and a checklist for judging any token sale, in any country, on the strength of what its issuers are willing to put in writing.
