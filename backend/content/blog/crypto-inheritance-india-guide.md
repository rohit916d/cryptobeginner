# Crypto Inheritance in India: What Happens After You?

Crypto inheritance is the question most beginners avoid until it is too late: if something happened to you tomorrow, could your family actually reach the crypto you hold? For a bank account, the answer is usually a paperwork exercise. For crypto, the honest answer is often no — not because the law denies your family, but because access in crypto depends on keys and passwords, not just on ownership documents. This guide explains the difference in plain language, with the Indian context, so you can make a simple, safe plan while everything is calm.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This article is also not legal advice; inheritance planning depends on your personal facts, so consult a qualified lawyer for your own will and estate documents.

## Why Crypto Is Different From Bank Money

A bank account lives inside an institution. The bank knows who you are, holds your KYC records, and has a defined process for nominees and legal heirs. If the account holder dies, the family produces documents, the bank verifies them, and the balance is transferred. The money was never dependent on a secret only you knew.

Self-custody crypto removes that institution completely. A wallet is controlled by a private key, usually backed up as a recovery phrase of 12 or 24 words. Whoever holds that phrase controls the funds; whoever loses it loses access permanently. There is no branch to visit, no password reset, and no officer who can unlock the wallet on seeing a death certificate. The blockchain does not know you have died, and it cannot check your family's documents.

That single difference shapes everything about crypto inheritance. Your family needs two separate things: the legal right to inherit, and the practical ability to access. A will can help with the first. Only planning can provide the second.

## Where Your Crypto Sits Decides What Happens

### Crypto on an Indian exchange

If your holdings sit on an Indian exchange such as CoinDCX, WazirX, or ZebPay, the exchange controls the keys and your account is an entry in its database, linked to your PAN and KYC. In that case, your family at least has a door to knock on. Exchanges generally ask for documents such as a death certificate, proof of the claimant's identity and relationship, and — depending on the situation and the platform's policy — succession documents. The exact list and timeline vary, so families should read the specific exchange's terms and contact its official support channel only.

A nomination, where an exchange offers one, can make this smoother, but do not assume a nominee automatically becomes the owner. In Indian financial practice, a nominee is generally a trustee who receives assets on behalf of the legal heirs. Keep your nomination details updated, and tell your family which exchanges you use — an account nobody knows about may never be claimed at all.

### Crypto in your own wallet

If you moved crypto to your own hot or cold wallet, there is no company in the middle. Your hardware device, phone wallet, and recovery phrase are the whole system. If your family does not know the wallet exists, or cannot find the phrase, the funds simply stay on the blockchain, visible but unreachable, potentially forever. No court order can move them without the key.

| Where the crypto is | What family can do | What can block them |
|---|---|---|
| Indian exchange account | Approach the exchange with death and succession documents | Unknown account, missing documents, unclear heirship |
| Hardware (cold) wallet | Restore the wallet on a compatible device using the recovery phrase | Phrase lost, hidden, or never shared in any recoverable way |
| Phone or software wallet | Unlock with device access plus PIN or phrase | Locked phone, no PIN known, phrase not backed up |
| Wallet known only to you | Nothing practical, even with a valid will | No key, no access — ownership papers alone cannot move crypto |

## The India Legal Picture, in Simple Terms

India has no separate crypto inheritance law. Crypto is treated in tax law as a Virtual Digital Asset, and inheritance generally follows the same broad succession framework that applies to other property: a valid will, where one exists, and the applicable succession law for your religion and family situation where it does not. Who qualifies as a legal heir, and which documents — such as a succession certificate or probate — may be needed, depends on personal facts.

Two practical points matter for beginners. First, paperwork takes time, especially if there is no will, so a clear will that at least acknowledges your crypto holdings can reduce disputes and confusion. Second, legal documents and access secrets should be handled differently: a will may pass through the hands of lawyers, executors, witnesses, and registrars, so it is a poor place to store a recovery phrase in full. This article is general education only — for a will that covers digital assets properly, sit with a qualified Indian lawyer for an hour. It is money well spent.

## The Core Dilemma: Secrecy Versus Access

Every inheritance plan runs into the same tension. While you are alive, your recovery phrase must stay secret, because anyone who sees it can empty the wallet in minutes. After you are gone, that same secrecy can lock your family out completely. Sharing the phrase casually today solves the second problem by creating the first one.

The way out is not to choose one extreme, but to separate knowledge into layers. Your family does not need your phrase today. They need to know that crypto exists, roughly where it is held, and exactly where the sealed access instructions will be found when needed. The secret itself stays protected until that moment.

## A Simple 5-Step Inheritance Plan

**Step 1: Make an inventory — without secrets.** List each platform and wallet: exchange name, the email or phone used for the account, wallet type (for example, a hardware wallet kept at home), and the networks used. Do not write passwords or recovery phrases in this list. Values in INR can be noted approximately and dated, since prices move; the list is a map, not a vault.

**Step 2: Write a separate access letter.** In a short letter, explain where the recovery phrase backup is stored, where the hardware device and its PIN information can be found, and any steps your family should follow first — starting with "do not rush, and do not enter the phrase on any website." Store this letter with your will or with a trusted lawyer, not in your email or cloud notes.

**Step 3: Choose who needs to know.** Tell at least one trusted adult — a spouse, sibling, or parent — that the plan exists and where the inventory is kept. Consider whether a second person, such as your lawyer, should hold a sealed copy of the access letter, so no single person's memory is the only bridge to your assets.

**Step 4: Keep records with the plan.** Add your exchange statements and a simple transaction record file noting dates, amounts, and transaction IDs. Good records help your family prove what you held, and they matter for tax compliance as well. Profits from transfers of virtual digital assets are generally taxed at 30%, with 1% TDS on certain transactions. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

**Step 5: Review once a year.** Update the inventory when you open a new exchange account, buy a new hardware wallet, or change where backups are stored. An outdated plan that points to a wallet you emptied, or misses the one you actually use, can be worse than none.

## What Never to Do

Some shortcuts quietly destroy the security the plan is meant to protect:

- Never store a recovery phrase in a WhatsApp chat, email, photo, or cloud document — one hacked account exposes everything.
- Never write the full phrase inside the will itself, for the readership reasons explained above.
- Never tell many relatives the phrase "just in case" — every extra copy is an extra risk while you are alive.
- Never rely on a single hiding place with no backup; fire, water, or a house move can erase the only copy.
- Never trust an "inheritance agent" or website that offers to recover a family member's wallet for an upfront fee — this is a known scam pattern that targets grieving families.

## If You Are the Family Member Reading This

If you have lost someone and believe they held crypto, move slowly. First, secure the paperwork: death certificate, your identity and relationship proof, and any will. Look for the person's records — exchange apps, statements, emails from exchanges about logins or statements, and any sealed letter kept with important documents. Contact each Indian exchange only through its official website or app support, and expect a documents-based process rather than a quick release.

Above all, be scam-aware. Grieving families are targeted with fake support accounts promising instant recovery. No genuine exchange employee will ask for a recovery phrase, and no real recovery ever requires typing a phrase into a website. If you find a hardware wallet, keep it safe and untouched until you have trustworthy guidance.

## Conclusion

Crypto inheritance in India comes down to one sentence: your family's legal right means little without planned access, and planned access means little if it destroys your security today. You do not need a complicated structure to fix this — an inventory without secrets, a separate sealed access letter, one informed trusted person, tidy records, and a yearly review cover most beginner situations. Do it while it feels unnecessary. That is exactly when it is easiest to do well.
