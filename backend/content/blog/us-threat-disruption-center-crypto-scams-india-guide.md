The United States has launched a new **Threat Disruption Center** to choke off the money flowing to overseas scam networks — the criminal operations behind fake investment schemes, pig-butchering fraud, sextortion and other online cons that drain billions of dollars a year, much of it through cryptocurrency. The centre was announced on Wednesday 19 November 2026 by the Homeland Security Task Force's National Coordination Center, which is co-led by the US Departments of Homeland Security and Justice, and its main private-sector partner is blockchain intelligence firm TRM Labs. This guide explains what the centre actually is, how it is supposed to work, why the contract behind it has already drawn questions, and what — if anything — it means for crypto users in India.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a government enforcement initiative; it does not recommend buying, selling or holding any asset.

## What Happened: The Facts at a Glance

The story, as reported on 19 November 2026 by Cointelegraph, Bloomberg, CryptoBreaking, KuCoin and several other outlets, is:

- **A new federal unit called the Threat Disruption Center was launched on 19 November 2026.** It was announced by the Homeland Security Task Force's National Coordination Center, a body co-led by the US Departments of Homeland Security (DHS) and Justice (DOJ). It is described as an operational cell inside that coordination centre, not a new standalone agency.
- **Its target is the money, not just the scammers.** The centre exists to interrupt the financial pathways used by foreign criminal groups behind scams, cybercrime and sextortion. Instead of only investigating crimes after the money is gone, it is meant to act while a fraud operation is still collecting payments — finding related victim complaints, identifying the wallets and accounts receiving the money, and working with banks and crypto platforms to stop further transfers.
- **It connects a very large network.** The centre links more than 30 federal agencies and over 440 state and local partners with commercial banks and cryptocurrency platforms. The point of that scale is speed: a victim's report in one state can be matched with reports elsewhere, and the combined picture can be passed to a bank or exchange before the scammers move the funds on.
- **It was created under a March 2026 executive order.** According to the Homeland Security Task Force, the centre was set up under an executive order signed in March 2026 that directed federal agencies to work more closely with private-sector companies to combat online fraud and other cybercrime targeting Americans. The order is the legal basis for the public-private structure of the new unit.
- **TRM Labs is the main private-sector partner.** Blockchain intelligence firm TRM Labs was selected to provide and maintain Ardence, the investigative platform the centre will use to map scam networks and identify victims. TRM will also supply engineering and investigative staff to support the work. Bloomberg reported that the US government is paying TRM about $95 million under a one-year contract for this technical support, including an artificial-intelligence platform, and that more than 60 TRM employees are involved in the project.
- **The scale of the problem is enormous.** The FBI recorded more than $17.7 billion in online fraud losses reported by Americans in 2025, according to Cointelegraph's report of the announcement, and overseas scam networks are estimated to drain about $10 billion a year. A separate Bloomberg-cited figure puts total US cybercrime losses at nearly $21 billion in 2025, up about 25% on the year, with crypto-related losses accounting for more than half. The two totals measure slightly different things, but they point in the same direction: online fraud is now a multi-billion-dollar industry, and crypto is one of its main payment rails.
- **It follows an enforcement campaign already under way.** On 27 October 2026, the US Justice Department said its Scam Center Strike Force had restrained more than $1 billion in cryptocurrency and seized 700 fraudulent crypto investment websites. The same announcement detailed charges against six people allegedly involved in an NFT investment fraud operation based in Madagascar. The new centre adds shared technology and private-sector support to that existing campaign.

## How the Centre Is Supposed to Work

To understand why the centre was created, it helps to understand why scam investigations are usually slow — and why speed matters more than almost anything else when crypto is involved.

When someone is defrauded, the usual sequence is slow and fragmented. The victim reports to local police, who may not have crypto expertise. The report is filed, and if it reaches a federal agency at all, it arrives weeks later. Meanwhile the scammers are moving the money: from the victim's bank account to a crypto exchange, from one coin to another, across a bridge to a different blockchain, through a mixer or a decentralised exchange, and finally to cash. Each hop takes minutes. By the time an investigator has a complete picture, the trail is cold and the money is gone. That is the gap the Threat Disruption Center is designed to close.

The model, as described by TRM Labs and the agencies involved, works in four steps. First, victim reports are collected and screened — TRM's chief executive, Esteban Castaño, said the government aims to use artificial intelligence to screen victim reports nearly in real time, looking for patterns that connect separate complaints to the same criminal network. Second, the Ardence platform maps the network: the wallets, accounts, websites, phone numbers and communication channels that belong to it, and the victims it has already reached. Third, the centre identifies opportunities to intervene while the fraud is still running — warning victims who have not yet paid, asking banks and exchanges to block a payment, or freezing funds that are still sitting in an account. Fourth, investigators follow the mapped network to its operators, so that one report can lead to a whole operation rather than a single arrest.

The emphasis on intervention before the money moves is the genuinely new part. Traditional enforcement is reactive: it punishes after the fact. This centre is meant to be preventive in the narrow sense of stopping the next payment, not the first one. Castaño made the point bluntly in comments carried by the reporting: scammers typically do not stop after one successful payment, and they often spend months or years draining a victim's savings and retirement funds. Every payment the centre blocks is therefore not just one loss prevented — it is the start of a longer drain that never happens.

There is also a practical reason the centre works through banks and crypto platforms rather than through arrests alone. A scam network can replace an arrested operator in days, but it cannot easily replace a frozen bank account or a blocked exchange wallet. Cutting off the payment rail is, in the centre's logic, the fastest way to make a scam operation unprofitable — and an unprofitable scam operation is one that shuts down on its own.

## Why TRM Labs and the $95 Million Contract Matter

The choice of TRM Labs as the centre's main private-sector partner is the part of the story that has drawn the most attention — and the most questions.

TRM Labs is a blockchain intelligence company: it builds software that traces cryptocurrency transactions across blockchains, links wallets to known criminal activity, and helps investigators and exchanges identify illicit funds. Its Ardence platform, which the centre will use, is designed to visualise scam infrastructure — the web of wallets, accounts and websites that make up a criminal network — and to identify victims connected to it. TRM says Ardence will be the centre's primary investigative tool, supported by its own engineering and investigative staff.

The contract behind the partnership is substantial. Bloomberg reported that the US government is paying TRM about $95 million for a year of technical support, including the AI platform, and that more than 60 TRM employees are working on the project. That figure matters for two reasons. First, it shows how seriously the government is taking the problem: $95 million for one year of technical support is a large commitment for a single investigative unit. Second, it raises the question of how the contract was awarded — and that question already has a history.

According to the Bloomberg reporting carried by KuCoin, the contract was awarded non-competitively to TRM Labs in July 2026. Its competitor Chainalysis — the largest company in the same field — subsequently filed a lawsuit alleging irregularities in the award process. That lawsuit was dismissed in September 2026. The dismissal means the award stands, but the episode is worth knowing about for a simple reason: when a government picks one private company to run the technology behind a major enforcement effort, the choice of vendor matters, and the fact that the main rival challenged it in court and lost is part of the public record. None of this affects what the centre does day to day. But it is the kind of detail that separates a careful reading of the story from a press-release summary, and it is the reason this guide mentions it.

There is also a wider point about the role of private companies in crypto enforcement. TRM Labs and Chainalysis are the two firms whose software most exchanges and investigators use to decide whether a wallet is "clean" or "tainted". When one of them becomes the technology provider for a government disruption centre, its tools effectively become part of the enforcement infrastructure. That is not a criticism — it is how modern financial-crime enforcement works, with banks and analytics firms doing much of the tracing that governments cannot do alone. But it does mean that the centre's effectiveness depends heavily on a single company's platform, and that the quality of that platform's data will shape which wallets get frozen and which victims get warned.

## Why This Story Matters in India

The Threat Disruption Center is a US government unit. It has no jurisdiction in India, and Indian users cannot report to it directly. That is worth saying plainly, because a headline about a new anti-scam centre can easily be misread as a new place for Indian victims to complain. It is not. But the story still matters in India for three reasons: the scams it targets also target Indians, the model it uses is the one Indian agencies are already applying, and the reporting routes it highlights are the ones Indian users should be using already.

Start with the scams themselves. The fraud networks the centre is built to disrupt — fake investment platforms, pig-butchering romance-investment cons, fake trading apps, sextortion — are the same networks that have been defrauding Indians all year. This site has covered the Indian side of that story repeatedly in the last few weeks alone. The Enforcement Directorate's Chennai investigation, covered here on 6 November, involved more than 600 complaints and over ₹400 crore, with mule bank accounts and crypto used to move the money. The Andhra Pradesh CID case, covered on 5 November, involved a Tenali victim, 27 transactions in Bitcoin, Solana and Dogecoin worth about ₹15 crore, and a court order directing the conversion of seized crypto into USDT. The Korvio Coin case, covered on 1 November, involved an alleged ₹500 crore Ponzi-style fraud with more than 2.48 lakh users. These are Indian cases, investigated by Indian agencies, against networks that operate across borders — exactly the kind of operation a disruption centre is designed to break up.

The second reason is the model. The centre's approach — connecting investigators, banks and crypto platforms so that suspicious payments can be blocked quickly — is the same approach Indian agencies have been building. The Enforcement Directorate's searches at CoinDCX and Delta Exchange, covered here on 7 November, were about exactly this: whether rupee deposits were converted into USDT and routed through foreign exchanges. The crypto-cards scrutiny reported on 2 November was about the same question from the payments side. And the banking problems that hit Coinbase, Unocoin and ZebPay users in India this month — withdrawals halted or delayed because of issues at a banking partner — are a reminder, however uncomfortable, that the link between crypto platforms and the traditional banking system is where enforcement actually happens. When that link works well, illicit payments can be stopped. When it breaks, ordinary users cannot get their money out. Both sides of that coin are visible in India right now.

The third reason is the most practical: the centre's launch is a reminder that reporting quickly is the single most useful thing a victim can do. The whole design of the unit rests on one assumption — that a victim's report, if it reaches investigators fast enough, can still lead to frozen funds. That assumption is just as true in India as in the United States. An Indian user who has been scammed should report to the National Cybercrime Reporting Portal at cybercrime.gov.in or call the 1930 helpline, and should also contact the exchange or bank involved immediately, with every record of the transaction: wallet addresses, transaction hashes, screenshots of the conversation, the website or app used, and the bank account or UPI details. The faster that information reaches someone who can act on it, the better the chance that the money is still where it can be frozen.

One more India-specific point is worth making. The centre's launch comes in a week when Bitcoin has fallen hard — trading near $62,700 on 19 November, down about 15% in a week, with the Fear and Greed Index at 10, in extreme fear, after the Federal Reserve's October minutes pointed to further rate hikes. Falling markets are prime time for scams: fraudsters promise guaranteed returns precisely when legitimate investments are losing money, and desperate investors are more likely to believe them. The coincidence of a market slump and a new anti-scam centre is a useful reminder that the two stories are connected — and that the most dangerous moment to trust a stranger's investment promise is the moment your own portfolio is down.

## What the Centre Cannot Do

A new government unit with a dramatic name invites over-claiming, so it is worth being precise about the limits.

First, it cannot guarantee recovery. Freezing works only if the money is still sitting somewhere a bank or an exchange can act on. Once stolen crypto has been swapped across chains, passed through a mixer or a decentralised exchange, and cashed out through an unregulated route, there is usually nothing left to freeze. The centre improves the odds by acting faster, but it does not change the basic arithmetic of crypto theft: the longer the delay, the lower the chance of recovery, and the chance is never high.

Second, it has no authority in India. It cannot freeze an Indian bank account, compel an Indian exchange, or investigate a crime committed in India. Its writ runs to US agencies, US banks and the platforms that choose to cooperate with it. An Indian victim's remedy remains the Indian system — the cybercrime portal, the 1930 helpline, the local police, and the exchange's own support and compliance teams.

Third, it is not a refund scheme. There is no application form, no compensation fund and no promise that victims will be made whole. The centre's job is disruption — stopping the next payment, mapping the network, warning the next victim. That is valuable, but it is not the same as getting your money back, and anyone who tells you otherwise is either mistaken or running a scam of their own.

Fourth, it does not change the rules for ordinary crypto users. It does not impose new requirements on Indian exchanges, change the 30% tax on crypto gains or the 1% TDS on covered transfers, or alter the FIU-IND registration requirement for platforms operating in India. Losses from a scam cannot be set off against other income under the current rules, which makes prevention — not recovery — the only reliable protection for an Indian user. ## What Indian Users Should Do If They Are Targeted

The centre's launch is a good moment to repeat the practical checklist, because the advice has not changed — only the urgency has.

- **Report immediately, in two places at once.** File a complaint on the National Cybercrime Reporting Portal at cybercrime.gov.in or call 1930, and contact your exchange or bank at the same time. Do not wait to see whether the scammer replies, and do not wait for the "withdrawal" they promised. Every hour reduces the chance that the funds can still be frozen.
- **Keep every record.** Save the wallet addresses, transaction hashes, screenshots of chats, the website or app link, and the bank account or UPI details involved. Investigators need the trail, and a screenshot you delete today is evidence you cannot recover tomorrow.
- **Never pay a "recovery fee".** A common follow-up scam targets people who have already been defrauded: someone contacts you claiming to be from an enforcement agency, an exchange, or a recovery firm, and asks for a fee to release your frozen funds. Real agencies and real exchanges do not work this way. The Threat Disruption Center does not contact victims to offer refunds, and neither does any Indian agency.
- **Never share a seed phrase or private key.** No legitimate support team, investigator or government official will ever ask for your recovery phrase, your private key, or a screenshot of either. Anyone who asks is a scammer, whatever uniform or logo they claim.
- **Treat guaranteed returns as a warning sign.** Any scheme that promises fixed daily profits, guaranteed doubling, or risk-free returns from crypto trading is a fraud. The centre exists because these promises work on real people — and the people they work on are usually the ones who can least afford the loss.
- **Check before you trust a platform.** Before sending money to any investment platform, check whether it is a real, registered business, whether Indian users have reported problems with it, and whether its promised returns are plausible. A quick search of the platform's name alongside the words "scam", "complaint" or "review" takes two minutes and has saved many people far more.
