You can now walk into a shop in India, scan the same UPI QR code you would use for Google Pay or PhonePe, and pay with cryptocurrency. The shopkeeper still receives rupees, the payment still takes under two minutes, and — according to a report in The Economic Times on 10 November 2026 — there is nothing the payments regulator can formally do to stop it. This guide explains how the crypto-to-UPI QR route works, why it sits in a legal grey zone, and what Indian users should know before trying it.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a reported payment method; it does not recommend any app or service, and it does not predict any price movement.

## What Happened: Crypto Goes Shopping in Rupees

The Economic Times published its report on the morning of 10 November 2026 under the headline "Grey zone in Indian payment laws: Crypto goes shopping in rupee using QR-linked loophole". The report's finding is simple and striking: web applications such as goat.cash, together with conversion merchants acting as intermediaries, are converting the crypto in a buyer's wallet into rupees to pay sellers of any goods and services — in less than two minutes.

The report gives a concrete example. A digital activist quoted by ET bought a cup of latte and petrol on a Monday morning by scanning the vendors' QR codes. "Once a buyer shares the India location, the UPI option crops up," the activist told the paper. The same story was picked up the same day by news aggregators, and the mechanism it describes is corroborated by other services in the market: CryptoSe, a non-custodial crypto-to-UPI platform built by Dadu Fintech, advertises exactly the same flow — "Scan any UPI QR. Pay with crypto. Merchant gets INR." — and says it is registered with FIU-IND as a Virtual Digital Asset Service Provider.

The story matters because it is the first time a mainstream Indian newspaper has documented crypto being spent at ordinary shops through India's own payment rails, rather than on an exchange or through an offshore card. The crypto-cards route — offshore prepaid cards that let Indians spend crypto — was already under scrutiny: ET reported on 2 November 2026 that many such cards issued to resident Indians had been deactivated after the Reserve Bank of India and the Financial Intelligence Unit got involved. The QR route, the new report argues, is harder to stop than the cards were.

## How Paying With Crypto Through a UPI QR Code Works

The flow described by ET has four steps, and each one is worth understanding separately because the legal questions attach to different steps.

**Step 1: Scan the vendor's QR code.** The buyer opens the goat.cash application and scans the same QR code the shop displays for UPI payments. No special merchant onboarding is needed on the vendor's side — the vendor's existing QR code is enough, because the vendor never touches crypto at all.

**Step 2: Enter the details and load stablecoins.** The buyer types in the purchase amount, an email ID or mobile number, and the country of location. Then the buyer loads USD Coins — USDC, a stablecoin pegged to the US dollar — into a designated wallet created for the transaction. The buyer can move USDC from a regular wallet into that designated wallet.

**Step 3: The intermediary converts the crypto.** Almost instantaneously, the application scans a list of merchant intermediaries and picks the one offering the best rate for USDC. The USDC equivalent of the purchase amount is debited from the designated wallet and goes to the intermediary acting as a liquidity provider. The difference between the rate at which the intermediary sells USDC in the market and the rate quoted for the conversion is the margin it earns — a margin it probably splits with the application provider, according to ET.

**Step 4: The vendor is paid in rupees.** The intermediary pays the vendor in rupees from its own INR bank account to settle the transaction, and then sells the USDC to recover its cost and fees. From the vendor's point of view, nothing unusual has happened: a normal UPI payment has arrived in rupees.

The whole chain is what makes the route different from simply "paying with crypto". At no point does the shopkeeper accept, hold or price anything in crypto. The crypto leg and the rupee leg are separate transactions, joined together by the intermediary in the middle.

## Why It Is a Grey Zone, Not Clearly Legal or Illegal

The ET report's central point is that this arrangement does not fit neatly into any existing rulebook. Here is the legal picture as the report lays it out.

First, crypto is legal to hold in India, but it is not legal tender. No shop is obliged to accept it, and no one can be forced to accept it as payment. That much is settled. But the QR route never asks a shop to accept crypto — it asks the shop to accept rupees, which is what every shop already does.

Second, the Supreme Court's 2020 decision matters. A three-judge bench of the apex court set aside the Reserve Bank of India's April 2018 circular, which had banned banks and regulated financial entities from providing services to crypto-related businesses. That decision reopened banking access for the crypto industry. When ET contacted the National Payments Corporation of India — the RBI-regulated entity that developed UPI — an official said that, as per the directions of the Supreme Court, it is not possible to formally stop such transactions.

Third, the report's own legal summary is blunt: "Technically, no law is broken as vendors are eventually paid in rupees (and not in cryptos)." But the same paragraph adds the qualification that gives the story its "grey zone" framing: "This is not banned, but it sits uneasily with the law. Crypto is legal to hold, though not legal tender. Whoever converts on a user's behalf, in the course of business, must register with FIU-IND. Else, it may be running an unauthorised payment system under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act."

The transaction, in other words, has two legs, and different rules apply to each. The first leg — converting a user's virtual digital assets into INR — may be undertaken by a VDA service provider registered with FIU-IND. The second leg — the UPI-based settlement to the merchant — should be handled by an RBI-authorised entity, such as a PPI issuer or a Payment Aggregator. The open question is whether the rules kick in when the two legs are combined into a single smooth experience that feels no different from UPI or Google Pay. That combination is exactly what these apps sell, and no regulator has yet given a clear answer on it.

An independent explainer on crypto-to-UPI legality reaches a similar conclusion from the other direction: there is no basis in the cited official material for calling every crypto-to-UPI payment in India categorically illegal — but that does not mean every exchange or bank supports it, or that a particular route is approved. FIU-IND registration, it notes, is not a government endorsement of a service. And NPCI's often-quoted April 2022 statement — that it was "not aware of any crypto exchange using UPI" — was a statement about what NPCI knew on that date, not a blanket ban.

## How This Connects to the Crypto-Cards Crackdown

The QR route did not appear out of nowhere. It is, in ET's own words, "akin to crypto cards backed by Visa and Mastercard, and issued by international service providers" — the offshore prepaid crypto cards that let Indians spend crypto at any merchant that accepted the card network.

Those cards were the subject of ET's 2 November 2026 report, which said the RBI and FIU were examining them and that several users' access had already been blocked. The cards worked on the same principle as the QR route: crypto in the user's wallet, converted by an intermediary, with the merchant paid in rupees. The difference is the last mile. A card is issued by an identifiable provider, and a regulator can pressure the card network or the issuer to switch it off. A QR code, by contrast, belongs to the merchant, and the payment that reaches the merchant is an ordinary UPI transfer from an Indian bank account. There is no card to deactivate and no foreign issuer to lean on — which is why ET's report frames the QR route as a loophole that is structurally harder to close than the cards were.

For Indian users, the connection matters because the two routes share the same regulatory logic. If the RBI and FIU concluded that offshore crypto cards needed scrutiny, the same scrutiny is likely to reach QR-based conversion services — especially the intermediaries doing the conversion as a business without FIU-IND registration. The question is not whether the route works today; it is whether the intermediary doing your conversion is registered and compliant, because that is where enforcement would land first.

## What It Costs: The Stablecoin Premium and the Margin

There is a second cost that has nothing to do with the law, and it is the one most likely to surprise a first-time user: the price of the stablecoins themselves.

Stablecoins like USDC and USDT typically trade at a premium in India — they cost more in rupees than their dollar peg would suggest. Our own guide to the USDT premium in India documented readings of around 6% above the reference rate in early November 2026, and ET's report notes the same premium as a routine feature of the Indian market. That premium is a cost you pay on top of the conversion margin: you buy USDC at a premium, the intermediary takes a margin on the conversion, and the vendor is paid the full rupee amount. The two costs together can make a small purchase noticeably more expensive than paying in rupees directly.

That is worth remembering before treating the QR route as a cheaper or cleverer way to pay. For a one-off experiment with a small amount, the cost is trivial. For regular spending, the premium plus the margin adds up — and unlike a card, there is no cashback or reward to offset it. The route's appeal is convenience and novelty, not price.

## The Tax Angle: Spending Crypto Is a Taxable Transfer

This is the part most likely to catch Indian users out, because it is easy to think of a QR payment as "just spending" rather than "selling". Under Indian tax law, it is a transfer of a virtual digital asset — and transfers are taxed.

Gains on transferring crypto are taxed at a flat 30% plus a 4% cess, which works out to 31.2%, with no set-off of losses against other income. Sales attract 1% TDS under Section 194S. Those rules apply whether you sell crypto on an exchange, swap one token for another, or spend it through a QR-payment app: the moment your crypto leaves your wallet in exchange for goods or services, a taxable transfer has taken place. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

In practice, that means every QR payment creates a tax record you should keep: the date, the crypto you spent, and the rupee value of the purchase. A ₹500 coffee paid with USDC is a small transfer, but it is still a transfer, and a year of small payments is a long list of transfers. If you use the route, keep the app's receipts and a simple spreadsheet of dates and rupee values — that is what a tax filing needs, and reconstructing it months later from wallet history is far harder.

There is also a record-keeping point on the intermediary's side. FIU-IND-registered providers are required to maintain transaction records and report suspicious activity under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act. A registered service will ask for KYC details and will apply 1% TDS at source — CryptoSe, for example, advertises exactly that. An unregistered service that asks for nothing and keeps no records is not doing you a favour; it is the service most likely to disappear or draw enforcement attention.

## The Safety Checklist Before You Try It

If you are curious about the route, treat the first try as an experiment, not as a new way to pay for everything. This checklist covers the basics.

**1. Check whether the service is FIU-IND registered.** Anyone converting crypto on your behalf in the course of business must register with FIU-IND as a reporting entity. A legitimate service will state its registration number — CryptoSe, for instance, publishes its temporary VDASP registration number on its site. If an app gives no registration details at all, do not use it.

**2. Start with a trivial amount.** Try the route once with the smallest purchase you can — a coffee, not a month's groceries. Confirm the full flow works, the vendor is paid, and you receive a receipt, before you trust it with anything larger.

**3. Know the real cost before you pay.** Ask what rate you are getting for your USDC, and compare it with the market rate. Remember the stablecoin premium and the intermediary's margin are both built into the price. If the app does not show you the rate before you confirm, that is a reason to stop.

**4. Never share your seed phrase or private keys.** No genuine payment app needs your seed phrase. If any app, bot or support agent asks for it, that is a scam — walk away and report it.

**5. Keep every receipt and record the rupee value.** Save the app's confirmation for every payment, and note the date and rupee amount. You will need these for your tax filing, and they are also your only proof if a payment goes wrong.

**6. Do not use it to move large sums.** The route is designed for small retail payments. Large or repeated conversions through an intermediary you have not verified are exactly the pattern that draws attention from banks, the FIU and the Enforcement Directorate — and the ED's recent searches at Indian exchanges show that attention is active right now.

**7. Report fraud through official channels.** If you lose money through a QR-payment app, report it on the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal at cybercrime.gov.in or call the 1930 helpline. Keep screenshots, transaction details and the app's name. Reporting does not guarantee recovery, but it is the route Indian authorities use to track these schemes.

## What to Watch Next

Three things will show whether the QR route becomes a lasting feature of Indian payments or a short-lived loophole.

The first is what the regulators say next. The RBI and FIU have already acted against offshore crypto cards, and the ED has searched Indian exchanges over cross-border crypto flows in the past fortnight. A statement from the RBI, FIU-IND or NPCI specifically addressing QR-based crypto conversion — or an enforcement action against an unregistered intermediary — would be the clearest signal of where this is heading. Until then, the grey zone stays grey.

The second is whether the big, registered Indian exchanges enter this space. If a FIU-IND-registered exchange or a regulated payment company launches its own crypto-to-UPI product, with KYC, TDS and receipts built in, the route stops being a loophole and becomes a product. Watch for that announcement: it would change the risk calculation for every user at once.

The third is the stablecoin premium. The QR route only makes sense while users hold stablecoins they are willing to spend. If the premium on USDT and USDC in India narrows — or if the rupee's own digital payment options keep improving — the economic case for paying through crypto weakens. The premium is the market's own verdict on how hard it is to move between crypto and rupees in India, and it is worth watching as closely as any regulator's statement.

## The Bottom Line

The Economic Times reported on 10 November 2026 that apps like goat.cash let Indians pay any shop by scanning its UPI QR code, converting crypto — usually USDC — into rupees through an intermediary, and settling with the vendor in under two minutes. The vendor is paid in rupees, not crypto; the NPCI has said it cannot formally stop such transactions under Supreme Court directions; and the report's own legal reading is that no law is technically broken — but anyone converting crypto for users as a business must register with FIU-IND, and the payment leg should run through an RBI-authorised entity.

For Indian users, the honest summary is this: the route works, the law around it is unsettled, the costs are real — a stablecoin premium plus a conversion margin — and spending crypto this way is a taxable transfer at 30% plus 1% TDS. Try it with a small amount if you are curious, use only a service that shows its FIU-IND registration, keep every receipt, and do not mistake a clever workaround for a settled, regulated way to pay. The grey zone may not stay grey for long.
