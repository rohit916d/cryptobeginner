## ED Attaches ₹12.64 Crore Hotel Over Crypto, Hawala Money

India's Enforcement Directorate has provisionally attached a hotel in Karaikal, Puducherry, alleging it was built with money earned from gold smuggling, hawala dealings and illegal cryptocurrency transfers carried out in France. The five-storey Hotel Le Royale Palace, valued by the ED at about ₹12.64 crore for the land and building together, is the latest property to be frozen in a month in which the ED has moved repeatedly against crypto-linked money laundering — from the ₹400 crore Chennai cyber-fraud arrests to the ₹112.91 crore HashPe Ponzi case and the FEMA searches at CoinDCX and Delta Exchange.

The attachment was announced on 26 November 2026 by the ED's Chennai Zonal Office-II under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA), 2002, and was reported the same day by The Times of India, the IANS news agency, Times Now, Mirror Now, CryptoTimes and several other outlets, all working from the ED's own press release. This guide explains what the ED alleges, how the money is said to have moved from France to a hotel in Puducherry, what a provisional attachment actually means, and what — if anything — Indian crypto users should take from the case.

## What the ED Did

The ED's Chennai Zonal Office-II issued a provisional attachment order for Hotel Le Royale Palace in Karaikal, Puducherry — a five-storey hotel, together with the land it stands on. The agency valued the property at approximately ₹12.64 crore, a figure the IANS report describes as the ED's combined estimate for the land and the building. The order was made under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002, the law the ED uses to freeze and eventually confiscate property it says represents the proceeds of crime.

The attachment is directed against Mougamadou Nizamoudine and his three sons — Mohamed Mubeen Nizamoudine, Shahine Shafique Mougamadou Nizamoudine and Rashine Nizamoudine. According to the ED, the investigation began on the basis of credible information received from the Government of the French Republic about alleged smuggling, hawala and other offences committed abroad by Nizamoudine and his sons.

The ED had already searched three premises in Karaikal, Puducherry and Chennai on 28 October 2026, seizing digital devices and incriminating documents. The attachment of the hotel, about a month later, is the next step in the same investigation. The agency has said its investigation is continuing, and no arrests, charges or convictions have been reported in connection with this case.

One figure in the coverage needs a careful note. The Times of India reported the hotel as being worth ₹364 crore, in both its headline and its body text. The IANS report — carried by Punjab Kesari, Lokmat Times, Yes Punjab, AsiaPost, The Saveratimes and BhaskarLive — and the reports from DT Next, Times Now, Mirror Now and CryptoTimes all put the ED's valuation at ₹12.64 crore, with IANS explicitly stating that this is the ED's estimate of the combined value of the land and building. A few international aggregators repeated the higher figure, converting it into roughly $39–43 million. This guide uses ₹12.64 crore, because it is the figure in the ED press release as reported by the IANS syndication and the majority of outlets — but the discrepancy itself is a useful reminder: when two numbers circulate for the same property, the number to trust is the one in the agency's own release, not the one in the loudest headline.

## What the Family Is Alleged to Have Done

The ED's allegation, as reported, is a classic three-part laundering story: earn illicit money abroad, move it to India through informal channels, and then park it in a visible, respectable asset.

**The alleged offences in France, 2019–2023.** The ED says Nizamoudine and his sons carried out gold smuggling, hawala transactions and illegal cryptocurrency transfers in France between 2019 and 2023, operating on a commission basis — in other words, they are alleged to have been paid a fee for moving other people's money, rather than trading on their own account. The investigation was triggered by information from the French government, which suggests the French authorities had already been watching the network before the ED got involved.

**The alleged movement of money to India.** According to the ED, the proceeds were transferred to India from 2019 onwards through two routes: foreign-currency cash deposits into a Non-Resident External (NRE) account, and wire transfers from the United Arab Emirates routed through agents. The cash-deposit route and the UAE wire route are both familiar features of hawala-style networks: money changes hands abroad, and the equivalent value appears in India through a different channel, with no single bank transfer linking the two ends.

**The alleged integration: a hotel in Karaikal.** The ED says the funds were then used to acquire land and construct Hotel Le Royale Palace in Karaikal. In money-laundering terms, this is the "integration" stage — the point at which dirty money is turned into a clean-looking, income-generating asset. A hotel is a particularly useful vehicle for that purpose: it is a large, visible, legitimate business, it generates cash income of its own, and its construction costs can absorb large sums without attracting the attention that a sudden bank balance would.

Two important limits apply to everything above. First, these are allegations, not findings. The ED has attached the property and is still investigating; no court has decided that the family committed any offence. Second, the ED's press release does not name the cryptocurrencies involved and does not give a separate value for the crypto transfers. The crypto element is one of three alleged channels — alongside gold smuggling and hawala — and the reporting does not break down how much of the money moved through each.

## How Hawala, Crypto and Gold Fit Together

For a beginner, the combination of gold, hawala and crypto in one case can look confusing. In practice, the three are different tools for the same job: moving value across borders without leaving the paper trail that a normal bank transfer creates.

**Hawala** is an informal value-transfer system that predates modern banking by centuries. A person hands money to a hawala operator in one country; a trusted counterpart pays out the equivalent amount in another country. No money physically crosses the border, and no single bank record connects the sender to the receiver. It is not illegal in itself to use informal transfer networks in every jurisdiction, but in India, moving money this way to evade foreign-exchange controls is an offence under the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA) and, where the money is the proceeds of crime, under the PMLA.

**Gold smuggling** works on the same principle in physical form: gold is bought cheaply in one market, carried across a border without declaration, and sold at a profit in another. The profit, and the value itself, can then be fed into the same informal networks.

**Cryptocurrency** is the newest tool in the same toolkit. A crypto transfer can move large value across borders in minutes, settled on a public blockchain but with wallet addresses that do not, on their own, identify their owner. That is why crypto keeps appearing in ED cases — not because crypto is illegal, but because it is fast, borderless and, when mixed with other channels, hard to trace end to end. The same features that make crypto useful for a legitimate user sending money to family abroad make it useful for someone trying to hide the origin of funds. The difference is the intent and the source of the money, not the technology.

The Karaikal case is a textbook illustration of the pattern: the alleged network did not rely on any single method. Gold, hawala and crypto were used together, with cash deposits into an NRE account and wires from the UAE as the final legs into India. That layering — several methods, several countries, several years — is exactly what makes these cases slow to investigate and hard to prove, and it is why the ED's case rests on French government information and a month of searches before the attachment.

## What a Provisional Attachment Actually Means

A provisional attachment under the PMLA sounds final, but it is not. It is a temporary freeze, and understanding the difference matters for anyone reading enforcement news.

When the ED provisionally attaches a property, the owner cannot sell it, transfer it, mortgage it or otherwise deal with it while the case is pending. The attachment does not transfer ownership to the government, and it does not mean the property will be confiscated. The ED must place the attachment before an adjudicating authority, which examines whether there is reason to believe the property represents proceeds of crime. The people named in the case can contest the attachment, present their own evidence and argue that the property was bought with legitimate funds.

If the adjudicating authority confirms the attachment, it stays in force while the investigation and any trial proceed. Only after a conviction — or, in some circumstances, after a final determination that the property is proceeds of crime — can the property be finally confiscated. That process can take years. In the meantime, the hotel in Karaikal remains where it is, frozen in place, while the investigation continues.

This is why the language in enforcement reporting matters. "Attached" does not mean "seized and sold". "Alleged" does not mean "proven". And "proceeds of crime" is the ED's characterisation of the money, not a court's finding. Readers who treat an attachment as a conviction will misunderstand every ED case they read — including this one.

## The Pattern: ED Crypto Enforcement in November 2026

The Karaikal attachment is not an isolated case. November 2026 has been the busiest month yet for ED action touching crypto, and the Karaikal case is the fifth distinct enforcement story this site has covered in the month. Seeing the pattern helps put this case in context — and shows why the ED is paying so much attention to crypto's role in moving illicit money.

On 6 November, the ED's Chennai office arrested two men in a ₹400 crore cyber-fraud case built on mule bank accounts and USDT laundering, with more than 600 complaints and ₹300 crore-plus in transactions across 31 accounts. On 1 November, the ED's Korvio Coin case alleged a ₹500 crore Ponzi-MLM fraud with 2.48 lakh users and $219 million in transactions. On 11 November, the ED's HashPe case alleged a ₹112.91 crore Ponzi built on fictitious tokens and 1%-a-day return promises, with ₹14.16 crore of property attached. And on 4–5 November, the ED searched three Bengaluru premises linked to CoinDCX and Delta Exchange under FEMA, alleging that INR deposits were converted to USDT through a Costa Rica-based paper entity and routed to offshore exchanges for trading in tokenised foreign stocks.

The Karaikal case differs from each of those in a specific way. It is not an exchange case, like CoinDCX and Delta. It is not a Ponzi or a fraud against retail investors, like Korvio or HashPe. And it is not a domestic cyber-fraud ring, like the Chennai ₹400 crore case. It is a cross-border laundering case in which crypto is one channel among three, the alleged offences happened in France, the money arrived through an NRE account and UAE wires, and the end asset is a hotel. That is a different fact pattern from every other ED crypto case this month — which is why it gets its own explainer rather than a mention inside one of the others.

What the cases share is the ED's focus: the agency is treating crypto not as a curiosity but as a routine part of how illicit money moves, and it is using the PMLA's attachment powers aggressively against the assets at the end of the chain — hotels, properties, bank balances — rather than only against the people at the start of it.

## What This Means for Indian Crypto Users

The honest answer is that this case changes nothing about the rules for ordinary Indian crypto users — and that is worth saying clearly, because an ED headline about crypto can easily be misread as a crackdown on crypto itself.

**Crypto is not illegal in India.** This case does not change that. The ED is alleging specific crimes — gold smuggling, hawala, illegal crypto transfers — by specific people, in France, between 2019 and 2023. Using crypto to move the proceeds of crime is a crime; using crypto to buy, hold or trade, within the rules, is not.

**Your taxes are unchanged.** Crypto income in India is still taxed at a flat 30% (plus applicable surcharge and 4% cess) under Section 115BBH, with 1% TDS under Section 194S on transfers above the specified thresholds, and losses cannot be set off against other income. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

**The practical lessons are about hygiene, not fear.** The Karaikal case is a reminder of three things every Indian crypto user should already be doing. First, keep your crypto activity on registered, FIU-IND-compliant Indian exchanges and keep records of where your money came from — if your funds ever need explaining, a clean paper trail is your best defence. Second, be wary of anyone offering to move money across borders for a commission, especially through informal channels or "agents" — that is the exact pattern the ED alleges here, and it is how ordinary people get pulled into laundering networks without realising it. Third, treat any scheme that promises to convert crypto into property, gold or cash at a suspicious discount as a red flag, not an opportunity.

**If you are a victim of crypto fraud, report it.** The National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal at cybercrime.gov.in and the helpline 1930 are the right first steps for reporting fraud, phishing or unauthorised transfers. Reporting early matters: in the ED cases this month, the complaints of victims — more than 600 in the Chennai case alone — were often what started the investigation.

## What Happens Next

Three things are worth watching from here. The first is the adjudication: the ED must place the provisional attachment before the adjudicating authority, which will decide whether to confirm it, and the family will have the opportunity to contest it. That process will also test whether the ₹12.64 crore valuation holds up, or whether the ₹364 crore figure reported by The Times of India has any basis in the ED's own records.

The second is the investigation itself. The ED has said it is continuing, and the French connection means it may depend on further information from the French authorities — including, potentially, details of which cryptocurrencies were used and how much moved through crypto versus gold and hawala. The press release, as reported, does not name the coins or break down the amounts, so any future ED update that fills in those gaps would be genuinely new information.

The third is the broader pattern. With five distinct ED crypto-enforcement stories in November alone, the agency's attention is clearly not a one-off. The question for Indian crypto users is not whether the ED will keep acting — it plainly will — but whether the next cases follow the same pattern of attaching the assets at the end of the chain. For now, the Karaikal hotel joins a growing list of properties frozen in that pattern, and the case is a reminder that in money-laundering investigations, the building at the end of the money trail is often where the story becomes visible.
