# What Are ICOs and IDOs? Crypto Token Sales Explained

Every few months, a new crypto project announces that it is selling its tokens early — before the token is listed on any exchange. The promise sounds simple: get in before everyone else, while the price is low. The reality is far messier. Token sales have produced some legitimate projects, but they have also produced some of the biggest scams in crypto history. This guide explains what ICOs and IDOs are, how they work, why they are risky, and how a beginner in India should think about them.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Token sales are high-risk and unregulated in most cases — never put in money you cannot afford to lose entirely.

## What Is an ICO?

ICO stands for **Initial Coin Offering**. It is the crypto version of an IPO (Initial Public Offering), the process by which a company sells shares to the public for the first time. In an ICO, a crypto project creates a new token and sells it directly to buyers, usually to raise money to build the project.

The typical ICO works like this:

1. **The project publishes a whitepaper** — a document explaining what the project does, how the token works, and how the money will be spent.
2. **A sale window opens** — the team announces a start date, a token price, and how many tokens are for sale.
3. **Buyers send crypto to the project** — usually Bitcoin or Ether, sometimes a stablecoin, to a wallet address the team publishes.
4. **Tokens are distributed later** — buyers receive their tokens in a wallet, either immediately or after the project launches.
5. **The token (hopefully) gets listed** — if the project succeeds, the token may be listed on an exchange where it can be bought and sold.

ICOs became famous in 2017, when hundreds of projects raised billions of dollars this way. Many of those projects never delivered anything. Because an ICO is run entirely by the project team, there is usually no independent check on whether the team is real, whether the code exists, or whether the money will be used as promised.

## What Is an IDO?

IDO stands for **Initial DEX Offering**. It is a newer version of the same idea, but the sale happens on a **decentralised exchange (DEX)** or a launchpad platform instead of the project's own website.

In an IDO, the process is handled by a smart contract — a piece of code on a blockchain — rather than by the team's own wallet:

1. **The project partners with a launchpad** — a platform that hosts token sales, such as a DEX or a dedicated IDO platform.
2. **Buyers connect a wallet and commit funds** — usually a stablecoin or the platform's own token.
3. **The smart contract distributes tokens automatically** — buyers receive tokens according to the sale rules, and the remaining tokens go into a liquidity pool.
4. **Trading opens immediately** — because the token is listed on a DEX straight away, buyers can usually trade it within minutes of the sale ending.

IDOs are generally considered more transparent than ICOs because the sale rules are written in code that anyone can inspect, and the token has a market price immediately. But transparency about the sale process is not the same as safety — the project itself can still fail, and the token's price can still fall sharply.

## ICO vs IDO: The Key Differences

| Feature | ICO | IDO |
|---|---|---|
| **Where the sale happens** | Project's own website | Decentralised exchange or launchpad |
| **Who handles the money** | The project team directly | A smart contract |
| **When you can trade** | Only after an exchange lists the token | Usually immediately on a DEX |
| **Transparency** | Low — you must trust the team | Higher — sale rules are on-chain |
| **Entry requirements** | Usually just a wallet and crypto | Often requires holding the launchpad's token |
| **Risk level** | Very high | Very high |

Both formats share the same core risk: you are paying for a token in a project that has not yet proven itself. A better sale mechanism does not make a bad project good.

## Why Do Projects Run Token Sales?

From the project's side, a token sale is a way to raise money without going through a bank, a venture-capital firm, or a stock exchange. The team sells a share of its token supply to the public and uses the funds to hire developers, pay for marketing, and build the product.

From the buyer's side, the appeal is early access. If a project becomes popular, early buyers hope the token will be worth more later than the price they paid. This is the same logic as buying shares in a startup — except that a startup has audited accounts, a registered company, and legal obligations to investors. Most crypto projects have none of these.

## The Risks Every Beginner Must Understand

Token sales are among the riskiest things in crypto. Before you even think about one, understand these risks:

**1. Most new tokens lose value.** A large share of tokens sold in ICOs and IDOs fall in price after launch. Early excitement fades, insiders sell, and buyers are left holding tokens that nobody wants. There is no guarantee that a token will ever be worth what you paid for it — or anything at all.

**2. Scams are everywhere.** Fake token sales are a classic crypto scam. A team may copy a real project's website, announce a "presale" on Telegram or WhatsApp, collect money, and disappear. Rug pulls — where the team removes the liquidity from a DEX pool — are common after IDOs. If a sale asks you to send crypto to a personal wallet address shared in a chat group, it is almost certainly a scam.

**3. The team may not deliver.** Even honest teams fail. Building a blockchain product is hard, and most startups — crypto or otherwise — do not survive. A token sale raises money first and builds later, which means your money is funding a promise, not a product.

**4. Lock-ups and vesting.** Many sales do not give you all your tokens at once. Instead, tokens are released gradually over months (a vesting schedule). You may watch the price fall while most of your tokens are still locked and you cannot sell them.

**5. No refunds, no insurance.** If the project fails or the team disappears, there is no refund. Crypto has no equivalent of deposit insurance, and there is no regulator you can complain to for most overseas token sales.

**6. Fake urgency and hype.** Token sales often use countdown timers, "limited allocation" claims, and influencer promotions to pressure people into buying quickly. Genuine projects do not need to rush you. Pressure to decide in minutes is a warning sign, not a selling point.

## ICOs, IDOs and the Law in India

India does not have a dedicated law for ICOs or IDOs. That does not mean they are free of rules — it means they sit in a grey area, and buyers have very little protection.

Here is what an Indian beginner should know:

- **No SEBI registration.** Token sales are not regulated like share offerings. SEBI has warned the public about unregistered crypto investment schemes, and a token sale has none of the investor protections that a stock-market listing carries.
- **Tax still applies.** Gains from selling tokens are taxed as income from Virtual Digital Assets — a flat 30% tax, with no set-off of losses — and a 1% TDS applies on transfers above the threshold. This applies whether you bought the tokens on an Indian exchange or in an overseas token sale. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.
- **FIU-IND registration matters.** Exchanges serving Indian users must register with FIU-IND, India's financial intelligence unit. A token sale hosted on an unregistered offshore platform offers you no such oversight.
- **Sending money abroad has rules.** Buying tokens in an overseas sale usually means sending crypto or foreign currency out of India. The Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA) governs such transfers, and getting this wrong can create legal trouble. If you are unsure, speak to a qualified professional before sending anything.
- **Recovery is almost impossible.** If an overseas token sale turns out to be a scam, Indian authorities have very limited ability to help you recover funds. You can report fraud at cybercrime.gov.in or by calling 1930, but cross-border crypto recovery is rare.

The practical takeaway: the absence of a ban is not the same as approval. An unregulated sale is a sale where you carry all the risk.

## The 5-Minute Check Before Any Token Sale

If you are considering a token sale — and most beginners should not — run this check first. If any answer is "no" or "I don't know", walk away.

1. **Can you find the real team?** Real names, real photos, real LinkedIn or GitHub profiles, and a history of work in the field. Anonymous teams are a major red flag.
2. **Is there a working product?** A demo, a testnet, or at least public code on GitHub. A whitepaper alone is not a product.
3. **Is the contract address official?** Get it from the project's official website or verified social account — never from a Telegram message, a WhatsApp forward, or a comment under a YouTube video. Fake contract addresses are a common scam.
4. **What is the token supply and distribution?** How many tokens exist, how many are being sold, and how many does the team keep? If the team and insiders hold most of the supply, they can dump it on buyers after the sale.
5. **Is there a vesting schedule?** Check when team and early-investor tokens unlock. A big unlock shortly after the sale often means heavy selling pressure.

This check takes five minutes and filters out most bad sales. It does not guarantee a good one — it only removes the obviously dangerous ones.

## Red Flags That Should Stop You Immediately

- The sale is announced only in a Telegram or WhatsApp group, with no official website.
- You are asked to send crypto to a personal wallet address, not a smart contract.
- The project promises guaranteed returns, fixed profits, or a "sure 10x".
- A celebrity or influencer is promoting it without disclosing that they are paid.
- The whitepaper is copied, vague, or full of buzzwords with no technical detail.
- The team is anonymous or uses stock photos.
- The sale has a countdown timer pressuring you to buy "before the price rises".
- The project claims to be "approved by SEBI" or "licensed by RBI" — neither regulator licenses crypto token sales.

## What Beginners Should Do Instead

If you are new to crypto, token sales are the wrong place to start. The safest way to learn is also the simplest:

- **Learn first, buy later.** Understand Bitcoin, Ethereum, wallets, and exchanges before touching anything exotic.
- **Use registered Indian exchanges.** Buy established assets through FIU-IND registered platforms where you have customer support, INR deposits via UPI, and a clear tax trail.
- **Start small and stay diversified in learning.** If you eventually decide to explore new projects, treat any money in a token sale as money you are prepared to lose completely.
- **Keep records.** Every purchase, sale, and transfer matters for your tax filing. Record the date, amount, and platform for every transaction.

Token sales are not a shortcut to wealth. They are a high-risk fundraising tool for early-stage projects, and the early buyers — not the team — carry almost all of the risk.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between an ICO and an IDO?

An ICO (Initial Coin Offering) is a token sale run directly by a project team, usually on its own website — you send crypto to the team's wallet and receive tokens later. An IDO (Initial DEX Offering) is a token sale run on a decentralised exchange or a launchpad platform, where the sale is handled by a smart contract and the token can usually be traded immediately after the sale. IDOs are more transparent than old-style ICOs, but they carry the same fundamental risks: the project may fail, the token price may fall, and scams are common.

### Are ICOs and IDOs legal in India?

India has no dedicated law that legalises or bans ICOs and IDOs specifically. Crypto assets are taxed as Virtual Digital Assets — a flat 30% tax on gains and 1% TDS on transfers above a threshold — but buying tokens in an overseas token sale does not give you any legal protection, and many such sales have no registration with SEBI or any Indian regulator. Any gains are taxable in India regardless of where the tokens were bought, and you should speak to a qualified tax professional before participating in a token sale.

### Can you lose all your money in an ICO or IDO?

Yes. Tokens bought in an early sale can lose most or all of their value if the project fails, the team disappears, the token is never listed on an exchange, or the sale turns out to be a scam. Unlike a bank deposit, there is no insurance, no refund mechanism, and no regulator to complain to in most cases. This is why beginners are advised to learn how token sales work and to treat any money put into one as money they can afford to lose entirely.

## The Bottom Line

ICOs and IDOs are how new crypto projects raise money by selling tokens before they are listed. IDOs are more transparent than the old ICO model, but the underlying risk is the same: you are paying for a promise, not a proven product. Most new tokens lose value, scams are common, and in India there is no regulator to protect you if things go wrong.

For a beginner, the right move is almost always the same: learn the basics, use registered Indian exchanges, keep careful records, and stay away from early token sales until you can read a project — its team, its code, its token distribution, and its vesting schedule — well enough to judge it for yourself. In crypto, patience is not just a virtue. It is the cheapest form of protection you have.
