ZachXBT $133M EU crypto theft is the newest major security story in a week already dominated by crypto crime — and it is also one of the strangest. An independent on-chain investigator says more than $133 million in cryptocurrency was withdrawn from exchange accounts belonging to an unidentified European entity, that the money was rapidly converted and moved toward Monero, and that the victim may be a large Western European financial institution that has not disclosed what happened. No victim has confirmed any of this. No regulator has confirmed it either. What is confirmed is narrower, but still serious: a respected investigator published wallet addresses and a trail of transactions, a blockchain analytics firm traced more than $108 million of it, and at least one major exchange, Kraken, has restricted accounts linked to the activity.

This guide explains what happened step by step, separates what is known from what is only suspected, and draws out the lessons for Indian users. It makes no prediction about any coin's price and recommends no coin, exchange, or service.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a reported security incident; it does not recommend any coin, exchange, or service, and it treats unconfirmed claims as unconfirmed.

## What Happened, in One Paragraph

According to blockchain investigator ZachXBT, an unknown actor withdrew large amounts of cryptocurrency from accounts on centralised exchanges starting on 1 November 2026, converted much of it from stablecoins into Ethereum, and then moved it through instant exchange services and cross-chain bridges toward Monero, a privacy-focused cryptocurrency that is far harder to trace. ZachXBT first put the suspected total at more than $106 million, then raised it to more than $133 million on 3 November after identifying another $27 million-plus in withdrawals from centralised exchanges. Blockchain analytics firm Bitquery independently traced $108.8 million in stablecoin withdrawals from accounts at Kraken, Crypto.com, and OKX. Kraken said it had restricted accounts linked to the suspected theft and was working with law enforcement. The identity of the victim, and how the attacker gained access, remain unconfirmed.

## Who Is ZachXBT, and Why Do People Listen?

For beginners, the name needs an introduction, because this whole story rests on one investigator's work. ZachXBT is an independent on-chain investigator who has built a large following by tracing stolen cryptocurrency through public blockchain records and publishing wallet addresses, transaction trails, and timelines. He is not a company, not a regulator, and not a law-enforcement agency. His findings are not official findings. But his track record is the reason major news outlets report his alerts: he has repeatedly published detailed, checkable transaction evidence before victims or exchanges have said anything publicly, and other analysts have often been able to verify his trails from the same public records.

That distinction matters for how to read this story. When ZachXBT publishes addresses and amounts, other analysts can check the transactions themselves, and in this case Bitquery did exactly that, tracing a large part of the same flow. When ZachXBT offers a theory about who the victim is, however, nobody else can check it from the blockchain alone, because blockchain records show addresses and amounts, not the real-world identity of the person or institution behind an account. Keep those two categories separate throughout this guide: the transaction trail is partly verifiable, the victim theory is not.

## The Timeline: From $106 Million to $133 Million

The story unfolded over about two days, and the rising total is itself part of the story — it grew because more withdrawals kept being found, including some that happened after the first public alert.

**1 November 2026: the withdrawals begin.** According to Bitquery's tracing, $108.8 million in USDC stablecoins was withdrawn from exchange accounts beginning at 12:25 UTC on 1 November, with accounts at Kraken supplying about 95% of the traced total and the rest from Crypto.com and OKX. ZachXBT's original alert described funds leaving multiple exchange accounts over about 24 hours as USDC and USDT stablecoins. The attacker then swapped the stablecoins for Ethereum, converted roughly $62.98 million of stablecoins into 27,553 ETH within about four hours through UniswapX, a swap system on Ethereum, and began moving funds onward.

**2 November 2026: the first alert, and more money moves.** ZachXBT published his first alert putting the suspected theft at more than $106 million and listing 11 wallet addresses associated with it. He warned that a portion of the withdrawals he later found had occurred after his initial disclosure — in other words, the activity did not stop when it became public. Bitquery's snapshot at 13:47 UTC on 2 November found about $78.3 million still sitting in wallets linked to the suspected attacker, including about $68.9 million in ETH, $5.6 million in DAI, and $3.7 million in USDC, while about $30.5 million had already moved out of the tracked addresses. Only the USDC balance could potentially be frozen by its issuer, Circle; ETH cannot be frozen by an issuer, and DAI has no freeze function. Tether, the issuer of USDT, had already frozen $1.1 million, according to the same reporting.

**3 November 2026: the estimate rises, and exchanges respond.** On 3 November, ZachXBT published a follow-up identifying another $27 million-plus in centralised-exchange withdrawals, taking his estimate above $133 million, and listing additional wallet addresses. He also set out his working theory about the victim, discussed below. On the same day, Kraken said it had restricted accounts linked to the suspected theft, stressed that the incident did not originate on the Kraken platform, and said it was actively investigating reports that some assets were subsequently moved through accounts on its platform and was working with law enforcement to support recovery. Cointelegraph's updated reporting on 3 November also noted responses from OKX and Crypto.com. Multiple outlets — including Unchained, Cointelegraph, CoinEdition, and Gadgets 360 — carried the updated figure on 3 November, which is why this story qualifies as a genuine multi-source breakout rather than a single stray report.

## The Victim Theory: What ZachXBT Claims, and What He Does Not Prove

Here is the part of the story that needs the most care. In his 3 November follow-up, ZachXBT wrote that his working theory was that a large financial institution in Western Europe was exploited and was actively being covered up. He did not name the institution. He did not publish evidence for a cover-up. And every major report of the story has carried the same caveat: the victim's identity and the cover-up claim remain unverified.

That does not mean the theory is baseless — investigators form working theories from patterns, such as the size and behaviour of the accounts involved — but a working theory is not a finding. There are several possible explanations for why no victim has come forward, and beginners should hold all of them in mind. One is that the victim is indeed an institution that has chosen not to disclose, whether for legal, reputational, or investigative reasons. Another is that the victim is a different kind of entity — a trading firm, a fund, or a high-net-worth individual — whose accounts simply look institutional in size. A third is that disclosure is delayed while exchanges and law enforcement investigate, which is common after large incidents. Until a victim, a regulator, or a law-enforcement agency confirms a loss, the honest description is "suspected theft from an unidentified European entity," not "a European bank was hacked for $133 million."

ZachXBT also argued that the lack of a proper incident disclosure allowed more than $30 million to be laundered that might otherwise have been frozen by European law enforcement. That is his argument, and it raises a real policy question about how quickly victims should disclose — but it, too, rests on the unproven premise that a victim knew, understood, and withheld the incident. It should be read as criticism from an investigator, not as an established fact about any institution's conduct.

## Why This Is Not the Binance Hack, and Why That Distinction Matters

Because this incident arrived days after the Binance theft, it is easy to merge the two in your head. Do not. The Binance incident involved around $6.3 billion drained from Binance's hot wallets on 29 October 2026, and the FBI attributed it to the North Korea-linked TraderTraitor group on 31 October. ZachXBT has said the opposite about this European case: he is highly confident the Lazarus Group was not behind it, because the laundering patterns look different. In the Binance case, investigators described rapid, automated conversions into Ethereum and movement through decentralised finance services. In this case, the trail runs through centralised-exchange accounts, instant exchange services such as Kyrrex and WellWallet, and conversion into Monero through a service called Wagyu, followed by movement across blockchains through bridges. Different routes, different fingerprints, different conclusions.

The distinction matters for Indian readers for a practical reason. If you read every large theft as "North Korea did it again," you will draw the wrong security lesson. The Binance case is a story about hot-wallet security at the world's largest exchange and about state-linked hacking. This case, as far as the evidence goes, is a story about access to exchange accounts themselves: the attacker appears to have withdrawn from accounts the victim had used before, using wallets that had been active previously, which points investigators toward compromised account access or keys rather than a flaw in any blockchain's code. Those are different failure modes, and they demand different defences.

## The Allowlist Lesson: Why a Familiar Wallet Was Not Enough

One of the most instructive details in this incident is also the most unsettling for ordinary users. Many exchanges offer a withdrawal allowlist, sometimes called an address book or trusted-address list: you register the wallet addresses you control, and withdrawals to any other address are blocked or delayed. It is one of the standard security recommendations, including in our own guides. Bitquery's analysis found that eight of the 11 wallets in the original tracing had been active before the incident, with some dating back to May 2026. In other words, the attacker withdrew to wallets the victim had used before — the kind of destination an allowlist is designed to permit.

That does not mean allowlists are useless. It means they are not sufficient on their own, and beginners should understand their limit. An allowlist answers the question "is this destination familiar?" It does not answer the question "is the person making this withdrawal really the account holder?" If an attacker gains control of an exchange account — through stolen login credentials, a compromised device, a SIM-swap, or access to an API key — then withdrawals to familiar addresses will sail through an allowlist, because the destination looks legitimate. The defence that matters in that scenario is everything before the withdrawal: a unique password, hardware or app-based two-factor authentication rather than SMS where possible, alerts on logins and withdrawals, withdrawal delays or approval steps for large amounts, and strict control over API keys. An allowlist is one layer. It was never meant to be the only layer, and this incident is a vivid demonstration of why.

There is a second lesson in the numbers. Of the roughly $78 million still in tracked wallets on 2 November, only about $3.7 million was in USDC that its issuer could freeze. The rest was in ETH and DAI, which no issuer can freeze. That is a structural feature of cryptocurrency, not a bug: once funds leave a centralised exchange and are converted into assets without an issuer freeze function, recovery depends on exchanges flagging and holding deposits when they arrive, on law enforcement, and on the slow work of tracing — not on a single button that reverses the transfer. Beginners who assume stolen crypto can simply be "frozen" like a bank account are misunderstanding the system. Sometimes it can be, when a stablecoin issuer acts. Often it cannot, and speed matters enormously in the first hours, which is why investigators publish addresses publicly: to give every exchange a chance to flag the funds before they move again.

## What This Means for Indian Users

India is not directly involved in this incident — no Indian exchange or Indian victim has been named — but the lessons transfer directly, because Indian users use the same global exchanges, the same account-security tools, and the same kinds of wallets.

First, secure the account, not just the coins. Most beginners focus on where their crypto is stored and neglect the exchange account itself. Use a password you use nowhere else, turn on app-based two-factor authentication, turn on login and withdrawal alerts, and review active sessions and API keys regularly. If your exchange offers withdrawal allowlisting, turn it on — but remember its limit, as explained above, and do not treat it as a guarantee.

Second, treat large, unusual withdrawals as an emergency, even if they go to a familiar address. If you receive a withdrawal alert you did not trigger, act immediately: contact the exchange, freeze the account if the option exists, and change your credentials from a clean device. In this incident, funds kept moving after the first public alert, which shows how fast the window for freezing closes.

Third, ignore recovery scams. After every major theft, scammers pose as investigators, exchange support staff, or recovery agents offering to retrieve stolen funds for a fee, and they often ask for your seed phrase or private keys. No legitimate investigator or support team will ever ask for those. Anyone who does is a scammer, full stop. This applies whether or not you were affected by this incident — scammers reuse the headlines regardless.

Fourth, keep records for tax purposes. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. In India, gains on transfers of virtual digital assets are generally taxed at a flat 30%, with 1% TDS on transfers in many cases, and losses generally cannot be set off against gains. That framework applies to your own transfers, not to thefts by others, but good records — dates, assets, amounts, exchanges, and wallet addresses — are what let you explain any transaction later, including an unusual one. If you ever need to report a compromised account to an exchange or to the police, those records are your starting point.

Fifth, keep the Binance comparison in perspective. The same week brought the Binance theft, this suspected European theft, and reports of a separate incident at Brazil's Mercado Bitcoin, in which an estimated $40 million was drained from hot wallets according to security firm Hypernative, as noted by CoinDesk on 3 November. A cluster of incidents does not prove a single cause, and it does not mean every exchange is unsafe. What it does mean is that this is a period of elevated risk, and the right response is steady hygiene — strong account security, self-custody for funds you are not actively trading, and scepticism toward anyone promising fast recovery — not panic, and not a rush to move funds through unfamiliar services in the middle of a scare.

## The Bottom Line

ZachXBT $133M EU crypto theft is a story with two layers, and beginners should keep them apart. The verifiable layer is a trail of transactions: more than $108 million traced by Bitquery from exchange accounts starting 1 November 2026, about $63 million swapped into Ethereum within hours, funds moved through instant exchanges and bridges toward Monero, and Kraken restricting linked accounts on 3 November. The unverified layer is the victim theory: that a large Western European financial institution was exploited and is covering it up. That may turn out to be true, partly true, or wrong, and until a victim or an authority confirms it, it should stay labelled as a theory.

What is already useful, without waiting for confirmation, is the security lesson. An attacker who can operate an exchange account can defeat an allowlist by using familiar wallets, and once funds are converted out of freezable stablecoins, recovery becomes slow and uncertain. Strong account security, fast reaction to alerts, and a refusal to hand your keys to anyone are the defences that actually work in that world. Watch for what comes next — a victim statement, a regulator comment, or further tracing — and update your understanding when it arrives, rather than treating the first headline as the final word.
