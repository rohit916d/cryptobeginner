Bitcoin has lost about 109,000 non-empty wallets in less than a month, according to on-chain analytics firm Santiment. The count of funded Bitcoin addresses peaked at 59.99 million on 16 October 2026 and has since fallen by roughly 108,700, to about 59.88 million. The decline came as Bitcoin slipped below $75,000, trading near $74,500 on 10 November and around $73,300 on the morning of 11 November — roughly ₹69 lakh in Indian terms. Santiment reads the trend as a possible sign of retail capitulation: smaller holders losing patience after weeks of falling prices. This guide explains what the number actually measures, what capitulation means, why the figure is easy to misread, and what Indian crypto users should take away from it.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a published on-chain metric and the current Indian rules; it does not recommend buying, selling or holding any asset, and it does not predict where prices will go.

## What Happened: 109,000 Fewer Funded Wallets Since 16 October

The headline figure comes from Santiment, which shared the data on 10 November 2026 and was reported the same day by CCN, KuCoin, Finbold, TradingView, Blockonomi and other outlets. The facts, as reported across those sources, are:

- Bitcoin's non-empty wallet count reached 59.99 million on 16 October 2026, narrowly missing the round 60 million mark.
- Since then, the count has fallen by approximately 108,700 wallets, to about 59.88 million.
- During the same period, Bitcoin's price fell from about $84,890 to around $74,547, according to TradingView's coverage of the Santiment data.
- On 10 November, Bitcoin briefly touched $72,584.60 — its lowest daily low since 20 August — and closed the day at $73,700.30, according to Kraken daily candle data.
- The Crypto Fear & Greed Index stood at 29, in "Fear" territory, down from 32 the previous day.

Put simply, the network has slightly fewer funded addresses than it did a month ago, and the decline happened during a stretch of falling prices. That is the whole of the hard data. Everything else — what it means, who is selling, and what comes next — is interpretation, and the interpretation is where beginners most often get misled.

## What Is a "Non-Empty Wallet"?

To understand the number, it helps to know what is being counted. A "non-empty wallet" in Santiment's metric is a Bitcoin address that holds a balance greater than zero. It is not a person, an account, or an app. It is a single address on the blockchain with some Bitcoin in it.

That distinction matters for three reasons:

**One person can own many addresses.** A single user can generate a fresh address for every transaction, which is standard practice in many wallets for privacy. If that user consolidates their coins into one address — a common thing to do during a sell-off, when people move funds to an exchange or tidy up their holdings — several funded addresses can disappear without anyone leaving Bitcoin at all.

**One address can belong to many people.** Custodial wallets, such as the hot wallets run by exchanges, hold coins for thousands or millions of customers in a small number of addresses. If an exchange moves customer funds between its own wallets, or if customers withdraw coins to their own addresses, the count of funded addresses can move in ways that have nothing to do with how many individuals hold Bitcoin.

**Dust and tiny balances count too.** An address holding a fraction of a cent of Bitcoin still counts as non-empty. When prices fall, some holders sweep small balances into a single address or spend them, and those addresses drop out of the count.

So a fall of 108,700 funded addresses means the network has fewer addresses holding a balance. It does not establish that 109,000 people sold their Bitcoin, and it does not prove that network adoption has reversed. CCN's own coverage makes exactly this point: the 0.18% decline "does not establish that 109,000 people abandoned the asset or that network adoption has reversed."

## What Is Retail Capitulation?

"Capitulation" is a market term for the moment when investors give up. After weeks of losses, fear and frustration build until holders who have been waiting for a recovery decide they cannot take it any more and sell — often at or near the point of maximum pessimism. It is called "retail" capitulation when the selling appears to come mainly from smaller, individual holders rather than from large institutions or long-term holders.

Santiment's reading is that the falling wallet count is evidence of this behaviour among smaller holders: people with modest balances emptying their wallets as prices slide. The firm has also pointed to the mood around the market — the Fear & Greed Index at 29, in "Fear" territory, fits the picture of discouraged holders.

It is important to keep the label in its place. Capitulation describes behaviour that has already happened: wallets emptied, coins moved, patience exhausted. It is a description of the past, not a prediction about the future. Santiment itself has been careful on this point, noting that similar episodes of retail capitulation have appeared before major market recoveries in the past — but also that the accumulation data alone does not confirm that prices have bottomed out. A pattern that has appeared before recoveries is not a promise of one.

## The Other Side of the Data: Bigger Wallets Kept Buying

The wallet-count decline is only half of what Santiment reported. The same data shows a split between smaller and larger holders that complicates any simple "everyone is leaving" story:

- **Wallets holding 1 to 10 BTC accumulated 10,076 Bitcoin over the past two weeks**, according to Santiment.
- **Wallets holding 10 to 100 BTC added another 4,131 Bitcoin** in the same period — a combined 14,207 BTC accumulated by wallets in the 1–100 BTC range.
- **Bitcoin held on exchanges has fallen to about 1.28 million BTC**, the lowest in the recent data, which means fewer coins are sitting on trading platforms and available for immediate sale.
- **Strategy, the largest corporate holder of Bitcoin, bought another 1,036 BTC for about $81.9 million between 2 and 8 November**, taking its total to 850,364 BTC as of 8 November 2026, according to TradingView's coverage.
- **The Coinbase Premium Index was negative at −0.1468% on 10 November**, pointing to weaker relative demand on the US exchange — a sign that US buyers were not rushing in either.

Read together, the picture is not a one-way exit. Some smaller holders appear to be emptying wallets while some mid-sized holders are adding to theirs, and coins are leaving exchanges rather than piling up on them. That is a mixed, noisy picture — which is exactly why a single number like "109,000 wallets lost" should never be read on its own.

## Why Wallet Counts Are Easy to Misread

On-chain metrics look precise because they are numbers. That precision is deceptive. Here are the main traps beginners should watch for when a headline quotes a wallet count:

**The percentage is tiny.** A fall of 108,700 sounds dramatic, but it is about 0.18% of a 59.99 million base. The network still has roughly 59.88 million funded addresses. A headline that says "Bitcoin loses 109,000 wallets" is accurate, but the scale of the change is small relative to the total.

**Consolidation looks like exit.** When prices fall, holders often move coins from several addresses into one — to an exchange to sell, or to a single hardware wallet for safekeeping. Both actions reduce the funded-address count. Only the first involves selling; the second is the opposite of leaving.

**Addresses are not people.** Glassnode data cited in the coverage underlines the distinction between funded addresses and actual network activity. A person can hold coins across dozens of addresses, and an exchange can hold thousands of customers' coins in one. Any claim of the form "X people left Bitcoin" based on an address count is a guess, not a measurement.

**Exchange balances tell a different story.** If holders were rushing for the exits, you might expect coins to flood onto exchanges, ready to sell. Instead, exchange supply has fallen to about 1.28 million BTC. That does not prove holders are confident — it is consistent with coins moving into self-custody, or with selling already completed — but it does show the exit story is incomplete.

**The metric lags the mood.** Wallet counts move slowly and reflect actions taken over weeks. By the time a "109,000 wallets lost" headline appears, the selling it describes may already be weeks old. It is a rear-view mirror, not a windscreen.

## What Santiment Actually Claims — and Its Limits

It is worth stating Santiment's own position precisely, because the coverage of it has been mixed. The firm says the decline in non-empty wallets is a potential sign of retail capitulation, and that similar capitulation episodes have preceded major recoveries in past cycles. It also says the trend could take on a different meaning if Bitcoin holds roughly $74,000 to $75,000 while wallet numbers keep falling — its proposed sign that selling pressure may be easing. And it says a recovery toward $78,000 to $80,000 would further support its interpretation.

None of that is a forecast. Santiment's own caveat, repeated in the coverage, is that the accumulation data alone does not confirm that the market has bottomed out. The firm is describing a pattern and the conditions under which it would regard that pattern as meaningful — not telling anyone what will happen next.

For a beginner, the honest way to read the claim is: an analytics firm sees smaller holders giving up, sees larger holders buying, and regards the combination as a pattern that has appeared before recoveries — while explicitly warning that it proves nothing on its own. Any article or social-media post that turns that into "Bitcoin is about to recover" or "the bottom is in" has gone beyond what the data supports.

## What This Means for Indian Crypto Users

For an Indian reader, the wallet-count story sits on top of rules that do not change with any single metric:

**Selling has a tax cost.** In India, selling crypto is a taxable transfer. Gains are taxed at a flat 30% plus a 4% cess (31.2% in total), with no set-off of losses against other income, and sales attract 1% TDS on the sale value. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. That means a decision to sell during a downturn locks in the tax treatment of whatever gain or loss the sale produces — it is a real cost of reacting to a headline, and it is worth understanding before acting on any market news.

**INR prices move with the dollar and the rupee.** Bitcoin near $73,300 translates to roughly ₹69 lakh at current exchange rates. When the rupee weakens against the dollar, the INR price of Bitcoin can rise even while the dollar price falls — and the reverse is also true. Indian holders should always check which currency a headline is quoting before judging how much their own holdings have moved.

**Your wallet hygiene matters more than the network's wallet count.** The number of funded addresses on the network tells you nothing about the safety of your own coins. What protects an Indian holder is the basics: a seed phrase stored offline and never shared, two-factor authentication on exchange accounts, withdrawing long-term holdings to self-custody where appropriate, and never entering a seed phrase into a website or app that asks for it. The recent wave of Indian enforcement and fraud cases — from the ED searches at CoinDCX and Delta Exchange to the AP CID fraud bust — is a reminder that the biggest risk for most Indian users is not a market move but a compromised account or a scam.

**Fear is a mood, not a signal.** The Fear & Greed Index at 29 tells you the market is frightened. It does not tell you what to do. Fear can persist for weeks, and it can be wrong in either direction. The useful response to a fear reading is to check your own plan — how much you hold, whether you can afford to lose it, and whether your coins are stored safely — rather than to copy the crowd's mood.

## How to Read On-Chain Headlines Like This

On-chain data will keep producing headlines, and most of them will be framed as drama. A short checklist helps separate the useful information from the noise:

1. **Ask what is actually counted.** Addresses, transactions, exchange balances and active users are different things. A headline about wallets is a headline about addresses, not about people.
2. **Ask for the percentage, not just the number.** 109,000 sounds large; 0.18% of the total puts it in context.
3. **Look for the counter-signal.** In this story, the same data set showed mid-sized wallets accumulating 14,207 BTC. A report that quotes only the scary half of the data is telling half the story.
4. **Check who is interpreting, and what they actually said.** Santiment's interpretation comes with caveats. If a post drops the caveats, it is no longer reporting the data — it is selling a narrative.
5. **Separate the metric from your own situation.** A network-wide wallet count does not change your tax bill, your seed phrase, or your financial position. Decisions about your own holdings should be based on your own circumstances, not on a single on-chain chart.

## The Bottom Line

Bitcoin's non-empty wallet count has fallen by about 109,000 since 16 October 2026, and Santiment reads the decline as a possible sign of retail capitulation among smaller holders. The number is real, the interpretation is plausible, and the caveats are essential: addresses are not people, the decline is 0.18% of the total, consolidation can look like exit, and the same data shows mid-sized wallets accumulating thousands of Bitcoin while exchange balances fall. For Indian users, the practical takeaway is not a market call but a reminder — understand what the metric measures, know the tax cost of any sale, keep your own coins secure, and treat fear readings as information about the crowd's mood, not as instructions. On-chain data is a useful window into the market. It is not a crystal ball.
