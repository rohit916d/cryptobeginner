On the evening of 12 November 2026, the world's most valuable artificial intelligence company lost its chief executive without warning — and within hours, Bitcoin was trading below $72,000 for the first time since August. Nvidia announced that co-founder Jensen Huang had resigned as president, CEO and board member, effective immediately. AI stocks sold off, Nvidia's own shares fell nearly 6%, and the selling spread into crypto, where Bitcoin dropped to about $71,200 and Ethereum briefly slipped under $2,000. More than $700 million of leveraged crypto positions were liquidated in 24 hours. This guide explains what happened, why a resignation at a chip company moved the crypto market, and what Indian users should — and should not — take from it.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a published news event; it does not recommend buying, selling or holding any asset, and it does not predict where prices will go.

## What Happened: The Facts at a Glance

The facts, as reported on 12 November 2026 by FXStreet, TradingView, Crypto Briefing, Brave New Coin, The Market Periodical, AMBCrypto and Bitcoinsistemi, are:

- **Nvidia's CEO resigned with immediate effect.** Nvidia announced on the evening of 12 November that Jensen Huang, who co-founded the company and had led it since 1993, had resigned as president, chief executive and board member, effective immediately. No successor was named in the first reports, and no reason for the departure was given in the initial coverage.
- **Nvidia shares fell sharply.** Nvidia traded at about $163.60, down roughly 5.8% at the time FXStreet reported the news, and the selling spread across AI and semiconductor stocks. The announcement came after US markets had closed, so the first full reaction in stocks was still to come when the crypto move was already under way.
- **Bitcoin fell below $72,000 for the first time since 20 August.** Bitcoin dropped to about $71,182 at its lowest, according to CoinMarketCap data cited by The Market Periodical, after starting the day near $74,700. Finnhub's snapshot at 21:43 UTC on 12 November showed Bitcoin at $71,591.99, down about 3.9% on the day. The move took Bitcoin below its 200-day average of roughly $73,173 and below the short-term-holder cost basis of about $75,600 — a level this site's earlier coverage has flagged as an important threshold.
- **Ethereum briefly fell under $2,000.** Ethereum dropped to about $1,990 before recovering to around $2,000, according to FXStreet. The broader crypto market lost about 2.7% in 24 hours, with total market capitalisation falling to roughly $2.38 trillion.
- **Liquidations topped $700 million in 24 hours.** Coinglass data cited across the day's coverage put total crypto liquidations at roughly $700–745 million in 24 hours, with about $630–642 million of that coming from long positions — traders who had bet prices would rise. Bitcoin alone accounted for about $282 million of liquidations, with roughly 95% of those being long positions. Different outlets cite slightly different totals because they cover different reporting windows, so the precise figure varies by source.
- **US spot Bitcoin ETFs kept losing money.** US spot Bitcoin ETFs recorded about $299.8 million in net outflows in the last completed session, according to Farside Investors data cited by TradingView, bringing redemptions over the past five trading sessions to roughly $1.44 billion. Spot Ethereum ETFs saw about $144.8 million in outflows in the same session, taking their five-session total to about $524 million.
- **In India, Bitcoin traded at roughly ₹67.1–67.3 lakh.** Gadgets 360's price tracker put Bitcoin at about ₹67.13–67.32 lakh on 13 November, with Ethereum at about ₹1.89 lakh. Indian prices track the global dollar price, converted at the rupee rate, plus the usual local premium that comes from INR–USDT conversion spreads and domestic liquidity.

Two caveats matter before going further. First, the resignation is a corporate-governance event at Nvidia. Nothing about Bitcoin's network changed on 12 November: blocks kept being produced, and the next automatic difficulty adjustment — estimated at about a 7.7% cut, the largest since June — is a routine recalibration, not a reaction to the news. Second, the figures above are snapshots from a moving market. Prices, liquidation totals and ETF flows differ slightly between sources because each one measures a different window. The direction of the move is consistent across every source; the exact decimals are not the point.

## Who Is Jensen Huang, and Why Does His Exit Matter?

Jensen Huang co-founded Nvidia in 1993 and has been its chief executive ever since — one of the longest continuous runs at the top of any major technology company. Under his leadership, Nvidia went from a maker of graphics cards for computer games to the company whose chips power most of the world's artificial intelligence systems. By 2026, Nvidia had become the most valuable company in the world by market capitalisation, and its share price had become the single most watched number in the AI trade.

That is why a resignation matters more here than it would at most companies. Investors did not just own Nvidia shares; they owned a story — that demand for AI computing would keep growing, that Nvidia would keep supplying it, and that Huang was the person steering that story. A sudden departure, announced after the market closed and effective immediately, removes the central figure of that story without a named successor and without an explanation in the first reports. Markets do not like that combination, and the first reaction is usually to sell first and ask questions later.

It is also worth saying plainly what the resignation is not. It is not a crypto story. Nvidia does not set Bitcoin's price, hold Bitcoin as a treasury asset, or operate a crypto exchange. The link between the two is indirect, and it runs through investor sentiment — which is exactly what the next section explains.

## Why a Chip-Company Resignation Moved Crypto

The connection between Nvidia and Bitcoin is not a wire or a contract. It is a habit of mind: many investors now treat AI stocks and crypto as part of the same trade, and when one side of that trade wobbles, the other side feels it.

**First, both are priced on confidence in future growth.** Nvidia's value rests on the belief that spending on AI infrastructure will keep rising for years. Bitcoin's price rests on the belief that demand for a scarce digital asset will keep growing. Neither belief can be proved from today's cash flows alone, so both are sensitive to the same thing: how confident investors feel about the future. A shock to that confidence — a surprise resignation at the flagship AI company — hits both at once.

**Second, the same money often holds both.** The investors who bought Nvidia on the AI story are, in many cases, the same investors who bought Bitcoin on the digital-gold or risk-asset story. When a portfolio takes a hit in one place, its owner often reduces risk everywhere at once, selling the assets that are easiest to sell first. Bitcoin trades 24 hours a day, seven days a week, with no closing bell and no circuit breakers, so it is often the first place that selling shows up — and the place where it keeps going after stock markets have closed for the day.

**Third, this was not the first time the link showed.** On 6 November, the Nasdaq suffered its worst day in about 18 months — down 5.52% — on post-midterm AI-regulation fears and doubts about AI valuations, and Bitcoin fell with it to about $75,369. The 10 November hot US inflation reading, which strengthened expectations of a December rate rise, pushed Bitcoin below $74,000 the same evening. The pattern is consistent: when technology stocks fall hard on a named driver, crypto falls with them, and often further, because it never closes.

**Fourth, leverage turned a fall into a rout.** A large share of crypto trading is done with borrowed money. When prices fall, traders who borrowed to bet on rising prices are forced to sell — their positions are liquidated — and that forced selling pushes prices lower, which liquidates more positions. About $222 million of long positions were liquidated within 30 minutes during the sharpest part of the 12 November fall, according to reports citing Coinglass data. The cascade is the same mechanism this site explained after the October liquidation wave: the initial trigger was a news event, but the size of the move was amplified by leverage unwinding.

None of this means Bitcoin and Nvidia will always move together. They have diverged before, and they will again. But in a week when both were already under pressure — Bitcoin below its 200-day average, ETFs seeing five straight sessions of outflows, inflation data pointing to tighter US monetary policy — a surprise at the top of the world's most valuable company was enough to tip an already fragile market into a sharp fall.

## The Liquidation Cascade in Plain Language

The word "liquidation" appears in almost every report of a sharp crypto fall, and it is worth understanding, because it explains why a 3% fall in Bitcoin can feel like a much bigger event.

Most crypto exchanges let traders borrow money to take larger positions than their own cash would allow. A trader with $1,000 can, for example, control $10,000 of Bitcoin. If the price rises, the gains are multiplied. If it falls, the losses are multiplied too — and if the losses reach the trader's deposit, the exchange closes the position automatically and sells the Bitcoin. That forced sale is a liquidation.

On 12 November, the forced sales were heavily one-sided: roughly 95% of Bitcoin liquidations were long positions, meaning traders who had bet on rising prices. When the price broke below $72,000 — a level many traders had treated as a floor — a wave of those positions was closed at once. The sales pushed the price lower, which closed more positions, which pushed the price lower again. The largest long-liquidation cluster sat at about $71,000, just below the day's low, which is where the next wave of forced selling would have gathered if the price had kept falling.

For Indian users, the practical point is simple: leverage is what turns a bad day into a disaster for individual traders. The price fall itself was about 3–4% in a day. The traders who lost the most were not the ones who simply owned Bitcoin — they were the ones who had borrowed to bet on it. That distinction is the difference between a volatile day and a wiped-out account, and it is why every guide on this site repeats the same warning about leverage.

## What the Numbers Say — and What They Don't

A few numbers from 12 November deserve a closer look, because each one is easy to misread.

**Below $72,000, and below the 200-day average.** Bitcoin's fall took it below $72,000 for the first time since 20 August, and below its 200-day average of about $73,173. These are levels traders watch, and breaking them tends to trigger more selling — partly because automated trading systems are programmed to sell when a level breaks, and partly because the break itself becomes the headline. But a price level is not a law of nature. It is a line on a chart that traders have agreed to watch, and its importance comes from that agreement, not from anything in Bitcoin's code.

**Below the short-term-holder cost basis.** Bitcoin also traded below about $75,600, the average price at which recent buyers bought in. When the price sits below that level, the average recent buyer is holding a loss. That matters for sentiment — people who are losing money are more likely to sell — but it says nothing about where the price goes next. The same measure has been below the price for long stretches in past corrections and above it in rallies.

**Five straight sessions of ETF outflows.** US spot Bitcoin ETFs have now seen about $1.44 billion leave over five trading sessions, with $299.8 million in the latest session. Outflows mean investors are selling ETF shares, which usually means the funds are selling Bitcoin. But ETF flows are a symptom, not a cause: they reflect the same loss of confidence that moved the price, and they can reverse as quickly as they started. The same funds saw a brief $74.6 million inflow on 4 November, in the middle of the current downtrend.

**A difficulty adjustment is coming, and it is routine.** Bitcoin's mining difficulty is estimated to fall by about 7.7% at the next adjustment, expected around 13 November, which would be the largest cut since June. The reason is simple: blocks have been arriving slower than the 10-minute target, averaging about 10 minutes 49 seconds in the current period, so the network is recalibrating to keep block times steady. This is an automatic process that happens roughly every two weeks. It is not a reaction to the Nvidia resignation, and it is not a signal about the price. It is worth knowing about only because it will be reported as if it were one.

The honest summary is this: every number from 12 November describes a market under pressure, not a market with a known destination. A fall to a 12-week low is a fact. What happens next is not in any of these numbers, and anyone who tells you it is — in either direction — is selling you a prediction, not an explanation.

## What This Means for Indian Users

The Nvidia resignation is a US corporate story, and its direct effect on Indian crypto users is limited. But the fallout touches Indian users in a few concrete ways.

**Rupee prices follow the dollar price.** Bitcoin in India was trading at roughly ₹67.1–67.3 lakh, down from about ₹79.8 lakh a day earlier according to Gadgets 360's tracker — a fall of roughly 15–16% in rupee terms over the preceding 24 hours, reflecting both the dollar decline and the exchange-rate movement. Indian exchanges usually quote a small premium over the global price, because of INR–USDT conversion spreads and local liquidity. That premium was still visible on 13 November, with some Indian platforms quoting higher than the global converted rate. The premium is a cost of buying in India, and it does not disappear when prices fall.

**Tax rules are unchanged.** Crypto gains in India are taxed at a flat 30% plus a 4% cess, with 1% TDS on transfers, and none of that changed on 12 November. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. A fall in price does not change the tax treatment of a sale, and losses cannot be set off against other income under current rules — a point that matters more, not less, on days when many people are selling.

**Indian exchanges are not Nvidia.** The resignation has no direct connection to any Indian exchange. But the week has already been a difficult one for Indian exchanges: Unocoin suspended all services on 12 November after a security breach, and the Enforcement Directorate's searches at CoinDCX and Delta Exchange are still an open investigation. If you hold crypto on an Indian exchange, the relevant question is not what Nvidia's CEO did — it is whether your exchange is operating normally, and whether you have your own records of what you hold.

**Volatile days are scam days.** Sharp falls reliably produce a wave of scams: fake "recovery" services offering to retrieve lost funds, fake exchange-support accounts messaging users during outages, and fake tips claiming to know the bottom. None of these can do what they promise. Verify any claim through an exchange's official website or verified account, never share a seed phrase, private key or one-time password with anyone, and treat any message that creates urgency — "act now or lose everything" — as a warning sign in itself.

**Leverage is the real risk for most users.** The traders who lost the most on 12 November were leveraged long traders, not people who simply held Bitcoin. If you use leverage, understand that a 3–4% move in the underlying asset can close your position entirely. If you do not use leverage, a 3–4% fall is a bad day, not a catastrophe. That difference is the single most important thing to understand about days like this one.

## What to Watch Next

Four things will show whether 12 November was a one-day shock or the start of a longer slide — and none of them is a prediction.

- **Nvidia's next announcement.** The first reports named no successor and gave no reason for the resignation. Who Nvidia names as the next chief executive, and how the market reacts to that choice, will matter more than the resignation itself. A credible, well-received appointment would steady the AI trade; a prolonged vacancy or a contested choice would not.
- **The US producer price index, due 13 November at 19:00 IST.** The October PPI is the week's main remaining data release, with consensus at about +0.4% month on month. It follows the hot October CPI reading of 10 November, which strengthened expectations of a December rate rise. A hot PPI would add to the pressure on risk assets; a soft one would ease it. Either way, it is a data point, not a verdict.
- **Whether Bitcoin reclaims $75,600.** That level — the short-term-holder cost basis — is the first hurdle on the way back up, and the $73,000–$73,200 zone is the support traders are watching below. These are levels to watch, not targets to trade on: a reclaim would show buyers returning, and a break below $71,000 would show the liquidation pressure continuing. Neither outcome tells you what happens the week after.
- **ETF flows.** Five straight sessions of outflows totalling about $1.44 billion is a real drag on the market, because ETF selling adds to the supply that buyers must absorb. A return to inflows would be the clearest sign that institutional sentiment has stabilised. Until then, the flow data is part of the same story as the price: confidence, once lost, takes time to return.

None of these is a signal to act on by itself. They are the facts to watch while the dust settles, and they are the facts that will determine whether 12 November is remembered as the day a CEO resignation knocked crypto to a 12-week low, or as the day the AI trade started to come apart.
