# Bitcoin's Two-Year Low Near $55,000: The Whipsaw in India

Bitcoin fell to a two-year low near $55,000 on Saturday, 21 November 2026 — and then, within hours, climbed roughly $5,000 to push back above $60,000. The two moves together are what traders call a whipsaw: a violent fall followed immediately by a violent rebound, with leveraged traders caught on both sides. On Friday, about $2 billion of long positions — bets that prices would rise — were wiped out. On Saturday, about $230 million of short positions — bets that prices would keep falling — were liquidated in just four hours. This guide explains what happened on 21 November, why the selling started, how the rebound worked, and what — if anything — the whipsaw means for an Indian reader.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a reported market event; it does not recommend any token, investment or action, and it does not predict any price movement.

## What Happened on Saturday 21 November

The fall began on Friday, 20 November, when Bitcoin dropped below $60,000 for the first time since July — the move this site covered in its previous post. That was not the bottom. According to Bitcoinist, Bitcoin fell to $55,106 on Coinbase in the 06:00 UTC hour on Saturday, its weakest print since September 2024, before recovering to around $57,800 by mid-morning. Goodreturns, citing Bloomberg, reported that Bitcoin had fallen 5.10% to $55,830 on Friday, its lowest level since September 2024, and then extended its losses in Asian trading on Saturday, dropping another 5.7% to $55,533. The figures differ slightly because they come from different exchanges and different hours of a fast-moving weekend — but every source agrees on the shape of the move: a two-year low, reached on Saturday morning.

The rebound was just as fast. According to CryptoPotato, Bitcoin slid to $55,000 and then recovered above $60,000 within hours on 21 November, climbing roughly $5,000 from the low. A verified snapshot at 22:30 UTC on 21 November put Bitcoin at $60,252, or about ₹55.8 lakh, up 2.3% over 24 hours but still down 5.4% over seven days, with a market capitalisation of $1.21 trillion and $39.91 billion in 24-hour volume. By Sunday morning, 22 November, Bitcoin was trading at roughly $58,900, or about ₹54.9 lakh to ₹55.6 lakh, according to the Sunday Guardian — still well below where the week began, but far above Saturday's low.

The damage was broad, and the recovery was broad too. Ether slid toward $1,500 before trading near $1,700, according to CryptoPotato, while BNB recovered from $500 to $545 and XRP bounced from a test of the $0.85 support level to $0.92. CryptoPotato named HYPE and ZEC as the two biggest losers of the prior day, and both regained a significant portion of their losses in the rebound. Bitcoin is now down roughly 53% from its all-time high of over $126,000 in October 2025, according to the Sunday Guardian — a fall of more than half its value in about six weeks.

## Why Bitcoin Fell: The Same Drivers, Accelerated

The trigger for Saturday's low was not a crypto event. It was the continuation of the same broad sell-off this site covered on Friday: doubts about artificial-intelligence stock valuations, expectations of tighter US monetary policy, and money leaving Bitcoin ETFs.

The AI trade was the first driver. As Vikaas M Sachdeva, CEO of BitDelta India, told Goodreturns, "Bitcoin's decline towards $55,000 reflects a broad global sell-off triggered by growing concerns around AI valuations and the sharp correction in US technology stocks, including Friday's 5.6% slide in the Nasdaq-100. As investors move to reduce risk and raise liquidity, selling has extended beyond equities into both gold and Bitcoin." The same money that bought AI stocks also bought crypto for much of 2026, and when that story cracked, investors sold both at once. The selling spread further than stocks and crypto: gold fell on the same days, which is unusual, because gold is normally the asset investors buy when they sell everything else. When gold falls on the same day as stocks and crypto, investors are not rotating from one asset to another — they are raising cash.

The second driver was the Federal Reserve. Expectations of a December US rate hike, alongside elevated Treasury yields, added to the pressure on assets that pay no interest, such as Bitcoin. The December Fed meeting, on 8–9 December, now sets the near-term tone: traders currently price a 97.9% chance of a hike, according to BitcoinWorld. Higher rates raise the return on holding cash — two-year US Treasuries now pay around 4.7% — which makes a volatile asset like Bitcoin less attractive by comparison, and they raise borrowing costs across the economy, which squeezes the leveraged bets that crypto markets run on.

The third driver was ETF outflows. Spot Bitcoin ETFs saw notable net outflows of about $2.35 billion in recent sessions, according to the Sunday Guardian, as traders rushed to cash and increased short positions to hedge downside exposure. Goodreturns put November's total outflows from US spot Bitcoin ETFs at nearly $3.5 billion. That is the institutional side of the same story: money leaving the funds that were the main source of buying for much of the year. This site covered the record single-day outflow — $541.1 million from BlackRock's iShares Bitcoin Trust on 19 November — in its previous post; Saturday's low came after two more days of the same flow.

## The Rebound: $230 Million in Shorts Liquidated in Four Hours

The most instructive part of Saturday was not the fall but the rebound — because it shows how the same mechanism that makes a market fall fast can also make it rise fast.

When Bitcoin fell to $55,000, many traders bet that it would fall further. A short position is a bet on a falling price, made with borrowed money: the trader borrows Bitcoin, sells it, and hopes to buy it back cheaper later. If the price rises instead, the exchange automatically closes the position to protect itself — and closing a short position means buying Bitcoin back. That forced buying pushes the price higher, which triggers the next set of automatic closures, and so on. It is the liquidation cascade this site explained on 29 October and again on 5 November — only running in the opposite direction.

According to CoinGlass data cited by CryptoPotato, about $230 million of short positions were liquidated in four hours during the recovery, against roughly $23 million of longs. In other words, during those four hours, shorts accounted for nearly all of the forced closures. The selling had begun at the end of the previous business week and accelerated through the weekend, a period that has become more eventful since the launch of spot Bitcoin ETFs in early 2024, according to CryptoPotato — and the rebound was just as concentrated, with the whole $5,000 recovery happening within hours.

The scale of the two-sided damage is worth stating plainly. Roughly $2 billion in long positions were wiped out over the 24 hours before the rebound, according to CryptoPotato. Then, in the rebound, $230 million of shorts were wiped out in four hours. Traders who bet on prices rising lost on Friday and Saturday morning; traders who bet on prices falling lost on Saturday afternoon. That is the nature of a whipsaw: it punishes both directions in turn, and it punishes leveraged bets hardest of all.

## $1.57 Billion in Liquidations: How the Cascade Works

The weekend's moves were amplified by liquidations on both sides, and the numbers from Friday show how large the forced selling had become before the rebound even started.

Crypto exchanges recorded $1.57 billion in liquidations over 24 hours on 20 November, as Bitcoin dropped 4.11% to $59,370, according to U.Today, citing CoinGlass data. Long positions accounted for $1.43 billion of the total, with 265,525 traders liquidated. Bitcoin saw $554.28 million in liquidations, followed by Ethereum with $331.08 million and XRP with $14.67 million. The liquidations were triggered by the sharp decline in Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies, which coincided with the sell-off in AI-related stocks.

A leveraged position is a bet made with borrowed money: a trader puts up a small amount of their own cash and borrows the rest, so a small price move becomes a large gain or loss. When the price moves against the bet far enough, the exchange automatically closes the position. That forced sale pushes the price down further, which triggers the next set of automatic closures, and so on. It is a chain reaction — and on Saturday it ran in both directions within a single day: first down, as longs were closed into the $55,000 low, then up, as shorts were closed into the $60,000 rebound.

For comparison, the liquidation clusters this site has covered recently were about $1.5 billion on Friday 20 November itself, about $1.3 billion on 13–14 November, about $1.9 billion on the weekend of 14–15 November, and about $2 billion at the $62,006 low on 15 November. Saturday's two-sided damage sits in the same range — large, but not the largest of the month — and it was driven by the same mechanism. The key point for a beginner is that liquidations are not caused by a single person's decision. They are built into the way leveraged trading works, and that is why a market can fall further and faster than the original news would seem to justify — and then rebound just as violently.

## What This Means for Indian Readers

For most Indian readers, the direct effect of Bitcoin touching $55,000 and rebounding above $60,000 is the same as any sharp market move: it changes the price on the screen, and it changes nothing else about how crypto works.

The price Indians actually pay is higher than the dollar figures in headlines. On 22 November, Bitcoin traded at roughly $58,900, or about ₹54.9 lakh to ₹55.6 lakh, according to the Sunday Guardian, and the verified snapshot at 22:30 UTC on 21 November put it at $60,252, or about ₹55.8 lakh. At the 19 November fix of The Crypto Times India Premium Index, Bitcoin cost about 12.27% more in India than the global price, as noted in the previous post — a premium that exists because moving money into and out of Indian exchanges is constrained by banking rules, taxes and limited liquidity. So when headlines say "$55,000" or "$60,000", an Indian buyer is typically looking at a rupee price several lakh higher than a straight currency conversion would suggest. That gap is worth knowing about before comparing Indian prices with global ones.

The tax reality is unchanged. Gains on transferring virtual digital assets in India are taxed at a flat 30% plus a 4% cess — 31.2% in total — with no set-off of losses, and sales attract 1% TDS under Section 194S. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. A price fall does not change Indian tax, does not create an exemption, and does not change which platforms an Indian resident is allowed to use. It also does not change the fact that losses cannot be set off against other income under current rules — a detail that matters more, not less, in a falling market, and matters again when a rebound tempts a quick sale.

There is also a practical issue Indian users should check this week. INR withdrawals from several exchanges operating in India have been delayed this month: Coinbase India first reported a banking-partner outage on 14 November and said on 20 November that it was processing pending payments through an alternative banking channel, CoinDCX warned on 19 November that withdrawals were taking four to six hours or longer, and ZebPay flagged similar delays on 11 November. Gadgets 360 reported the three-exchange disruption on 20 November. Anyone moving rupees should check their exchange's current status before starting a transfer, and should not assume a withdrawal will complete at its usual speed — especially on a weekend, when a whipsaw can move the price several lakh while a transfer is still pending.

## The Scam Warning

Sharp price moves in either direction are exactly when crypto scams multiply, because fear and urgency make people act quickly — and a whipsaw creates both emotions in a single day. Expect messages claiming that a particular coin is "the next Bitcoin", that the crash is "the last chance to buy before $100,000", or that a well-known exchange or personality is giving away crypto to "help" holders. None of that is real. Expect also messages posing as exchange support, offering to "recover" losses or "unlock" a withdrawal for a fee, and asking for a seed phrase, password or OTP. No legitimate exchange or support team will ever ask for those.

A related warning applies to the $55,000 low and the $60,000 rebound themselves. As the price approaches a widely watched level, social media fills with confident claims about what will happen next — that the low "must hold", that the rebound "proves" the bottom is in, or that breaking $55,000 "proves" a much bigger fall. Those claims are guesses dressed up as analysis, and this guide makes no such claim: the levels are historical reference points, not predictions. Treat any message that tells you what the price will do next — in either direction — as noise, and report fraud on India's National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal at cybercrime.gov.in or the 1930 helpline. Never share a seed phrase or password with anyone claiming to help.

## What to Watch Next

Four things will show where this sell-off goes from here, and none of them is a price prediction.

The first is the Federal Reserve meeting on 8–9 December. The market has priced in a hike at near-certainty, with the open question being its size — 25 or 50 basis points — after Fed Governor Christopher Waller's comments. What the Fed actually does, and what it signals about January, will matter more for crypto than any single day's price move, because rates set the return on the cash that investors are currently fleeing to.

The second is whether the $55,000 low holds. It is the lowest print since September 2024, and it is now the reference point below the $57,700 summer low that was breached on Saturday. Whether the price holds above it, tests it again, or breaks through it is a fact that will be visible in the coming days — and it will be reported as a fact, not forecast here. What matters for readers is simply that the level exists and is being watched.

The third is ETF flows. November's outflows — nearly $3.5 billion in total, including the record $541.1 million IBIT day — are the institutional side of the same story: money leaving the funds. If outflows slow or reverse, that will show up in the daily Farside Investors data before it shows up anywhere else, and this site will cover it when it happens.

The fourth is the AI trade itself. The doubts about AI valuations, the 5.6% fall in the Nasdaq-100 and the falls in AI-linked Bitcoin miners were the trigger for the weekend's selling. Whether those stocks stabilise or keep falling will tell you whether the pressure on crypto is easing or building — because the two trades, for now, are moving together.

On Saturday, 21 November 2026, Bitcoin fell to about $55,106 — its lowest level since September 2024 — breaking through the $57,700 summer low that it had only approached the day before. Within hours it climbed roughly $5,000 to push back above $60,000, wiping out about $230 million of short positions in four hours after roughly $2 billion of long positions had been wiped out in the previous 24 hours. The trigger was the same broad sell-off in AI stocks and risk assets, amplified by Federal Reserve rate-hike fears, nearly $3.5 billion in November ETF outflows, and about $1.57 billion in liquidations on Friday alone. Bitcoin is now down roughly 53% from its October 2025 all-time high of over $126,000.
