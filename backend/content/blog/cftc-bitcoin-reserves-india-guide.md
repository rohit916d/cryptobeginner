## CFTC Lets US Exchanges Count Bitcoin Toward Their Reserves

The US Commodity Futures Trading Commission has taken another step toward treating crypto as a normal part of regulated finance. On 23 November 2026, CFTC staff issued Staff Letter 26-31, saying that regulated derivatives exchanges, swap trading venues and clearinghouses may count Bitcoin, Ether and qualifying payment stablecoins toward the financial resources they are required to hold. The catch is a discount: Bitcoin and Ether are counted only after a minimum 20% haircut, and payment stablecoins after a minimum 2% haircut.

The development was reported the same day by crypto.news and investinglive, and on 24 November by CoinTelegraph, Crypto Briefing and TradingView. CFTC Chair Michael Selig welcomed the advisory, describing it as another step toward clarifying how digital assets are treated in regulated US derivatives markets. Michael Saylor, one of Bitcoin's most vocal advocates, flagged the move on X.

This guide explains what the CFTC Bitcoin reserves guidance actually says, what it does not do, why it matters, and what — if anything — it means for crypto users in India.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. This article explains a regulatory development; it is not a recommendation to buy, hold or trade any asset. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose.

## What the CFTC Guidance Actually Says

Under CFTC rules, regulated derivatives platforms must hold enough financial resources to cover at least 12 months of operating costs on a rolling basis, so that a rough patch does not turn into a shutdown. Until now, they could only count cash and traditional financial assets toward that requirement — not crypto held on their balance sheets.

Staff Letter 26-31, from the CFTC's Division of Market Oversight and Division of Clearing and Risk, changes that. It says staff would not object to Bitcoin, Ether and qualifying payment stablecoins being counted toward the requirement, provided the right discounts are applied. The letter applies to three types of entities: swap execution facilities (SEFs), designated contract markets (DCMs) and derivatives clearing organizations (DCOs).

The haircuts are the heart of the guidance:

| Asset | Minimum haircut | What it means in practice |
|---|---|---|
| Bitcoin | 20% | $1 million of Bitcoin counts as $800,000 toward reserves |
| Ether | 20% | $1 million of Ether counts as $800,000 toward reserves |
| Qualifying payment stablecoins | 2% | $1 million of stablecoins counts as $980,000 toward reserves |
| Other crypto (not readily marketable) | 100% | Cannot be counted at all |

The haircut exists because crypto prices move sharply. A reserve that is supposed to keep an exchange running through a bad year cannot be counted at full value if the asset behind it could fall 20% in a week. The 20% figure is not new: it matches the capital charges the CFTC introduced for futures brokers in March 2026, which in turn mirrored the US Securities and Exchange Commission's treatment of broker-dealers.

The guidance also requires firms to think about what regulators call wrong-way risk — the possibility that crypto prices fall at the same time the exchange's own business is losing money, so the reserve shrinks exactly when it is needed most. Clearinghouses must also build liquidity risk into their haircuts and review them monthly.

## What the Guidance Does Not Do

The limits on the guidance matter as much as the headline, and several reports stressed them on the day it was issued.

**It is staff guidance, not a formal rule.** The advisory reflects the views of CFTC staff, not a rule approved by the Commission itself. It does not provide no-action relief, which means its durability depends partly on how the Commission chooses to act on it later. A future Commission could take a different view.

**It does not touch the liquidity floor.** Beyond the 12-month operating-cost cushion, these entities must also meet separate liquidity requirements: at least three months of operating costs in unencumbered, liquid financial assets for SEFs, and at least six months for DCMs and DCOs. Crypto assets — including payment stablecoins — cannot be used to meet those thresholds. The CFTC said crypto is neither cash under accounting rules nor a highly liquid security, so that bucket must still be filled with cash or highly liquid securities. For a DCM or DCO, that means crypto can cover at most the other half of the year, and each dollar of Bitcoin counts as no more than 80 cents.

**It does not cover default resources.** The guidance is limited to financial resources for operating costs and certain business losses. It does not address the use of crypto assets to meet the default resources a clearinghouse holds against a member failure — the much larger pool of money that protects the system if a big trader defaults.

**It does not create new demand for Bitcoin or Ether.** As investinglive put it, the direct price signal is small: the advisory frees up some capital on the balance sheets of crypto-native venues, but it does not create new demand for either coin. Nobody is required to buy crypto because of this letter; venues that already hold crypto simply get to count some of it toward a regulatory requirement.

## Which Exchanges Does It Affect?

The guidance covers the CFTC-regulated venues that already hold digital assets as part of their businesses — the firms that had asked the CFTC for clarification. According to CoinTelegraph, CFTC-regulated entities in this space include Coinbase Derivatives, Crypto.com's North American Derivatives Exchange and Bitnomial Exchange, as well as prediction markets Kalshi and Polymarket US.

That list connects this story to two other CFTC developments covered on this site. On 17 November, this site reported the CFTC approving Backpack and tZERO as blockchain-based derivatives exchanges — the first such licences granted. And on 20 November, it reported CFTC Chair Selig telling Hyperliquid it must register as an exchange or partner with one to serve US retail traders. The reserves guidance is a different piece of the same picture: the CFTC is building, piece by piece, a framework in which crypto-native venues operate under the same kinds of rules as traditional derivatives exchanges — licences first, then capital treatment, then enforcement of the registration boundary.

The guidance also follows two earlier steps in the same direction. In December 2025, the CFTC first allowed Bitcoin, Ether and USDC to serve as collateral for derivatives transactions. Three months later, its staff introduced the 20% and 2% capital charges for brokers holding crypto. Staff Letter 26-31 extends that same treatment from brokers to the trading venues and clearinghouses themselves.

## How This Differs From the Other CFTC Stories on This Site

Because the CFTC has featured in several posts on this site in the last ten days, it is worth being precise about what is new here.

The Backpack and tZERO post (17 November) was about licences — permission to operate as a designated contract market and a derivatives clearing organization. The Hyperliquid post (20 November) was about registration pressure — the CFTC chair saying an offshore venue must register or partner with a registered exchange, plus Hong Kong's regulator adding Hyperliquid to a suspicious-platforms list. The prediction-markets memo post (14 November) was about a US House Oversight Committee investigation into insider trading on Polymarket and Kalshi.

This story is about none of those things. It is about capital treatment: whether crypto already sitting on a regulated venue's balance sheet can count toward the money it must set aside to keep operating. No new licence was granted, no venue was warned, and no market was opened or closed. The mechanism is accounting and risk management, not permission or enforcement — which is also why its immediate market impact is modest, and why the reports covering it were careful to say so.

## What It Means for Crypto Users in India

The guidance applies only to CFTC-regulated US derivatives venues. It does not apply to Indian exchanges, and it changes nothing about how an Indian user buys, holds or sells crypto. But there are four things worth understanding.

**India has no equivalent rule.** FIU-IND registration — the requirement that Indian crypto exchanges register with India's Financial Intelligence Unit — is an anti-money-laundering requirement, not a capital rule. It does not say anything about what assets an exchange may hold as reserves, and it does not treat crypto as a balance-sheet asset for regulatory purposes. Indian exchanges operate under Indian banking and tax rules, and there is no Indian framework that lets an exchange count Bitcoin toward its own capital requirements. The US guidance is therefore a contrast, not a precedent that applies here.

**The Indian context is banking disruption, not capital rules.** The live issue for Indian users in November 2026 has been access to rupees, not exchange capital: Coinbase halted INR withdrawals in India on 14 November and only partially resumed them on 18 November, CoinDCX reported INR withdrawal delays on 19–20 November, and Unocoin has had services suspended since 9 November after a security breach. Those are banking-partner and security problems. A US rule about operating reserves does not fix any of them, and Indian users should not read it as a signal about the safety of their own exchange's INR rails.

**Tax rules are unchanged.** Gains from transferring virtual digital assets in India are taxed at a flat 30% (plus applicable surcharge and 4% cess) under Section 115BBH, with 1% TDS under Section 194S on transactions above the specified thresholds. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. Nothing in the CFTC guidance affects Indian tax treatment, and the BWA's Budget 2027 recommendations — including a lower TDS and loss set-off — remain proposals with no government commitment.

**The direction of travel is still worth watching.** The practical significance of Staff Letter 26-31 is not the dollars it frees up today, which are small. It is that a major US regulator is now willing, in writing, to treat Bitcoin and Ether as assets that can sit on a regulated exchange's balance sheet and count toward a prudential requirement — with a haircut, with limits, and with wrong-way-risk conditions, but counted all the same. That is a different posture from a regulator that treats crypto as something to be kept outside the financial system entirely. For Indian users, the lesson is about the global direction of regulation, not about any immediate change at home — and Indian regulation has repeatedly moved on its own timetable, as the BWA self-regulatory body launch in October and the continuing FEMA scrutiny of offshore routing both show.

## The Bottom Line

CFTC Staff Letter 26-31, issued on 23 November 2026, lets regulated US derivatives exchanges, swap venues and clearinghouses count Bitcoin and Ether toward their 12-month operating-cost reserves after a 20% haircut, and qualifying payment stablecoins after a 2% haircut. Crypto still cannot be used for the separate cash-only liquidity floor, it cannot cover a clearinghouse's default resources, and the letter is staff guidance rather than a formal Commission rule — so its practical effect is modest and its durability is not guaranteed.

For Indian users, the story matters as a signal about where US regulation is heading, not as a change to anything at home. Indian exchanges remain governed by Indian rules, INR access remains the live issue, and the 30% tax plus 1% TDS framework is unchanged. The CFTC is treating crypto as a real, if discounted, balance-sheet asset — and that, rather than any price move, is the news.
