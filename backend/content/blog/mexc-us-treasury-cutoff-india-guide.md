The US Treasury has just taken its biggest enforcement step against a crypto exchange in years — and it did it with a tool most Indian readers have never heard of. On 6 November 2026, the Treasury's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, known as FinCEN, issued an order that will cut crypto exchange **MEXC Global** off from the American financial system, after alleging the exchange facilitated at least **$16 billion in transactions linked to sanctions evasion, cybercrime and other illicit activity**. At the prevailing exchange rate, that is roughly **₹1.5 lakh crore**.

This guide explains exactly what the order does, what the $16 billion figure is made of, why the Treasury chose this particular legal tool, what MEXC has said in response, and what it means for Indian users — including why it matters in the same week that India's own Enforcement Directorate searched premises linked to CoinDCX and Delta Exchange. Every figure is attributed to its source, and nothing here is a prediction or a recommendation about any coin or exchange.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a reported regulatory action; it does not recommend any coin, exchange or trade.

## What the US Treasury Actually Ordered

FinCEN's order, issued on Friday 6 November 2026, finds MEXC Global to be a financial institution operating outside the United States that is a **"primary money laundering concern in connection with Russian illicit finance"**. The order bars covered US financial institutions from engaging in any transmittal of funds — conventional money or convertible virtual currency — to or from MEXC Global and its named affiliates.

The order was made under **Section 9714(a) of the Combating Russian Money Laundering Act**, a Russia-focused anti-money-laundering law. CoinDesk reported that MEXC is the largest crypto exchange ever targeted under this provision; the law had previously been used against smaller exchanges. The order is 24 pages long and was signed by FinCEN Deputy Director Jimmy L. Kirby, according to reporting on the document.

Three things about the order are easy to get wrong, so they are worth stating plainly.

**First, it is not a shutdown.** The order does not close MEXC, freeze its wallets, or tell users to withdraw their funds. It cuts off the exchange's access to the US banking system: American banks and other covered institutions may not send or receive money on MEXC's behalf once the order takes effect. MEXC said on 7 November that its platform is operating normally and that user assets are secure.

**Second, it is not an OFAC sanctions designation.** MEXC was not added to the US Treasury's Specially Designated Nationals (SDN) list, no property has been blocked, and the order does not rewrite Bank Secrecy Act duties or OFAC rules. It is a FinCEN funds-transmittal ban, not a sanctions blacklisting. That distinction matters because a sanctions designation would have far broader consequences for anyone dealing with the exchange anywhere in the world; this order binds US financial institutions specifically.

**Third, it has not taken effect yet.** The ban begins **30 days after the order is published in the Federal Register**. As of the morning of 7 November, the Federal Register's public index showed no MEXC document — FinCEN's own cover note says publication is pending — so the 30-day clock has not started and no calendar date for the ban to bite can be fixed yet. The order also has no scheduled end date: once in force, it stays in force until FinCEN says otherwise, and violations may result in civil or criminal penalties.

## The $16 Billion Figure, Explained

The headline number is the part of the story that needs the most care, because it is a government allegation, not a court finding. FinCEN says its analysis of public and non-public information, including blockchain data, linked at least **$16 billion** in transactions through MEXC between May 2021 and May 2026 to sanctions evasion, cybercrime and other illicit activity. Some reports put the figure at **$16.7 billion**. Either way, it is an allegation of illicit flows *through* the exchange over five years — not a claim that MEXC itself pocketed $16 billion, and not a criminal conviction or a judicial judgment against the company.

The Treasury's breakdown, as reported by CoinDesk, Reuters, CoinTelegraph and others, has three main parts:

| Alleged flow | Amount | Period |
|---|---|---|
| Total transactions linked to sanctions evasion, cybercrime and other illicit activity | At least $16 billion (~₹1.5 lakh crore) | May 2021 – May 2026 |
| Of which: linked to Russian sanctions evasion, including about $232 million with sanctioned entities | Nearly $1.5 billion (~₹14,100 crore) | May 2021 – May 2026 |
| Of which: direct transactions with more than 40 identified Iranian crypto exchanges | About $1 billion (~₹9,400 crore) | May 2021 – May 2026 |
| Of which: linked to Iran's Central Bank and the IRGC's Qods Force | About $22 million (~₹207 crore) | May 2021 – May 2026 |

Two further details in the order stand out. First, FinCEN says MEXC continued processing transactions with **Garantex** — a Russian exchange designated by OFAC in 2022 — and its successor **Grinex** as recently as June 2026, more than four years after the Garantex designation. Second, the order covers not only MEXC Global itself but named affiliates operating outside the United States, including the Seychelles-incorporated MEXC Global Ltd, Estonia's MEXC Estonia OÜ, Switzerland's GIDA Technology AG and the UK's MXC Global Ltd, plus their successors.

For scale, the Treasury said MEXC was founded in 2018, is incorporated in Seychelles, operates in more than 170 countries, and ranks among the world's 20 largest convertible virtual currency exchanges. As of September 2026 it processed more than **$1 billion in daily transactions** — roughly ₹9,400 crore a day — and held about **$5 billion in reserves**, roughly ₹47,000 crore. DefiLlama put the exchange's total assets at about $4.48 billion as of 7 November. The $16 billion in alleged illicit flows is therefore spread across five years of very large total volume — which is precisely why the Treasury's case turns on the *share* of flows it says were illicit, and on the specific sanctioned counterparties it names, rather than on the size of the exchange alone.

## Operation Economic Outcast: Why This Order, Why Now

The MEXC order did not come out of nowhere. The Treasury framed it as part of **Operation Economic Outcast**, a campaign launched in August 2026 to cut off financial entities that help Iran evade sanctions. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the action was intended to prevent sanctioned entities from using digital assets to bypass US financial restrictions.

That framing explains the legal tool. Section 9714 can only be used against foreign institutions tied to Russian illicit finance, which is why the order's formal finding is about Russian illicit finance even though the alleged flows also involve Iran and North Korea. The Treasury's announcement ties the three together: sanctioned groups in Iran, Russia and North Korea are alleged to have used MEXC as a channel, and the order is presented as one move in a wider campaign against sanctions evasion through crypto.

The timing also matters. MEXC announced last month — October 2026 — that it would stop serving users in Russia and Belarus. That decision came before the FinCEN order, and it did not prevent the order: FinCEN's findings cover the period from May 2021 to May 2026, and the order cites continued dealings with Garantex and Grinex as recently as June 2026. A platform's decision to exit a market does not erase the transactions that happened while it was there.

There is also a wider pattern this week. On 6–7 November, reporting emerged that Binance had emailed customers of its French entity to say their accounts would close on 31 December 2026, with monthly fees of 0.025% of balances — minimum 10 USDC — starting from 29 November, after Binance failed to win authorisation under the EU's Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) rules. CoinDesk reported on 7 November that users in Spain and Poland had received similar notices. The two stories are different — a US enforcement order against MEXC, and a European regulatory wind-down at Binance France — but they point in the same direction: in November 2026, large exchanges are facing hard deadlines from regulators in the world's biggest markets, and the consequences land on ordinary account holders in the form of closures, fees and withdrawal deadlines.

## What MEXC Has Said

MEXC responded overnight on 7 November 2026, saying it had noted the order and that its legal and compliance teams are reviewing it. The exchange said its platform is operating normally, user assets are secure, and it has launched an independent examination of its anti-money-laundering and sanctions controls. It also said it plans constructive communication with FinCEN and other regulators, and that it will take the allegations seriously.

That response is worth reading carefully for what it does and does not say. It does not dispute any specific figure in the order, and it does not claim the order is wrong. It says the exchange is reviewing the order and its controls — which is what a company in this position would be expected to say while its lawyers assess a 24-page government finding. It also confirms the practical point that matters most to users right now: the platform continues to operate, because the order's ban on US institutions has not yet taken effect.

What happens next depends on two things neither side controls yet. The first is the Federal Register: until the order is published there, the 30-day clock has not started. The second is MEXC's own review: whether the exchange challenges the findings, negotiates with FinCEN, or changes its controls in a way that satisfies the Treasury. None of that has happened yet, and none of it can be predicted from the information available today.

## What This Means for Indian Users

The FinCEN order binds only US financial institutions, so it does not change what an Indian resident can legally do, and it does not order Indian users to move their funds. But it matters for Indian users for four practical reasons.

**1. MEXC is an offshore exchange, and offshore exchanges are under scrutiny in India too.** MEXC operates in more than 170 countries and is one of the world's 20 largest exchanges by trading volume — which is why Indian traders watch it. In the same week as the FinCEN order, India's Enforcement Directorate searched three premises in Bengaluru linked to CoinDCX and Delta Exchange, alleging that Indian users' rupee deposits were converted into USDT and routed to offshore exchanges such as Binance, KuCoin, Gate.io and Bybit, in breach of FEMA. The allegations are unproven and both companies dispute them, but the direction is clear: Indian regulators are actively investigating whether Indian users' trades are being routed offshore. The MEXC order is a different regulator, in a different country, making a different kind of allegation — but it is the same underlying issue: regulators want to see crypto activity, and they are acting against platforms they say move it out of sight.

**2. Your tax obligations do not depend on the platform.** Whether you trade on an Indian exchange or an offshore one, Indian law still applies to you. Gains on crypto are taxed at a flat 30% plus a 4% cess — 31.2% in total — with no set-off of losses, and every sale attracts 1% TDS. Those rules follow the Indian resident, not the exchange. Using an offshore platform does not change what you owe, and it does not remove the need to report crypto income when you file your return.

Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

**3. Check whether your exchange is FIU-IND registered.** Indian exchanges must register with the Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU-IND) and follow know-your-customer and anti-money-laundering rules. Offshore exchanges that serve Indian users without that registration sit outside the framework Indian regulators can see — which is exactly the gap the ED's recent searches are about. Before using any platform, check whether it is FIU-IND registered, read its terms on withdrawals and account freezes, and keep your own records of deposits, trades and withdrawals. If a platform will not tell you plainly how it handles your funds, that is information in itself.

**4. Exchange risk is real, and it is not only about hacks.** Indian readers have seen a run of exchange stories in the last two weeks: the Binance security incident and its $6 billion-plus aftermath, the ED searches at CoinDCX and Delta Exchange, the CERT-In warning about AI-assisted attacks on crypto exchanges, and now the FinCEN order against MEXC and the Binance France wind-down. Hacks get the headlines, but regulatory action can change what an exchange can do just as suddenly — closing accounts, charging fees on leftover balances, or cutting off banking access. No exchange, Indian or offshore, can guarantee that its regulatory position will never change. That is why the standard advice from Indian regulators and from this site is the same: use registered exchanges, keep records, and do not leave on an exchange more than you are prepared to have tied up while a dispute or an investigation runs its course.

## What to Watch Next

Four things matter from here. **The Federal Register** is the first: when the order is published, the 30-day clock starts, and the effective date becomes fixed. Until then, the ban is announced but not yet in force. **MEXC's review** is the second: the exchange has said it is reviewing the order and its controls, and any formal response — a challenge, a settlement, or a compliance overhaul — will shape what the order means in practice. **Other exchanges** are the third: FinCEN used Section 9714 against MEXC as the largest exchange targeted under the provision, and the question now is whether the same tool is used against other platforms with similar exposure. **India's own investigations** are the fourth and most important for Indian readers: whether the ED's FEMA cases against CoinDCX, Delta Exchange and others lead to new rules for Indian exchanges, and whether the government clarifies its long-pending position on crypto regulation.

The Binance France story is worth watching alongside these. Accounts there close on 31 December 2026, and the monthly fee clock starts on 29 November — so European users have a three-week window to withdraw before fees begin. It is a concrete example of what a regulatory deadline looks like for ordinary account holders, and it is the closest parallel to the MEXC order's 30-day clock.

## The Bottom Line

On 6 November 2026, the US Treasury's FinCEN ordered American financial institutions to stop moving money to or from MEXC Global, alleging at least $16 billion in transactions linked to sanctions evasion, cybercrime and other illicit activity between May 2021 and May 2026 — including nearly $1.5 billion tied to Russian sanctions evasion, about $1 billion in direct dealings with Iranian exchanges, and continued transactions with the sanctioned Garantex exchange and its successor as recently as June 2026. The order, made under Section 9714 of the Combating Russian Money Laundering Act as part of Operation Economic Outcast, takes effect 30 days after publication in the Federal Register, which had not happened as of 7 November. It is not a shutdown and not an OFAC sanctions designation, and MEXC says it is operating normally while it reviews the order and its controls.

For Indian users, the order does not change the law — but it is a reminder of how the law works. Offshore platforms can face sudden action in major jurisdictions, Indian regulators are investigating offshore routing under FEMA, and your tax obligations follow you regardless of which exchange you use. Trade on FIU-IND-registered exchanges, keep proper records, and treat any exchange — Indian or offshore — as a service that can change its rules, not as a safe deposit box.
