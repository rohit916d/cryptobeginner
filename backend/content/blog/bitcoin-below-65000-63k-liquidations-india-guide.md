Bitcoin below $65,000 is the headline Indian crypto readers woke up to on Sunday 15 November 2026. On Saturday 14 November, Bitcoin fell to $63,320 — its lowest price since mid-August, and the first time it had traded below $65,000 since 19 August — as more than $1.9 billion in leveraged crypto positions was liquidated in 24 hours. Ether fell below $1,800, the total crypto market lost about 6% in a day to roughly $2.3 trillion, and the Fear & Greed index dropped to 13, deep in extreme fear. This guide explains what actually happened, how a liquidation cascade works, why the move was so fast on a Saturday, and what Indian readers should — and should not — take from it.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a market event; it does not recommend buying, selling or holding any asset.

## What Happened: The Facts at a Glance

The facts, as reported on 14–15 November 2026 by TokenPost, Crypto Briefing, Blockchain.News, CosAnalyst and UrduPoint, all drawing on the same market data, are:

- **Bitcoin fell to $63,320 on Saturday 14 November.** TokenPost and Crypto Briefing both report the low, and both describe it as the lowest since mid-August. CosAnalyst, citing Coinbase data, puts the 24-hour low at $63,270, with Bitcoin trading near $63,617 by about 2:15 PM Pacific Time on Saturday. UrduPoint reports Bitcoin at $64,733.68 on 15 November, down about 6% from a day earlier, and notes this is the first time the price has been below $65,000 since 19 August.
- **The break came in stages, then accelerated.** Bitcoin had already dropped below $69,000 on Friday. On Saturday it slipped under $65,000 at around 1 PM Pacific Time, then broke $64,000, and kept falling. The speed matters: more than $360 million in positions was liquidated in five minutes, according to Watcher.Guru, and about $691 million was liquidated in a single hour, according to CoinGlass data cited by AiCoin.
- **Liquidations passed $1.9 billion in 24 hours.** TokenPost reports more than $1.9 billion liquidated over the prior 24 hours, with bullish (long) positions making up roughly 93% of the total — about $1.8 billion — against about $123.5 million in short positions. A second TokenPost report puts the figure at $1.93 billion. About $925 million of it came in the final four hours of the move, almost all of it bullish bets.
- **The pain was concentrated on two venues.** Binance accounted for about $758 million in liquidations during the period, and Hyperliquid about $543 million, according to TokenPost. The largest single liquidation was a $16.17 million ETHUSDT long position on Binance.
- **Almost everything fell, not just Bitcoin.** Ether dropped below $1,800, trading at about $1,749–$1,766, down roughly 9–10% in 24 hours. BNB lost about 9%, XRP fell about 11.5% and Solana declined about 12%. TokenPost's snapshot of the 89 largest cryptocurrencies (excluding stablecoins) found 81 of them down over 24 hours, and only eight higher. The total crypto market capitalisation shed nearly 6% in a day to about $2.3 trillion, and The Kobeissi Letter put the market value erased over the week at roughly $330 billion.
- **Sentiment hit extreme fear.** The Crypto Fear & Greed Index fell to 13 on Saturday, down from 20 the day before, according to TokenPost. The index had already been at 13 on 14 November, the level this site's 200-week moving average post reported that morning — the reading has now persisted through a deeper fall.
- **ETF outflows stretched to seven straight sessions.** US spot Bitcoin ETFs saw about $185.45 million in net outflows on Friday, the seventh consecutive session of withdrawals, with about $1.91 billion pulled out since 5 November, according to SoSoValue data reported by Investing.com. One market note put two-week outflows at $2.35 billion and said BlackRock's IBIT had extended its redemption streak to 11 sessions.

## What a Liquidation Cascade Actually Is

For a beginner, the most important thing to understand about Saturday is that the $1.9 billion was not $1.9 billion of Bitcoin being sold by ordinary holders. It was leveraged positions being closed by force, and the forcing is what made the fall so fast.

A leveraged long works like this. A trader puts up, say, $1,000 of their own money and borrows more from the exchange to control a much larger position — $10,000, for example. If the price rises, the gains are multiplied. But if the price falls, the losses are multiplied too, and the exchange will not wait for the trader to run out of money. Once the position's losses approach the trader's own $1,000, the exchange automatically sells the position to recover the loan. That automatic sale is a liquidation.

A cascade starts when liquidations themselves move the price. The exchange's forced sale pushes the price down a little. That lower price pushes the next group of leveraged longs past their own liquidation point, so their positions are sold too, pushing the price down further. Each round feeds the next. That is why the numbers on Saturday came in bursts — $360 million in five minutes, $691 million in one hour, $925 million in four hours — rather than as a steady drip. The cascade is a mechanical chain reaction in the leverage system, and it can run while the underlying asset has had no new fundamental news at all.

Two features of Saturday's numbers show the cascade clearly. First, the split: about 93% of the liquidated positions were longs, people betting the price would rise, against only about $123.5 million in shorts. When a market falls, it is the longs who get liquidated; when it rises, it is the shorts. A 93–7 split is the signature of a one-directional fall, not a two-sided fight. Second, the concentration: Binance and Hyperliquid together accounted for about $1.3 billion of the $1.9 billion. Liquidations cluster where the leverage is, and on Saturday the leverage was on the big perpetual-futures venues.

It is worth saying plainly what a cascade is not. It is not evidence that Bitcoin is broken, and it is not a verdict on where the price goes next. Cascades have happened in every major crypto sell-off, including the one on 29 October that this site explained in its liquidation-cascade guide, and they have happened in traditional markets too. They describe how leveraged traders lose money when a move accelerates. They say nothing about whether the asset is cheap or expensive at the new price.

## Why Saturday Moved So Fast

Three things combined to make Saturday's fall faster than the falls earlier in the week, and all three are visible in the numbers reported on the day.

**First, the weekend thins the market out.** Saturday afternoon in the United States is the quietest part of the crypto week: American stock markets are closed, many professional trading desks are staffed down, and the buyers who would normally absorb selling are fewer. The same amount of forced selling moves the price further when there are fewer orders on the other side. That is why the worst of the cascade — $925 million in the final four hours — happened on a Saturday, not on a weekday. Weekend moves are not more meaningful than weekday moves; they are often just faster, because the market is thinner.

**Second, the week had already loaded the spring.** Bitcoin had fallen more than 20% since early October, according to CoinDesk, and had already broken below $70,000 on Thursday and below its 200-week moving average on Friday. Each of those breaks put a new group of leveraged longs underwater. By Saturday, the market was full of positions that were close to their liquidation points, so a relatively small further fall was enough to set off a large cascade. The $360 million liquidated in five minutes is the clearest sign of this: five minutes of selling does not create that much damage unless a lot of positions were already standing right at the edge.

**Third, the backdrop gave sellers a story to sell into.** Analysts quoted in the day's coverage pointed to several overlapping pressures, and it is worth listing them with the same caution the coverage itself used. CoinDesk's 13 November report linked the slide to the hot October inflation print, which lifted the odds of a December Federal Reserve rate rise to around 90%, to the turmoil in technology stocks after Nvidia's chief executive resigned, and to investors raising cash for Anthropic's heavily oversubscribed IPO — while also reporting that analysts are split on whether the IPO link is real, with some seeing a broader move out of riskier assets instead. A market note cited by Crypto Briefing added 5.139% US Treasury yields and renewed whale selling, and quoted CoinShares' James Butterfill warning that tighter monetary policy, whale selling and weaker technology-sector sentiment could limit Bitcoin's upside. None of these is a proven cause of Saturday's specific move, and the coverage itself is careful on that point: the same reports that name the drivers also note that the evidence does not prove any single one caused the fall. What they show is that the market entered the weekend with several reasons to be nervous at once, and a cascade does not need a single cause — it needs a push, and then it supplies its own momentum.

## The ETF Outflow Streak: What It Is and Is Not

The second number worth understanding is the $1.91 billion in ETF outflows since 5 November, with seven straight sessions of net withdrawals. It is a real and significant figure, but it is easy to misread.

A spot Bitcoin ETF is a fund that holds actual Bitcoin and issues shares that trade on a stock exchange. When investors sell more shares than they buy, the fund redeems shares and sells some of its Bitcoin, and the flow is recorded as an outflow. When they buy more than they sell, the fund buys Bitcoin, and the flow is an inflow. The $185.45 million outflow on Friday, and the roughly $1.91 billion over seven sessions, mean that more money left these funds than entered them during that stretch.

What the streak does not mean is that institutions, as a group, have decided Bitcoin is finished. ETF flows are dominated by traders who move in and out quickly, and the same funds that are seeing outflows now took in very large amounts earlier in the year. Seven sessions is a short window, and flows can reverse without warning — they have done so repeatedly in 2026. The outflow streak is best read as one more measure of the same mood that produced the cascade: buyers stepped back, sellers stepped forward, and the funds reflected that. It is a description of the past week, not a forecast of the next one.

There is one useful comparison. The outflow streak and the liquidation cascade are two different kinds of selling. ETF outflows are mostly unleveraged: an investor sells shares, the fund sells Bitcoin, and the trade is done. Liquidations are leveraged: a trader's position is closed by force, and the forced sale can trigger the next one. That is why the cascade moved the price so much faster than the ETF flows did, even though both were happening at the same time. The ETF number tells you about demand over days; the liquidation number tells you about leverage over minutes.

## What This Means for India

For Indian readers, the price translates directly into rupees. At about $63,320–$64,700, one Bitcoin costs roughly ₹60–61 lakh at the day's exchange rate — TokenTimes put it at ₹60,92,624 when Bitcoin was at $64,616, and Gadgets 360 listed about ₹60,03,752. That is down from about ₹71–75 lakh when Bitcoin was near $75,000 in early October. For someone who bought earlier in the year, the holding is worth materially less in rupees than it was a few weeks ago, and that loss is real even though no coins have moved.

Three India-specific points follow from the facts:

- **The tax does not change with the price.** Gains on virtual digital assets are taxed at a flat 30% plus a 4% cess, with 1% TDS on transfers, and those rates apply whether the market is rising or falling. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. A fall does not create a tax refund, and selling at a loss does not cancel tax owed on other gains — the rules treat each transfer on its own terms, and losses on crypto cannot generally be set off against other income.
- **Leverage is where Indian users get hurt fastest.** The $1.9 billion in liquidations is a warning about borrowed money, not about holding. Indian exchanges offer leveraged products, and the mechanics are identical to the ones described above: a small move against a leveraged position can close it automatically, and a cascade can close it at a worse price than the trader expected. The traders who lost the most on Saturday were not the ones who owned Bitcoin outright — they were the ones who had borrowed to bet on it rising.
- **The exchange outages are a separate, live problem.** The same week brought a different kind of risk for Indian users: Coinbase has halted INR withdrawals in India for nearly a week because of an outage at its banking partner, Unocoin has suspended all services since 9 November after a security breach, and ZebPay has warned of delays in INR withdrawals. This site covered the Coinbase halt on 14 November and the Unocoin breach on 12 November. Those are operational problems — money stuck in transit, services suspended — and they are unrelated to the price fall, but they are happening at the same time, and they are the more practical worry for an Indian user who needs to move rupees right now.

## What Indian Readers Should Do — and Not Do

This is an educational moment, not a trading moment. A few practical steps follow from what is actually known:

1. **Do not treat $65,000 as a magic line.** Round numbers feel meaningful, but the market does not respect them. Bitcoin broke $70,000 on Thursday, its 200-week average on Friday, and $65,000 and $64,000 on Saturday. Each break was reported as a milestone, and each was followed by another one. A level being broken tells you what already happened. It does not tell you where the price stops, and buying or selling purely because a round number gave way is trading on a headline, not on an analysis.
2. **If you use leverage, understand the liquidation price before you open the position.** Every leveraged position has a price at which the exchange will close it automatically, and that price is shown before you trade. The $1.9 billion in liquidations is the sum of thousands of traders who either did not know that price or assumed the market would not reach it. Weekend thin liquidity makes the risk worse, not better.
3. **Check your own exposure, not the market's.** The useful question after a fall like this is not "will it go lower" — nobody in the coverage claims to know — but "how much of my money is in this, and can I afford the loss if it falls further?" That is a personal question about your own finances, and it is the only one with a reliable answer.
4. **Do not confuse the cascade with the asset.** The forced selling of leveraged positions is a feature of the leverage market, not of Bitcoin. The network kept running through the whole move: blocks kept coming about every 10 minutes, and the mining difficulty cut reported on this site on 15 November — the 9.13% retarget — is a separate, mechanical event. A market can fall hard while the underlying network works exactly as designed.
5. **Watch the next weekday session, not the next headline.** The cascade happened on a Saturday, when markets were thin. The next real test is when US markets reopen on Monday 16 November, when fresh ETF flow data will show whether the selling is easing or accelerating, and when the week's data — US retail sales on Tuesday, Nvidia's earnings on Tuesday, and the Federal Reserve's meeting minutes on Wednesday — will give the market new information to react to. None of those events is a prediction; they are simply the next scheduled moments when the market will have something concrete to price in.

## What This Does Not Mean

Three things are worth stating plainly, because they are easy to get wrong when a big number flashes across the news:

- **This is not a signal that Bitcoin is going to zero.** Bitcoin has fallen more than 20% since early October and more than 6% in a single day, and it has done so before — most recently in the same week, at higher levels. A fall to $63,320 is a large move, but it is within the range of falls Bitcoin has recovered from in past cycles, and it is also within the range of falls that have been followed by further declines. Neither history is a prediction.
- **This is not a signal that Bitcoin is cheap.** "Extreme fear" at 13 on the Fear & Greed index describes sentiment, not value. The same coverage that reports the fear also reports that analysts are split on the causes of the fall, and that the ETF outflows and the liquidation cascade are still running. A market can stay fearful for a long time, and prices can keep falling after a cascade, as they did repeatedly this week.
- **This is not the same story as the mining difficulty cut.** The difficulty retarget reported on this site earlier on 15 November is about miners and computing power. This post is about the price and leveraged traders. The two are connected — the price fall put miners under pressure, which is part of why the difficulty fell — but they are different mechanisms, and neither one predicts the other.
