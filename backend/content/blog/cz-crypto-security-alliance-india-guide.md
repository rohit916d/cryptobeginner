A crypto security alliance is the latest idea to emerge from the largest crypto theft on record. In his first interviews since the 29 October 2026 Binance hack, Binance founder Changpeng Zhao, widely known as CZ, has called on crypto companies to stop defending themselves in isolation and instead form an industry-wide alliance on security. The proposal has been reported by several crypto publications between 3 and 4 November 2026, and it has already drawn both support in principle and sharp criticism of its messaging. This guide explains what CZ actually proposed, why he says artificial intelligence has changed the threat, what the alliance would not do, and what — if anything — it means for users in India.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a security proposal and its background; it does not recommend any exchange, coin, or product, and it does not predict prices.

## What CZ Actually Proposed

The core of the proposal is simple to state, even though building it would be hard. In interviews on the Talking Tokens podcast, with Yahoo Finance, and with CoinDesk, all published around 3 November 2026, CZ argued that crypto companies should cooperate on security in four specific ways:

- **Share lessons from incidents.** When one company is attacked, others should learn what went wrong quickly, rather than each company discovering the same attack method separately.
- **Share threat intelligence.** Companies should pass on indicators of attacks — such as suspicious infrastructure, fake recruiter profiles, or malicious files — so others can block them before they are hit.
- **Run cross-audits.** Companies should audit each other's security practices, so weaknesses are found by a fresh pair of eyes rather than only by the team that built the system.
- **Freeze stolen funds faster.** Exchanges, stablecoin issuers, and law enforcement should coordinate more quickly when stolen assets move, because the first hours after a theft are when freezing is most likely to work.

CZ also argued that companies need to invest more heavily in their own defences: AI-based defensive tools, more security staff, and partnerships with specialist AI security firms. The alliance is the collective half of that argument — better individual defences plus faster collective response. No alliance has been launched yet: no member list, launch date, or formal structure has been announced.

## Why Now: The AI Argument

The reason CZ gives for the proposal is that the attacker's toolkit has changed. His argument, as reported across the interviews, runs like this: artificial intelligence is making sophisticated attacks cheaper and faster to run, so the old pattern — in which a major platform might face a serious incident roughly every few months — is giving way to a world in which several platforms can be hit in a single day.

That argument connects directly to how the Binance attack unfolded, according to Binance's own first detailed incident report, published on 4 November 2026 and covered in our earlier guide. Attackers reportedly compromised an employee's computer through a fake job interview, then used an internal AI tool to move laterally inside Binance's systems before reaching the wallet-signing service on 29 October. Around $6.3 billion in digital assets was drained from hot wallets, in what has been described as the largest recorded crypto theft. The US Federal Bureau of Investigation has attributed the attack to North Korea's TraderTraitor group, an attribution North Korea has publicly denied.

CZ's second argument is about human nature rather than technology. Companies that go a long time without being hacked, he warned, can grow complacent — treating security as a box that was ticked long ago rather than as an ongoing discipline. A quiet stretch, in this view, is not evidence of safety; it can be the very thing that lets defences decay. That point is relevant well beyond Binance: every exchange, wallet provider, and crypto company that has enjoyed a calm period faces the same temptation to under-invest in security.

The AI argument also has a defensive side. If attackers use AI to find weaknesses faster, defenders need AI to find and fix weaknesses faster too. CZ noted, however, that access to the most capable defensive tools is uneven: large companies can afford them, smaller ones often cannot. An alliance that shares tools and findings could, in theory, narrow that gap.

## What the Alliance Would Not Do

Enthusiasm for a proposal is not the same as understanding its limits, and this one has several that beginners should keep in mind.

**It would not prevent hacks.** No alliance, audit programme, or intelligence-sharing arrangement can make any system unhackable. Attackers need only one opening; defenders must close all of them. The Binance attack illustrates the point: it reportedly combined social engineering, a tampered software download, and abuse of an internal tool over several months.

**It would not recover funds by itself.** Faster coordination can help freeze stolen assets, and Binance has credited stablecoin issuers, other exchanges, cybersecurity firms, and law enforcement with helping to freeze or intercept a large share of the stolen funds. But freezing depends on where the assets go, how quickly they move, and whether the destination will cooperate. Some funds are typically lost even in well-coordinated responses, and recovery is never guaranteed.

**It would not replace regulation or user responsibility.** An industry alliance is a voluntary arrangement between companies. It does not set law, it does not license exchanges, and it does not change what any exchange owes its users. In India, that distinction matters: an Indian user's protections come from Indian rules — including Financial Intelligence Unit registration for exchanges serving Indian users — not from a voluntary industry body headquartered elsewhere.

**It would not settle who is to blame.** The incident has already produced a public attribution dispute, with the FBI blaming North Korea's TraderTraitor group and North Korea denying the claim. An alliance focused on future defence would not resolve that dispute.

## The Disagreement: Even Rivals Argue About the Message

The proposal has not landed unchallenged. On 4 November 2026, OKX founder and chief executive Star Xu publicly criticised CZ's public response to the hack, including reported comments comparing the security of banks and crypto. Xu wrote that he did not want to criticise a competitor during a crisis, and that what he actually wanted was for companies to work together to raise security standards and better protect the whole industry.

Binance's own leadership has also responded to criticism directly. On 4 November 2026, Binance co-founder He Yi posted that Binance's security department accepts external criticism about its security and permission management, and that any fund gap would be borne fully by Binance, not by users. Binance has separately said it will cover user losses from company funds and its Secure Asset Fund for Users (SAFU).

CZ also addressed the question of government involvement. He said he had not spoken with US President Donald Trump or other US officials about the incident, but that the industry would welcome government assistance. That leaves the alliance, for now, as an industry idea rather than a government-backed programme.

## Why This Matters for Users in India

For Indian users, the alliance debate may feel distant — a proposal by a former chief executive of a global exchange, discussed in podcasts and interviews abroad. But three threads connect it directly to India.

**First, India has lived through its own version of this story.** In July 2024, WazirX suffered a major cyberattack in which about $235 million was taken from a multi-signature wallet managed by a custody provider. Indian users who followed that incident will recognise the same pattern: a sophisticated attack, a long recovery process, and hard questions about custody and disclosure.

**Second, Indian crypto users are specifically targeted.** Security researchers have documented campaigns, including the GhostCall and GhostHire campaigns reported by Kaspersky, that have targeted Web3 and cryptocurrency organisations in India since at least April 2025. Fake job interviews — the very method reportedly used against Binance — are a known tactic in these campaigns. An Indian user approached with an unexpected job offer, a coding assignment, or a software download should treat that approach with the same suspicion the Binance case now warrants.

**Third, India's market structure makes shared defences relevant.** Binance is a major liquidity provider for futures trading in India, and Indian users interact with global platforms alongside Indian exchanges such as CoinDCX, WazirX, and ZebPay. When a global platform is hit, the effects — paused withdrawals, market volatility, and scam attempts riding on the news — reach Indian users quickly, even if their own funds sit elsewhere. A faster industry response to the next incident would, in principle, benefit Indian users too, without changing their Indian legal position or tax obligations.

None of this means Indian users should wait for an alliance to protect them. The protections that matter most are the ones users control themselves, and they are available today.

## What Indian Users Can Do Right Now

The Binance attack method offers a practical checklist, because its early stages targeted people, not code. Here is what to apply:

1. **Treat unexpected job approaches as suspect.** A recruiter who contacts you out of nowhere, moves quickly to a coding assignment, or asks you to download and run unfamiliar software is following a known attack script. Verify the company and the person through independent channels — not through links or contact details they supplied.
2. **Never run code or install software from an unverified source.** A coding test should run in a sandboxed environment, not on a machine that holds your wallets, passwords, or exchange sessions. If an assignment requires disabling security warnings, that is a reason to stop, not to comply.
3. **Use the strongest account protections your exchange offers.** Hardware security keys or authenticator apps are stronger than SMS codes. Withdrawal allowlists — which restrict withdrawals to addresses you have pre-approved — add a delay that can stop a theft even if an attacker gets into your account.
4. **Bookmark official domains and ignore urgent messages.** After a major hack, impersonation scams surge: fake support accounts, phishing pages, and messages claiming your account needs urgent action. No legitimate exchange will ever ask for your seed phrase, your password, or an urgent transfer to a "safe" wallet.
5. **Keep records for tax time.** Gains are taxed at 30%, with 1% TDS on transfers. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. Good records — dates, amounts in INR, and exchange statements — make filing far easier than reconstructing trades from memory.
6. **Check an exchange's Indian standing before trusting it.** Exchanges serving Indian users must register with India's Financial Intelligence Unit. Registration is not a guarantee of security, but an unregistered platform operating in India is a warning sign worth taking seriously.

## What to Watch Next

The alliance proposal will be judged by what happens next, not by how it was announced. Here are the developments that would show it is real:

- **Named members and a structure.** An announcement listing participating companies and a governance model would turn a proposal into a programme. Until then, it remains an idea.
- **A first shared output.** A joint threat report, a published audit standard, or a documented case in which shared intelligence stopped an attack would be concrete evidence that cooperation is happening.
- **A published security audit from Binance.** Binance has indicated that a security audit report will be released. Its contents — and whether independent experts find it credible — will matter more than any interview statement.
- **Whether rivals join or go their own way.** If OKX, other major exchanges, and custody providers sign on, the alliance gains weight. If each company announces its own separate initiative instead, the industry will have cooperation in name but fragmentation in practice.
- **The next incident, whenever it comes.** The honest test of any security alliance is not its launch announcement but its performance under pressure: how fast it shares warnings, how quickly funds are frozen, and how openly members disclose what went wrong.

## The Bottom Line

A crypto security alliance, as proposed by CZ after the Binance hack, is a sensible idea with an unproven future. The logic is sound: attackers now share methods and move fast, so defenders who share intelligence, audit each other, and coordinate freezes should be harder to beat than defenders who work alone. But logic is not delivery. No members have been named, no structure announced, and no results demonstrated — and even a fully functioning alliance could not make any exchange unhackable or guarantee the recovery of stolen funds.

For Indian users, the right response is twofold. Welcome the idea of better industry cooperation, and judge it by its actions over the coming months. In the meantime, rely on the protections that already exist: strong authentication, withdrawal allowlists, suspicion of unexpected job approaches and downloads, verified official channels, and the Indian rules — FIU registration, 30% tax, and 1% TDS — that actually govern your position. The alliance, if it happens, will be a bonus. Your own security habits are the foundation.
