# Crypto Tax Notices in India: Beginner's Guide 2026

A letter or message from the income tax department can make anyone's heart skip a beat — especially when it mentions crypto. For many Indian crypto users, a tax notice is the first time they realise that buying and selling virtual digital assets leaves a trail the tax department can see. The good news is that most notices are not accusations. They are requests for information, corrections, or clarification, and a calm, timely response is usually all that is needed.

This guide explains what crypto tax notices are, why the department sends them, the common types beginners encounter, and exactly how to respond — without panic.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice or tax advice. Tax rules can change, and every person's situation is different. For a notice you have actually received, consider speaking to a chartered accountant or tax professional.

## Why the Tax Department Knows About Your Crypto

Crypto feels private, but in India it is not invisible to the tax system. Several everyday steps create records that the tax department can match against your return:

- **KYC on exchanges.** Indian exchanges registered with FIU-IND collect your PAN and verify your identity before you trade.
- **1% TDS on transfers.** When you sell crypto on an Indian exchange, 1% tax deducted at source (TDS) is usually deducted and reported against your PAN. That TDS appears in your Form 26AS and Annual Information Statement (AIS).
- **Bank trails.** Your INR deposits and withdrawals to and from exchanges pass through your bank account, which is also visible in your AIS.
- **Exchange reporting.** Registered platforms share transaction data with the authorities as part of their compliance duties.

This is not a reason to fear crypto. It simply means crypto activity is part of your financial record, and your income tax return should reflect it. Problems usually start when the return and the department's data do not match.

## Common Reasons Beginners Receive a Notice

Most crypto-related notices trace back to one of a handful of situations:

**1. A mismatch in reported income.** Your AIS shows crypto TDS or exchange activity, but your return does not mention virtual digital assets at all, or the figures differ. The department's systems flag the difference automatically.

**2. Crypto income not reported in Schedule VDA.** Crypto gains in India are reported under Schedule VDA in the income tax return. If you sold crypto and did not fill in this schedule, the department may ask why.

**3. A large unexplained deposit.** A big bank deposit used to buy crypto — or a large withdrawal after a sale — can prompt questions about the source of funds, especially if it does not match your declared income.

**4. TDS credit mismatch.** You claim a TDS credit in your return, but the department's records show a different amount, or the TDS was deducted under a different PAN detail.

**5. A return selected for scrutiny.** Sometimes a return is picked for a closer look for reasons unrelated to crypto, and the crypto entries are simply part of the review.

None of these automatically means a penalty is coming. In many cases the department is giving you a chance to explain or correct the record.

## The Main Types of Notices You Might See

Notices come in different forms, and the type tells you what the department wants. The most common ones beginners encounter are:

| Notice type | What it usually means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Intimation (Section 143(1)) | An automated check of your return — a calculation difference or a mismatch the system spotted. | Compare it with your return; if you agree, pay any demand or accept the adjustment; if not, respond online. |
| Inquiry (Section 142(1)) | The department needs more information or documents about something in your return. | Provide exactly what is asked, before the deadline. |
| Defective return (Section 139(9)) | Something is missing or wrong in how the return was filed — for example, Schedule VDA left blank when crypto gains exist. | Correct and re-file the return within the time given. |
| Reassessment (Section 148) | The department believes some income may have escaped assessment in an earlier year. | Read it carefully and respond with records; professional help is wise here. |
| Demand notice (Section 156) | A tax amount is due after an assessment or adjustment. | Check the calculation, then pay or challenge it through the proper channel. |

You do not need to memorise these sections. What matters is reading the notice itself: it states the assessment year, the issue, and the date by which you must respond.

## How to Respond: A Step-by-Step Approach

When a notice arrives, follow these steps in order. Do not ignore it — deadlines in tax notices are real, and missing one can turn a small issue into a penalty.

**Step 1: Verify the notice is genuine.** Log in to the official income tax e-filing portal and check the notice under the "e-Proceedings" or "Pending Actions" section. A genuine notice will appear there. Never click links in emails, SMS messages, or WhatsApp forwards claiming to be from the tax department, and never pay anyone who contacts you about a notice through unofficial channels.

**Step 2: Read it fully and note the deadline.** Find the assessment year, the section under which it was issued, what information is being asked for, and the last date to respond. Write the deadline down immediately.

**Step 3: Gather your records.** Collect the documents that relate to the issue: exchange transaction statements, bank statements showing deposits and withdrawals, your Form 26AS and AIS, and the copy of the return you filed. If you have kept per-transaction records — dates, amounts in INR, and TDS deducted — this step is much faster.

**Step 4: Compare your records with the department's data.** Look at what the notice says versus what your records show. Often the gap is a simple error: a missed entry, a wrong figure, or a TDS amount claimed in the wrong year.

**Step 5: Respond on the portal before the deadline.** Most notices can be answered online through the e-filing portal. Keep your reply factual and attach only the documents asked for. Save a copy of everything you submit.

**Step 6: Get professional help when needed.** If the notice involves reassessment, a large amount, or anything you do not understand, a chartered accountant is worth the fee. A wrong reply can be more expensive than the advice.

## What Happens If You Ignore a Notice?

Ignoring a tax notice is the one response that reliably makes things worse. If you miss the deadline, the department may proceed on the basis of the information it already has, raise a demand, or levy interest and penalties. In serious cases of non-compliance, further proceedings can follow.

The practical point is simple: a notice is a conversation, and the department expects a reply. Even a short, honest response with the right documents is far better than silence. If you genuinely cannot meet a deadline, many notices allow you to request more time through the portal — but ask before the deadline, not after.

## Crypto Tax Rules Behind the Notices

Understanding the basic rules helps you see why notices arise. In India, gains from transferring virtual digital assets are taxed at a flat 30% rate, and a 1% TDS generally applies to transfers above a threshold. Losses from crypto cannot be set off against other income or carried forward to later years.

Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

That is why accurate records matter so much. If your exchange statements, your TDS credits, and your Schedule VDA entries all tell the same story, most mismatches never happen. Keep a simple record for every trade: date, crypto and quantity, INR value at the time, fees, and TDS deducted. Save exchange statements and bank records for several years.

## Fake Tax Notices: A Scam to Watch For

A real tax notice arrives through official channels. A fake one often arrives as an urgent email, SMS, or WhatsApp message claiming your crypto is under investigation, your account will be frozen, or you must pay immediately through a link or a UPI ID. The message may use the income tax department's name and logo to look convincing.

Remember these rules:

- The income tax department does not ask for payments through WhatsApp, Telegram, or personal UPI IDs.
- It does not threaten immediate arrest over the phone or in a chat message.
- Any genuine notice can be verified by logging in to the official e-filing portal yourself — never through a link in the message.

If you receive a suspicious message, do not click, do not pay, and do not share personal details. You can report it through the cybercrime portal at cybercrime.gov.in or the helpline 1930.

## A Simple Routine to Avoid Notices

You cannot guarantee you will never receive a notice, but a small routine makes them far less likely — and far easier to answer:

1. **Report every sale.** File crypto gains in Schedule VDA every year you sell, even if the amount is small.
2. **Match your TDS.** Check your Form 26AS and AIS against your exchange statements before filing.
3. **Keep records as you go.** Note each trade when it happens, not months later at filing time.
4. **File on time.** Late or rushed returns are where mistakes creep in.
5. **Use registered platforms.** Trading on FIU-IND registered exchanges keeps your TDS and records clean.

## The Bottom Line

A crypto tax notice is usually a question, not a punishment. The tax department can see your exchange activity, your TDS, and your bank trails — so the safest approach is to report your crypto activity honestly, keep good records, and respond to any notice calmly and on time through the official portal. If a notice is complicated or the amount is large, a tax professional can help you answer it correctly.

The goal is not to fear the notice system, but to stay organised enough that a notice is a minor task instead of a crisis. File accurately, keep your records, and verify anything suspicious on the official portal before you act.
