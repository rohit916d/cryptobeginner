On 16 November 2026, the United States' derivatives regulator did something it had never done before: it approved a crypto firm to run a regulated futures market on blockchain rails. The CFTC approval of Backpack's blockchain derivatives exchange — and, days earlier, of tZERO's — gives two companies the licences to operate derivatives exchanges and clearinghouses in the US that execute and settle trades using blockchain technology. For Indian readers the licences change nothing directly. The venues are US-only, neither has a launch date yet, and no Indian user can sign up. But the approvals are the clearest sign so far of where American crypto regulation is heading: not around the existing rules, but through them.

## What Happened: The Facts at a Glance

The story broke on Monday 16 November 2026, when the Commodity Futures Trading Commission — the CFTC, the US regulator for futures and derivatives markets — issued a statement from its chairman, Michael Selig, and the exchange Backpack announced its own approvals on the same day. The facts, as reported on 16–17 November 2026 by The Block, Coinotag, GazeWire, NEXTPredict and The Crypto Guide, are:

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| What was approved | Regulated derivatives exchanges and clearinghouses that use blockchain to execute and settle trades |
| Who approved it | The US Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC), statement by Chair Michael Selig, 16 November 2026 |
| First firm | Backpack, through TLD Markets, Inc. (exchange) and TLD Clearing, Inc. (clearinghouse) |
| Second firm | tZERO, through tZERO DCM, LLC (exchange) and tZERO DCO, LLC (clearinghouse) |
| Backpack's dates | Clearinghouse registered 13 November; exchange approved 16 November; announced 16 November |
| tZERO's dates | Clearinghouse registered 16 October; exchange approved about 10 November; announced 11 November |
| What US users will get | Leveraged crypto perpetual futures trading 24 hours a day, with dollars or crypto as collateral |
| Launch date | None announced by either firm |
| What it does not do | It does not authorise Hyperliquid, Polymarket or any other platform, and it creates no new rules |

The CFTC's statement did not name the firm it had approved. It referred only to "a firm utilizing blockchain-based architecture." Backpack's US president, Mark Wetjen, later confirmed to NEXTPredict that the statement referred to Backpack, and the CFTC's public records show TLD Markets, Inc. receiving its exchange designation on 16 November, three days after TLD Clearing, Inc. was registered as a clearinghouse.

## What the CFTC Actually Approved

To understand why this matters, it helps to know what the two licences are. A designated contract market, or DCM, is the CFTC's licence for a regulated futures exchange — the venue where buy and sell orders meet. A derivatives clearing organization, or DCO, is the licence for a clearinghouse — the entity that stands between the buyer and the seller of every trade, guarantees the trade, holds the margin money and settles the payments. A derivatives business needs both. The exchange is where trading happens; the clearinghouse is what makes sure the trades actually settle and that a default by one trader does not cascade into defaults by others.

Until now, no firm had held both licences for a business that runs its execution and settlement on a blockchain. That is the sense in which the approvals are a first. Selig's statement called them the first exchange and clearinghouse applications approved for a firm using blockchain-based architecture for the execution and settlement of derivatives, and said they "demonstrate that there is a regulated pathway for a new generation of market infrastructure built using blockchain technology." The phrasing matters: the CFTC is saying that blockchain-native infrastructure can operate inside the existing derivatives rulebook, not that it needs a new one.

It is equally important to be clear about what the statement did not do. It did not create new rules or exemptions for blockchain-based exchanges. It did not set out a special framework for crypto derivatives. The firms that won the licences must meet the same standards as traditional exchanges and clearinghouses — the same capital, risk-management, surveillance and reporting requirements. The innovation is in the technology the firms use to run their systems, not in the rules they answer to.

That distinction is the story. For years the crypto industry's argument has been that its technology does not fit the old rulebook and needs a new one. The CFTC's answer, at least under its current chairman, is that the old rulebook works fine — apply for the licences, meet the standards, and you can build on blockchain. Whether other firms follow that route, and whether the CFTC keeps approving them, is the question the next year will answer.

## Backpack: What It Got and What It Will Offer

Backpack is a crypto exchange founded by former FTX and Alameda staff, best known outside the US for its global platform. Its American approvals came as a pair. TLD Clearing, Inc., which does business as Backpack Clearing, was registered as a derivatives clearing organization on 13 November 2026. TLD Markets, Inc., a Delaware corporation based in San Francisco, was approved as a designated contract market on 16 November. Backpack announced both on 16 November, the same day Selig issued his statement.

What Backpack says the licences will let it do is specific. American users will be able to trade leveraged crypto perpetual futures — contracts that track the price of a cryptocurrency without an expiry date, using borrowed money to amplify the position — around the clock, with dollars or crypto accepted as collateral and cross-margining across positions, all in a single order book shared with its global platform. Perpetual futures are the most popular crypto derivatives product in the world, but they have never been legally available to US retail traders on a regulated American venue. Offshore exchanges offer them; American exchanges have been limited to dated futures contracts with fixed expiries.

The announcement gives no date for when US trading starts. Backpack says the timing depends on its implementation calendar, and no launch window has been given. That is a real caveat. A licence is permission, not a product. Building the trading system, passing the CFTC's operational checks, connecting banks and custodians, and onboarding customers all take time, and none of it has been timetabled publicly. The approvals are the starting gun, not the finish line.

There is also a detail worth noting about who Backpack is. The firm was founded by people who worked at FTX, the exchange whose collapse in 2022 defined the last crypto crisis. That history will follow the company into every regulatory conversation it has, and it is part of why the CFTC's willingness to license it at all is significant: the regulator is judging the firm on its current systems and controls, not on its founders' former employer.

## tZERO: The Second Approval

tZERO is the quieter half of the story, and it got there first. The tokenization company registered tZERO DCO, LLC as a derivatives clearing organization on 16 October 2026, and its exchange subsidiary, tZERO DCM, LLC, received designated contract market approval about a week later, with the company announcing it on 11 November. Selig's 16 November statement covered both firms, but tZERO's approvals were already in place when it was issued.

tZERO's business is different from Backpack's. It is a financial-technology company focused on tokenized securities — digital versions of traditional assets such as shares — rather than a crypto exchange. Its new licences let it run a regulated derivatives exchange and clearinghouse on blockchain infrastructure, the same pair Backpack won. The company has also signed a merger agreement with FG Imperii Acquisition Corp., a special-purpose acquisition company, to take tZERO public, and the CFTC approvals are tied to the first earn-out condition of that deal — a financial milestone that pays out only if the approvals land.

That commercial detail is worth understanding because it shows how the approvals are being used. For tZERO, the licences are not only a business in themselves; they are a condition for a public listing. The market is now pricing regulated crypto infrastructure as an asset in its own right, and the CFTC's willingness to grant the licences is what makes that pricing possible.

## Why This Is a First — and Why It Is Not a Free Pass

The approvals land in the middle of a larger American shift toward bringing crypto inside the regulated financial system, and that context is what gives a pair of licences their weight.

In the same month, the US House Oversight Committee published a staff memorandum recommending that Congress consider restricting government officials from trading on prediction markets such as Polymarket and Kalshi, after an investigation that began in May 2026. The NYSE has chosen Avalanche to provide the ledger technology for a tokenized-securities trading venue. Coinbase has received final approval for a federally chartered national trust company. OKX and Intercontinental Exchange are preparing to open a tokenized-securities venue on 23 November. The pattern across all of these is the same: crypto infrastructure is being built inside regulated frameworks, with licences, charters and congressional oversight, rather than outside them.

But the approvals also have hard limits, and the reporting has been careful to spell them out. The CFTC's decision does not authorise Hyperliquid, Polymarket or any other decentralized trading platform to operate in the US, and it does not change their regulatory status. Polymarket remains an offshore platform for American users, and Hyperliquid remains unlicensed in the US. The licences belong to Backpack and tZERO only, and they cover only what those two firms applied for.

The approvals also do not settle the bigger legislative questions. The CLARITY Act, the bill that would divide crypto oversight between the CFTC and the Securities and Exchange Commission, remains stalled in Congress. The GENIUS Act, which regulates stablecoins, takes effect on 18 January 2027. And Selig's own push for voluntary registration of crypto firms — the idea that companies could opt into CFTC oversight before being forced to — is still only a proposal, with a rule proposal targeted for 14 December. The Backpack and tZERO approvals show that the existing licensing route works. They do not answer the question of what the law should say about the firms that never apply.

## What It Means for Indians

For an Indian reader, the honest answer is that the approvals change nothing you can do today — and that is itself the point worth understanding.

India has no equivalent of a DCM or a DCO for crypto. Indian exchanges are registered with FIU-IND as reporting entities under anti-money-laundering rules. That registration is about tracking suspicious transactions, not about licensing a derivatives market. No Indian exchange is licensed to offer crypto derivatives to retail users, and no Indian regulator has created a framework for one. The contrast with the United States is now sharp: America has just licensed its first blockchain-based derivatives exchanges, while India has not licensed any.

The second barrier is at the border. The licences are for US users, and Backpack's American service will require US identity checks. An Indian resident cannot simply open an account. Trading offshore crypto derivatives also runs into FEMA, India's foreign-exchange law, which restricts how residents can move money abroad for speculative purposes. Using a VPN or a foreign address to get around a geo-restriction would breach the exchange's terms of service and could breach Indian law. The approvals do not create a legal route for Indian users to trade American crypto perpetuals.

That matters because the same week has shown how fragile Indian users' access to their own money can be. Coinbase India has halted rupee withdrawals since 9 November after an outage at its banking partner, with no timeline for restoration. Unocoin has suspended all services since 9 November after a security breach, and on 14 November admitted that about $200,000 was stolen from three of its hot wallets. ZebPay paused crypto withdrawals and is targeting 17 November for resumption, with its rupee withdrawals running late because of a technical problem at its own banking partner. Three exchanges, one week, one common thread: Indian crypto access depends on traditional banking rails that can fail without warning.

The American story and the Indian story are two sides of the same coin. In the United States, regulators are building licensed rails for crypto derivatives. In India, users are discovering how much their existing access depends on unlicensed, informal arrangements with banks. Neither story is about price. Both are about plumbing — the pipes through which money moves — and the pipes are being built in one country while they are leaking in the other.

For Indian readers, the practical upshot is simple: your rules have not changed. Gains on crypto transfers are still taxed at a flat 30% plus a 4% cess, sales still attract 1% TDS, exchanges must still register with FIU-IND, and cross-border movement is still governed by FEMA. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. What has changed is the picture of where global regulation is heading: toward licensed, supervised crypto markets, and away from the offshore, unregulated venues where most crypto derivatives trading has happened until now.

## What to Watch Next

Four things will determine how this story develops, and all four are worth watching over the coming weeks.

The first is the launch dates. Neither Backpack nor tZERO has said when American users will actually be able to trade. Watch for the first announcement of a go-live date, and for whether the launch covers retail users or institutions first. A licence with no launch is a press release; a launch is a market.

The second is who follows. The approvals create a template: apply for a DCM and a DCO, meet the same standards as a traditional exchange, build on blockchain. Watch whether other firms — including the prediction-market platforms and the offshore perpetuals exchanges — apply, and whether the CFTC keeps saying yes. If it does, the United States could have a regulated perpetuals market within a year. If it stops at two, the approvals will look like an exception rather than a policy.

The third is the December rule proposal. Selig has targeted 14 December for a proposal on voluntary registration of crypto firms with the CFTC. That proposal, if it appears, would show whether the agency wants to bring more of the industry inside its perimeter — and whether Congress lets it. The CLARITY Act's fate in the Senate is the other half of that question: a law would settle the CFTC's jurisdiction over crypto for good, while a continued stalemate leaves everything to the regulator's discretion.

The fourth is the GENIUS Act. The stablecoin law takes effect on 18 January 2027, and stablecoins are the collateral that Backpack's perpetuals will run on. How the stablecoin rules interact with the new derivatives licences — which stablecoins qualify as margin, how they are custodied, who audits them — will shape what the new venues can actually offer.

## The Bottom Line

On 16 November 2026, the US Commodity Futures Trading Commission approved the first blockchain-based derivatives exchange and clearinghouse licences in American history, granting them to Backpack — through TLD Markets, Inc. as exchange and TLD Clearing, Inc. as clearinghouse — and to tZERO, which had secured the same pair of licences days earlier. The approvals let the two firms offer regulated crypto derivatives, including leveraged perpetual futures trading around the clock for US users, under the same standards as traditional exchanges. Neither firm has announced a launch date, no new rules were created, and the decision does not authorise Hyperliquid, Polymarket or any other platform.

For Indian readers, nothing in Indian law has changed, and the new venues are not open to you. But the direction is now visible: the world's largest derivatives regulator has decided that blockchain infrastructure can live inside the existing rulebook, and it has licensed the first firms to prove it. If you are an Indian user, the lesson is closer to home — your access to crypto still runs through banks and exchanges that can freeze without warning, as this week's Coinbase, Unocoin and ZebPay disruptions showed. Regulated rails are being built. They are just not being built here yet.
