Bitcoin fell below $70,000 early on Friday 13 November 2026, touching its lowest level since 19 August, as the US dollar climbed to a 19-month high and expectations of further US interest-rate rises hardened. The largest cryptocurrency dropped to about $69,000, down roughly 17% this quarter, and the selling gathered pace as leveraged traders were forced out of their positions: more than $1.3 billion of crypto futures bets were liquidated in 24 hours. This guide explains what happened, what the Dollar Index is and why a strong dollar pressures Bitcoin, and what Indian users should — and should not — take from the second sharp fall in two days.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a published news event; it does not recommend buying, selling or holding any asset, and it does not predict where prices will go.

## What Happened: The Facts at a Glance

The facts, as reported on 13 November 2026 by CoinDesk, NewsBytes, Business Today, TokenPost and KuCoin's daily market report, are:

- **Bitcoin fell below $70,000 for the first time since 19 August.** Bitcoin dropped to about $69,000 early on Friday, according to CoinDesk data, after starting the day near $73,783. A Finnhub snapshot at 04:18 UTC on 13 November showed Bitcoin at $69,002, down about 6.5% on the day, with a daily low of about $68,528. Business Today put Bitcoin at about $70,834, down 3.8% over 24 hours and 6.5% over the past week — the difference between sources reflects different reporting windows, not a disagreement about the direction.
- **The Dollar Index rose above 103, its highest since April 2025.** The Dollar Index, which tracks the greenback against a basket of major currencies, has risen by nearly 2% since early October, according to CoinDesk, as expectations of US Federal Reserve rate rises firmed. A stronger dollar is a headwind for risk assets, and Bitcoin is one of the most sensitive of them.
- **More than $1.3 billion of crypto futures positions were liquidated in 24 hours.** Coinglass data cited by CoinDesk put total liquidations above $1.3 billion, with Bitcoin accounting for about $677 million. Nearly $500 million of that was liquidated just as Bitcoin broke below $70,000 — a sign of how crowded the market had become with leveraged long positions. The largest single liquidation was a long Bitcoin position worth more than $31 million on Hyperliquid.
- **Ethereum fell below $2,000 for the first time since August.** Ethereum dropped to about $1,984–$2,007 depending on the source, down about 2% over 24 hours and about 10% over the past week, according to Business Today. NewsBytes reported Ethereum slipping below $2,000 for the first time since August, in the same move that took Bitcoin under $70,000.
- **US spot Bitcoin ETFs kept losing money.** US spot Bitcoin ETFs have now recorded about $1.44 billion in net outflows over five consecutive trading sessions, according to Farside Investors data cited by several outlets, with total outflows from the start of November reaching about $1.88 billion through the 11 November session. BlackRock's iShares Bitcoin Trust alone recorded outflows of about $269.7 million in the latest session.
- **Bitcoin is down about 44% from its record high.** Bitcoin's October 2025 record high was above $126,000, according to data cited across the day's coverage. At about $69,000–$71,000, it sits roughly 44% below that peak, and about 17% lower this quarter, reversing much of the rally of the previous three months.
- **In India, Bitcoin traded at roughly ₹64.9 lakh.** A price tracker put one Bitcoin at about ₹64,93,725 as of 09:16 IST on 13 November, down about 6.1% in rupee terms over 24 hours. Indian prices track the global dollar price, converted at the rupee rate, plus the usual local premium that comes from INR–USDT conversion spreads and domestic liquidity.
- **Geopolitical tensions added to the pressure.** NewsBytes reported that tensions in the Middle East intensified after Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps claimed it had destroyed a US early-warning radar in Bahrain, in response to an air attack on a telecoms tower in Sirik. The claim added to the risk-off mood that was already pushing investors away from risk assets.

Two caveats matter before going further. First, the figures above are snapshots from a moving market. Prices, liquidation totals and ETF flows differ slightly between sources because each one measures a different window, and Bitcoin was trading near $70,000–$71,000 at different hours of the morning. The direction of the move is consistent across every source; the exact decimals are not the point. Second, this was the second sharp fall in two days. Bitcoin had already dropped below $72,000 on 12 November after Nvidia's chief executive resigned without warning. The 13 November fall has a different driver — the dollar and US monetary policy — and it is worth understanding that driver on its own terms, because it is the one that will still matter after the Nvidia news fades.

## What Is the Dollar Index, and Why Does It Move Bitcoin?

The Dollar Index, usually written DXY, is a measure of the US dollar's value against a basket of six major currencies: the euro, the Japanese yen, the British pound, the Canadian dollar, the Swedish krona and the Swiss franc. The euro carries the largest weight, at about 58%. When the index rises, the dollar is strengthening against those currencies; when it falls, the dollar is weakening. On 13 November it traded above 103, its highest since April 2025.

The index matters for Bitcoin because the dollar is the world's reserve currency, and most global borrowing, trade and commodity pricing is done in dollars. When the dollar strengthens, three things happen that tend to weigh on crypto.

**First, a strong dollar usually means higher US interest rates are expected.** The dollar rose by nearly 2% since early October because traders increasingly expect the US Federal Reserve to raise rates, or at least to keep them high for longer. That expectation hardened after the October US inflation reading, published on 10 November, came in hotter than forecast — consumer prices rose 0.4% in the month, above the 0.3% analysts had expected — and pushed the US 10-year government bond yield above 5%. When safe assets like US government bonds pay more, holding an asset with no yield, like Bitcoin, becomes relatively less attractive. Money that was chasing returns in risk assets drifts back towards the safety of dollars and bonds.

**Second, a strong dollar tightens financial conditions everywhere.** Because so much borrowing around the world is denominated in dollars, a rising dollar makes that debt more expensive to service in local-currency terms. That squeezes liquidity — the pool of money available to buy assets — and crypto is one of the first places the squeeze shows up, because it trades around the clock and has no circuit breakers. The contraction is measurable: KuCoin's daily report noted that stablecoin supply — the dollars sitting on the sidelines ready to buy crypto — had fallen by $14.2 billion over the past 30 days, the largest contraction since June 2022. When the money available to buy crypto shrinks, prices have less support.

**Third, the dollar and Bitcoin compete for the same role in a crisis.** In calm times, Bitcoin is bought as a bet on future growth. In nervous times, investors reach for the dollar, not Bitcoin, as the safe place to park money. The 13 November fall happened on a day when both the dollar and geopolitical tensions were rising at once — the dollar at a 19-month high and a Middle East escalation claim in the headlines. That combination is about as unfavourable as it gets for an asset that depends on investors feeling confident enough to take risks.

None of this means the dollar and Bitcoin always move in opposite directions. They have moved together before, and they will again — Bitcoin's price depends on many things at once, including ETF flows, exchange security, regulation and its own supply dynamics. But over the past month, the pattern has been consistent: every time US rate-rise expectations have strengthened, Bitcoin has fallen. The 10 November hot inflation reading pushed Bitcoin below $74,000 the same evening. The 12 November Nvidia resignation pushed it below $72,000. And the dollar's climb to a 19-month high pushed it below $70,000 on 13 November. Three different triggers, one common thread: confidence in risk assets is falling.

## Why the $70,000 Break Triggered $1.3 Billion in Liquidations

The word "liquidation" appears in almost every report of a sharp crypto fall, and it is worth understanding, because it explains why a 4–6% fall in Bitcoin can feel like a much bigger event.

Most crypto exchanges let traders borrow money to take larger positions than their own cash would allow. A trader with $1,000 can, for example, control $10,000 of Bitcoin. If the price rises, the gains are multiplied. If it falls, the losses are multiplied too — and if the losses reach the trader's deposit, the exchange closes the position automatically and sells the Bitcoin. That forced sale is a liquidation.

On 13 November, the forced sales were heavily one-sided and heavily concentrated around one price level. TokenPost reported that a liquidation map — a chart showing where leveraged positions would be closed if the price reached them — put about $622 million of long-side liquidation intensity below $70,000 across major centralised exchanges. That figure is not a precise tally of positions, but a measure of how much forced selling was clustered just under the level. When Bitcoin broke through $70,000, nearly $500 million of positions were liquidated in short order, according to CoinDesk's reading of Coinglass data. The sales pushed the price lower, which closed more positions, which pushed the price lower again — the same cascade mechanism this site explained after the October liquidation wave, and again after the 12 November fall.

The largest single liquidation tells the story in miniature: one long Bitcoin position worth more than $31 million was closed on Hyperliquid, a decentralised exchange, as the price broke down. That is a single trader, or a single fund, losing a very large position in minutes — not because of a decision to sell, but because the market moved against a leveraged bet and the exchange closed it automatically.

For Indian users, the practical point is the same one this site has made after every liquidation wave: leverage is what turns a bad day into a disaster for individual traders. The price fall itself was about 4–6% in a day. The traders who lost the most were not the ones who simply owned Bitcoin — they were the ones who had borrowed to bet on it rising. That distinction is the difference between a volatile day and a wiped-out account, and it is why every guide on this site repeats the same warning about leverage.

## How This Fall Differs From 12 November's

It is tempting to treat the falls of 12 and 13 November as one event, but they had different triggers, and the difference matters for understanding what might come next.

The 12 November fall was driven by a corporate shock: Nvidia, the world's most valuable company, announced after US markets closed that its co-founder and chief executive Jensen Huang had resigned with immediate effect, with no successor named and no reason given. AI stocks sold off, Nvidia fell about 5.8%, and the selling spread into crypto, where Bitcoin dropped below $72,000 for the first time since August. The link was sentiment: investors who owned the AI story also owned crypto, and a shock to one side of that trade spilled into the other.

The 13 November fall was driven by macroeconomics: a dollar at a 19-month high, rising expectations of US rate rises, hot inflation data, and a geopolitical escalation claim. No company resigned, no exchange was hacked, and nothing about Bitcoin's network changed. The trigger was the price of money itself — the cost of holding dollars versus holding risk assets.

The distinction matters because the two kinds of trigger resolve differently. A corporate shock resolves when the company names a successor and the market judges the choice; Nvidia's next announcement is the thing to watch there. A macroeconomic pressure resolves only when the data changes — when inflation cools, or the Federal Reserve signals that rates will not rise further, or the dollar stops climbing. That is a slower process, and it is why the 13 November fall is the more important of the two to understand, even though the 12 November fall had the more dramatic headline.

It also matters because the two falls compounded each other. Bitcoin entered 13 November already below its 200-day average and below the short-term-holder cost basis of about $75,600 — the average price at which recent buyers bought in. It had already seen five straight sessions of ETF outflows totalling about $1.44 billion. And leveraged traders had already been hit once, on 12 November, when more than $700 million of positions were liquidated. A market that has just been knocked down once is more fragile the second time, and the dollar's climb was enough to knock it down again.

## What This Means for Indian Users

The fall below $70,000 is a global market story, and its direct effect on Indian crypto users is limited. But it touches Indian users in a few concrete ways.

**Rupee prices follow the dollar price — and the exchange rate.** Bitcoin in India was trading at roughly ₹64.9 lakh on the morning of 13 November, down from about ₹67.1–67.3 lakh the day before. That rupee price is the global dollar price converted at the rupee rate, plus the usual local premium. It means Indian users feel two movements at once: the dollar price of Bitcoin, and the rupee's own exchange rate against the dollar. When the dollar strengthens globally — as it did on 13 November — the rupee tends to weaken against it, which softens the fall in rupee terms slightly, but does not reverse it. The premium Indian exchanges charge over the global price is a cost of buying in India, and it does not disappear when prices fall.

**Tax rules are unchanged.** Crypto gains in India are taxed at a flat 30% plus a 4% cess, with 1% TDS on transfers, and none of that changed on 13 November. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. A fall in price does not change the tax treatment of a sale, and losses cannot be set off against other income under current rules — a point that matters more, not less, on days when many people are selling.

**Indian exchanges are not the story this time.** The week's earlier news was dominated by Indian exchanges: Unocoin suspended all services on 12 November after a security breach, and the Enforcement Directorate's searches at CoinDCX and Delta Exchange are still an open investigation. The 13 November fall has no direct connection to any Indian exchange. If you hold crypto on an Indian exchange, the relevant question is still whether your exchange is operating normally, and whether you have your own records of what you hold — but the dollar-driven fall is a separate issue from exchange safety.

**Volatile days are scam days.** Sharp falls reliably produce a wave of scams: fake "recovery" services offering to retrieve lost funds, fake exchange-support accounts messaging users during outages, and fake tips claiming to know the bottom. None of these can do what they promise. Verify any claim through an exchange's official website or verified account, never share a seed phrase, private key or one-time password with anyone, and treat any message that creates urgency — "act now or lose everything" — as a warning sign in itself.

**Leverage is the real risk for most users.** The traders who lost the most on 13 November were leveraged long traders, not people who simply held Bitcoin. If you use leverage, understand that a 4–6% move in the underlying asset can close your position entirely. If you do not use leverage, a 4–6% fall is a bad day, not a catastrophe. That difference is the single most important thing to understand about days like this one.

## What to Watch Next

Four things will show whether 13 November was a one-day shock or the start of a longer slide — and none of them is a prediction.

- **The US producer price index, due 13 November at 19:00 IST.** The October PPI is the week's main remaining data release, with consensus at about 0.4% month on month and the year-on-year figure expected at about 6.6%, up from 6.3%. It follows the hot October CPI reading of 10 November, which strengthened expectations of a December rate rise. A hot PPI would add to the pressure on risk assets; a soft one would ease it. Either way, it is a data point, not a verdict.
- **Whether the dollar keeps climbing.** The Dollar Index above 103 is the engine of this fall. If it keeps rising, the pressure on Bitcoin continues; if it stalls or reverses, the pressure eases. The dollar's direction depends on the same inflation and rate data, so the PPI release and the dollar move are really one thing to watch, not two.
- **Whether Bitcoin holds $70,000 — or reclaims $73,000.** TokenPost's liquidation map put about $622 million of long-side intensity below $70,000 and about $838 million of short-side intensity above $73,000. KuCoin's daily report put the first support at $70,000–$71,000 and said a sustained recovery would first require reclaiming $73,000. These are levels to watch, not targets to trade on: holding $70,000 would show buyers returning, and a break below it would show the liquidation pressure continuing. Neither outcome tells you what happens the week after.
- **ETF flows.** Five straight sessions of outflows totalling about $1.44 billion is a real drag on the market, because ETF selling adds to the supply that buyers must absorb. A return to inflows would be the clearest sign that institutional sentiment has stabilised. Until then, the flow data is part of the same story as the price: confidence, once lost, takes time to return.

None of these is a signal to act on by itself. They are the facts to watch while the dust settles, and they are the facts that will determine whether 13 November is remembered as the day a strong dollar knocked Bitcoin below $70,000, or as the day the slide that began with Nvidia's chief executive resigning turned into something longer.

## The Bottom Line

On the morning of Friday 13 November 2026, Bitcoin fell below $70,000 for the first time since 19 August, reaching about $69,000 at its lowest, as the US Dollar Index rose above 103 — its highest since April 2025 — and expectations of further US interest-rate rises hardened after a hot October inflation reading. More than $1.3 billion of leveraged crypto positions were liquidated in 24 hours, Ethereum fell below $2,000 for the first time since August, and US spot Bitcoin ETFs extended their outflow streak to five sessions and about $1.44 billion. The move was driven by the rising dollar and tightening financial conditions, amplified by leverage and by a market already weakened by the previous day's fall — not by any change to Bitcoin itself or to Indian rules. For Indian users, the practical points are that rupee prices follow the global price and the exchange rate, the 30% tax and 1% TDS are unchanged, leveraged traders bore the worst of the losses, and volatile days are when scams multiply. What happens next depends on the US inflation data due on 13 November, on whether the dollar keeps climbing, and on whether buyers return — none of which can be known in advance.
