# Bitcoin's $55K Two-Year Low and $60K Rebound: India Guide

Bitcoin fell to $55,106 on Saturday, 21 November 2026 — its lowest price since September 2024 — and then climbed back above $60,000 within hours. In a single day, the market wiped out leveraged traders on both sides: roughly $2 billion of long positions were liquidated as the price collapsed, and then about $230 million of short positions were liquidated in four hours as it bounced. This guide explains what happened on 21 November, why a stock-market sell-off in artificial-intelligence shares dragged Bitcoin down with it, how the rebound worked, and what the whipsaw means for an Indian reader.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a reported market event; it does not recommend any token, investment or action, and it does not predict any price movement.

## What Happened on 21 November

The day before, Bitcoin had already broken below $60,000 for the first time since July, as this site covered in its previous post. On Saturday, the selling continued into the early hours. According to Bitcoinist, Bitcoin dropped to $55,106 on Coinbase in the 06:00 UTC hour — 11:30 in the morning in India — its weakest print since September 2024. By mid-morning it had recovered to around $57,800, and later in the day it climbed roughly $5,000 to push back above $60,000, according to CryptoPotato.

The scale of the round trip is what makes this day stand out. A fall of about 9% from the session high to the low, followed by a recovery of roughly the same size, all inside a single day. CoinPaprika data captured at 04:06 UTC on 22 November put Bitcoin at $59,508 (about ₹55.15 lakh), up 5.6% over 24 hours but still down 8.2% for the week, with a market capitalisation of $1.20 trillion and 24-hour trading volume of $35.30 billion. A later snapshot at 22:30 UTC on 21 November had recorded $60,252 (about ₹55.84 lakh), up 2.3% over 24 hours but down 5.4% over seven days.

The rest of the market followed the same pattern. Ether slid toward $1,500 before trading near $1,700, BNB recovered from $500 to $545, and XRP bounced from a test of the $0.85 level to $0.92, according to CryptoPotato. Hyperliquid's HYPE token and Zcash were named as the two biggest losers of the previous day, and both regained a significant part of their losses in the rebound. The Crypto Fear & Greed Index stood at 11 on 22 November, still in "Extreme Fear" territory, according to Alternative.me data — barely changed from the reading of 9 recorded the day before, which shows that sentiment stayed fearful even after the price recovered.

## Why Bitcoin Fell to a Two-Year Low

Nothing in the crypto market itself caused the fall. The selling started on Wall Street, in the same artificial-intelligence trade that has driven markets for much of the year. The Nasdaq Composite shed about 5.3% on Friday, and Nvidia — the chipmaker at the centre of the AI boom — fell nearly 14%, according to Bitcoinist. Reuters linked the rout to weeks of concern about the profitability of AI infrastructure spending, rising borrowing costs, and regulatory risks after the Democrats' midterm election win. Anthropic's shares had plunged more than 14% a day after their Nasdaq debut, and chip stocks sank about 8% the day before.

When investors sell risky assets, they tend to sell them together. Bitcoin has behaved like a risk asset for years: it rises when investors feel confident and falls when they rush toward cash, even when the news has nothing to do with crypto. That is what happened on 20–21 November. Gold fell too, and the whole crypto market dropped with stocks. The Nasdaq post on this site, published on 6 November, explains the same transmission chain in detail — this weekend was the sharpest example of it since then.

Two other forces added to the pressure. First, traders priced in a roughly 97.9% chance of a US Federal Reserve rate hike at the 9 December meeting, according to BitcoinWorld. Higher rates make holding cash more attractive and borrowing more expensive, which weighs on every risk asset, including Bitcoin. Second, US spot Bitcoin ETFs recorded heavy outflows: $582.3 million in net outflows on Thursday 19 November, according to Yahoo Finance data cited by CoinMarketCap, and about $2.35 billion across recent sessions, according to the Sunday Guardian. When ETF investors sell, the funds must sell Bitcoin to meet redemptions, which adds selling pressure on top of everything else.

Then leverage did the rest. About $1.57 billion of crypto positions were liquidated over 24 hours on 20 November, with long positions — bets that prices would rise — accounting for $1.43 billion of the total and 265,525 traders liquidated, according to CoinGlass data reported by U.Today. Bitcoin accounted for $554.28 million of the liquidations and Ether $331.08 million. A separate tally put the total at $1.893 billion liquidated in the 24 hours to 21 November, with 91% of it in long positions, according to figures cited by Huobi HTX chief analyst Cloud. One widely cited figure put the total at $2.4 billion in 24 hours, with more than $1 billion of it in Bitcoin and $545 million in Ether. The exact total depends on the window measured, but every measure agrees on the shape: this was a long liquidation event, and the forced selling pushed prices lower, which forced more selling.

Put together, the chain is simple to follow. AI stocks fell, investors sold risk assets, ETF outflows added selling, and leveraged long positions were closed automatically as prices dropped. Each step made the next one worse. Bitcoin ended the move at $55,106 — its lowest since September 2024 and roughly 52% below its all-time high of around $126,000 reached in October 2025.

## The Rebound: How $230 Million in Shorts Got Wiped Out

The recovery was as violent as the fall, and it worked through the same mechanism in reverse. After Bitcoin hit $55,106, the selling exhausted itself: at that price, most of the leveraged longs that could be liquidated already had been. Traders who had opened short positions — bets that the price would keep falling — were now crowded into the same trade, many of them using borrowed money too.

When the price started to bounce, those short sellers faced the same automatic-closure problem the longs had faced hours earlier. A short seller who has borrowed Bitcoin to sell it must buy it back to close the position. If the price rises instead of falling, the exchange closes the position automatically once the trader's margin runs out — and that forced buying pushes the price higher, which closes more shorts. CoinGlass data cited by CryptoPotato showed about $230 million in short liquidations over four hours during the recovery, against only about $23 million in longs. In other words, almost all of the liquidations during the bounce were traders who had bet on further falls.

This is what traders call a short squeeze, and it is the mirror image of the long liquidation that caused the fall. The two events together — longs wiped out on the way down, shorts wiped out on the way up — are what make a whipsaw so damaging for leveraged traders: whichever direction you bet, the market moved against you within hours.

There was also reported dip-buying in the background. Blockchain monitoring by Lookonchain, reported by Panews, flagged a purchase of 10,356 Bitcoin — worth roughly $607 million at an average price of about $58,651 — attributed to Abraxas Capital while Bitcoin was in the sell-off. Neither Lookonchain's tracking nor the Panews report includes a public confirmation from Abraxas Capital itself, so this should be treated as on-chain observation, not a confirmed company announcement. Even so, it fits the pattern of the day: some large holders were buying while leveraged traders were being forced out.

## What Is a Whipsaw?

A whipsaw is a sharp price move in one direction followed quickly by a sharp move in the opposite direction. The name comes from the back-and-forth motion of a two-person saw: the market cuts one way, then cuts the other, and anyone caught in the middle gets hurt both times.

Whipsaws are common after big, fast falls, for three reasons. First, the fall itself is often exaggerated by forced selling, so prices can bounce hard once the forced selling stops — not because anything fundamental has changed, but because the selling was mechanical. Second, the bounce attracts traders betting that the fall will continue, and their short positions become fuel for the next move up. Third, weekend trading is thinner than weekday trading, because many large institutional desks are less active, so the same amount of buying or selling moves prices further. The 21 November move happened on a Saturday, when weekend liquidity is typically lower.

The important thing for a beginner to understand is that a whipsaw is not a signal about where the price is going next. The fall to $55,106 did not prove Bitcoin would keep falling — it bounced the same day. And the rebound above $60,000 does not prove the fall is over — the price slipped back toward $59,500 by Sunday morning, and one analyst cited by Panews noted that $60,000 has flipped from support to resistance, meaning the level that used to hold the price up may now cap it. Both readings are possible, and this guide does not predict which one will play out. The December 8–9 Federal Reserve meeting is the next scheduled event traders are watching, because the rate decision will shape the same risk appetite that drove this weekend's move.

## The Leverage Trap: Why Both Sides Lost Money

The 21 November whipsaw is a useful lesson in how leverage works, because it hurt traders on both sides of the market in a single day. Leverage means borrowing money to take a bigger position than your own capital allows. A trader with ₹10,000 who uses 10x leverage controls a ₹1,00,000 position. A 10% move against them wipes out their entire ₹10,000 — and the exchange closes the position automatically, at whatever price it can get, before the trader owes more than they deposited.

That automatic closure is the key. In a normal market, a trader who is wrong can wait, hope, or close the position themselves. With leverage, the exchange decides for them, and it decides at the worst moment — when the price has already moved sharply. On 21 November, leveraged longs were closed automatically as the price fell toward $55,106, and their forced selling pushed the price lower, closing more longs. Hours later, leveraged shorts were closed automatically as the price rose above $60,000, and their forced buying pushed the price higher, closing more shorts. The market's own plumbing turned a sharp move into a cascade, twice, in opposite directions.

This is why the liquidation numbers matter more than the price levels for understanding the day. About $1.9 billion of positions were liquidated in the 24 hours to 21 November, and then $230 million of shorts in four hours during the rebound. Those are not investments being sold by choice; they are positions being closed by exchanges because the traders behind them ran out of margin. Every forced closure added to the move that caused it.

For an Indian beginner, the practical point is simple: leverage multiplies losses exactly as fast as it multiplies gains, and it removes your ability to wait out a bad move. A spot holder who bought Bitcoin and watched it fall to $55,106 still held the same amount of Bitcoin at the end of the day. A leveraged trader who bet the same way lost everything when the position was liquidated — and a leveraged trader who bet the opposite way lost everything a few hours later, when the price bounced. The whipsaw punished both directions, which is exactly what leverage does in a volatile market.

## What It Means for India

For Indian holders, the most important fact is what did not change. The fall to $55,106 and the rebound above $60,000 did not change India's crypto rules, did not change anyone's wallet, and did not change the tax treatment of crypto gains. Gains on transferring virtual digital assets in India are still taxed at a flat 30%, with 1% TDS on transfers, and losses cannot be set off against other income or carried forward. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

That no-set-off rule matters more than usual after a weekend like this one. A trader whose leveraged position was liquidated has lost real money, and under Indian rules that loss brings no tax relief: it cannot be deducted from gains on other tokens, and it cannot be carried forward to a future year. The BitcoinWorld coverage of the weekend made the same point for Indian users — a liquidation loss is a loss with no tax relief attached. Spot holders are in a different position: an unrealised fall in the value of coins you still hold is not a taxable event at all, because tax applies when you transfer the asset, not while you hold it.

The price Indian readers actually pay is also different from the dollar figures in headlines. On 22 November, Bitcoin traded at roughly ₹54.85–55.65 lakh across Indian and global platforms, according to the Sunday Guardian. That is the price after converting dollars to rupees and after the spreads charged by exchanges serving India. The India premium — the gap between the Indian price and the global price — has compressed sharply: USDT traded at about ₹92.60 against a reference rate of ₹93.37, roughly flat to slightly below the reference rate, according to BitcoinWorld. That is a very different picture from early November, when this site reported a USDT premium of more than 6% in India. A premium near zero suggests Indian buyers were not rushing to exit rupees into stablecoins during the sell-off; the selling was global, not a flight from Indian platforms.

Two India-specific watch items from recent weeks remain relevant. First, INR withdrawals from some Indian exchanges have faced delays this month, with Coinbase, CoinDCX and ZebPay all reporting banking-partner problems — anyone moving money should check their exchange's current status before starting a transfer. Second, the Bharat Web3 Association submitted recommendations to the Finance Ministry on 21 November, ahead of the Union Budget 2027, asking for a review of the 1% TDS, permission to set off and carry forward losses, and a review of the 30% rate — the same issues this guide describes. Those are proposals, not changes, and nothing has been decided.

## What to Watch Next

The next scheduled event that matters for this story is the US Federal Reserve meeting on 8–9 December, when traders currently price in a roughly 97.9% chance of a rate hike, according to BitcoinWorld. That pricing can change quickly — it is a market expectation, not a decision — but it is the main variable analysts cited over the weekend, and it will shape the same risk appetite that drove Bitcoin down with AI stocks and back up again.

The other thing to watch is ETF flows. The $582.3 million of net outflows from US spot Bitcoin ETFs on 19 November was part of the selling pressure before the weekend, and whether those outflows continue or reverse will tell us more about institutional sentiment than any single price level. This site covered the record $541 million outflow from BlackRock's IBIT fund on 20 November in a separate post, and the pattern of outflows has been the steady background to the whole November slide.

For a beginner, the useful habit is to watch the drivers, not the price. The price on any given day is the result of stock markets, interest-rate expectations, ETF flows and leverage all acting at once. This weekend showed all four acting in the same 24 hours: stocks fell, rate-hike odds rose, ETFs saw outflows, and leverage turned a sharp fall into a cascade — then turned the rebound into a squeeze. Understanding that chain is more useful than memorising any price level, because the same chain will show up again the next time markets turn volatile.
