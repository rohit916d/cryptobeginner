# SIM Swap Attacks in Crypto: How Indians Lose OTP Access

A SIM swap attack in crypto begins with something most people trust without thinking: their mobile number. In India, that number is linked to bank alerts, UPI apps, email recovery, and crypto exchange logins. If a scammer quietly takes control of your number, every one-time password sent by SMS can start arriving on their phone instead of yours. You may not notice anything wrong until your signal disappears — and by then, the attacker may already be trying to enter your accounts.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose.

This guide explains how a SIM swap works, the warning signs Indian users should never ignore, and the practical steps that make this attack much harder to pull off.

## What Is a SIM Swap, Exactly?

Your SIM card is the small chip — or digital eSIM profile — that connects your phone number to the mobile network. A legitimate SIM replacement is a normal service: if you lose your phone, your provider can issue a new SIM and move your number onto it.

A SIM swap attack abuses that process. The scammer pretends to be you, convinces the provider to activate your number on a SIM they hold, and your own SIM stops working. The network now treats the attacker's phone as yours.

Think of it like a duplicate house key handed to a stranger. The lock did not break — the key system was fooled. That is why SIM swap is a social engineering attack: it targets people and procedures, not just technology.

## How the Attack Usually Unfolds

### Step 1: The scammer collects your details

Attackers gather personal information first: your full name, date of birth, address, and sometimes recent recharge details. This data often comes from phishing messages, fake customer-care calls, leaked databases, or oversharing on social media.

### Step 2: Your number is moved to their SIM

Armed with those details, the scammer requests a SIM replacement, claiming the original was lost or damaged. If the request passes the provider's checks, your number is activated on their SIM. Your phone loses network signal — often the first and only visible sign.

### Step 3: OTPs and password resets begin

Now the attacker requests password resets on services linked to your number. Each reset code arrives on their phone, because the network now delivers your messages to them.

### Step 4: Money and crypto are moved quickly

Attackers work fast, knowing the victim will notice the dead signal soon. On an exchange, they may log in, change security settings, and withdraw funds to a wallet they control. Crypto transfers are difficult to reverse, which is why exchanges are attractive targets.

## Warning Signs You Should Never Ignore

| Warning sign | What it may mean | What to do immediately |
|---|---|---|
| Your phone suddenly shows "no service" for a long time | Your number may have been moved to another SIM | Call your telecom provider from another phone |
| You receive OTPs or reset alerts you did not request | Someone is testing access to your accounts | Do not share the codes; secure your email first |
| A message says your SIM or number was changed | A replacement may have been processed | Contact the provider and ask for the request details |
| You cannot log in to your exchange or email | Passwords may already have been reset | Use official support channels and freeze activity if possible |
| A "telecom agent" asks for personal details by phone | Possible information collection for a swap | Disconnect and call the official customer-care number yourself |

The most important sign is the simplest: an unexplained, lasting loss of mobile signal. If your signal stays dead while people around you on the same network are connected, treat it as a security event, not a network glitch.

## Why SMS OTP Is the Weak Link

SMS one-time passwords were designed when a phone number felt as personal as a fingerprint. Today, a number can be moved between SIMs through a customer-service process, so SMS security quietly depends on that process never being fooled.

That is the core lesson for crypto users in India: an account protected only by SMS OTP is really protected by your telecom provider's identity checks. If those checks fail once, every code goes to the wrong phone. SMS OTP still stops casual attackers — it just should not be the strongest lock on an account that holds money.

## How to Protect Your Number and Accounts

### Use an authenticator app or security key where possible

On Indian exchanges such as CoinDCX, WazirX, and ZebPay, and on your email, choose an authenticator app or hardware security key instead of SMS whenever possible. Codes generated inside an app do not travel through your mobile number, so a SIM swap alone cannot capture them. Save backup codes offline, on paper.

### Set a SIM PIN and ask about port-out protection

A SIM PIN locks your physical SIM so it cannot be used in another phone without the code. Separately, ask your telecom provider what extra verification options exist for SIM replacement and number porting, and enable any additional protection offered.

### Guard the details a scammer needs

Never share personal details, OTPs, or SIM numbers with anyone who calls or messages you — including people claiming to be telecom staff or exchange support. Real support teams do not need your OTP. Avoid posting your phone number, date of birth, and address together on public profiles.

### Secure your email first

Email is usually the master key: password resets for exchanges flow through it. Protect it with a strong, unique password and app-based two-factor authentication. If an attacker controls your number but not your email or authenticator, their path becomes much narrower.

### Keep exchange withdrawals harder to abuse

Where your exchange offers withdrawal confirmations, address allow-listing, or cooling-off periods after security changes, turn them on. These features add friction for an attacker racing against time. Also keep only the funds you actively need on any single platform.

## What to Do If You Suspect a SIM Swap

Act in this order, and act quickly:

1. **Call your telecom provider from another phone.** Report a suspected unauthorised SIM change, ask for your number to be restored or frozen, and note the complaint number.
2. **Secure your email.** From a trusted device, change the email password and check its recovery settings and recent activity.
3. **Contact your crypto exchange through its official app or website.** Ask for a temporary freeze, review recent logins and withdrawals, and change your password.
4. **Inform your bank** if the same number is linked to your bank or UPI apps, and watch for unfamiliar transactions.
5. **Report the incident** on India's national cybercrime portal (cybercrime.gov.in) or helpline 1930, and keep screenshots, messages, and complaint numbers as records.

Speed matters because the attacker's advantage is temporary. Every minute you shorten their window reduces what they can reach.

If any crypto is moved during such an incident, keep records of dates, amounts in INR, and transaction IDs for your filings. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a SIM swap empty a hardware wallet?

No, not by itself. A hardware wallet's keys stay on the device and are not tied to your phone number. A SIM swap threatens accounts that depend on SMS recovery or OTPs — exchanges, email, and banking. This is one reason experienced users separate long-term storage from exchange accounts.

### Does an eSIM make me safe?

An eSIM removes the physical card, but the number can still be moved through provider processes if identity checks are fooled. Treat eSIM accounts with the same care: strong provider-account protection, minimal sharing of personal details, and app-based authentication on important accounts.

### Should I use a separate number for crypto accounts?

Some users keep a less-public number for financial accounts, which reduces exposure from data leaks. It can help, but it is not a substitute for authenticator apps — an unlisted number can still leak.

## Conclusion

A SIM swap attack is really an identity attack on your phone number, and in India that number unlocks far more than calls. The defence is simple: move important accounts from SMS OTP to authenticator apps, protect the details scammers collect, and treat a sudden lasting loss of signal as an emergency. Spend twenty minutes today checking your exchange and email security settings.
