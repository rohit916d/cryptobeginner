Russia has just drawn the clearest line yet between two ways of regulating crypto — and the contrast with India is striking. On 6 November 2026, the Bank of Russia published a directive that lets ordinary Russians buy crypto on regulated domestic exchanges for the first time, but only three coins, only after passing a test, and only up to **300,000 rubles a year — about ₹3.46 lakh** — through each broker. India, by comparison, puts no limit at all on how much crypto you can buy. It simply taxes what you earn when you sell.

This guide explains exactly what Russia approved, how the ₹3.46 lakh cap works in practice, what Indian investors can learn from the comparison, and what happens next as the rules take effect on 17 November 2026. Every figure is attributed to its source, and the comparison uses the rules as they stand today — not as anyone predicts they will change.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains reported regulatory changes; it does not recommend any coin, exchange or trade.

## What Russia Actually Approved

The Bank of Russia's directive, signed by governor Elvira Nabiullina under Federal Law No. 282-FZ on digital currency and digital rights, was published on 6 November 2026 and takes effect ten days later, on **17 November 2026**. From that date, trading organisers — exchanges — may admit the approved assets to public trading. The law itself took effect on 1 September 2026, after presidential approval in August, and the 6 November directive is the document that turns the law's framework into concrete retail rules.

Three things change on 17 November. First, ordinary Russians — legally, "non-qualified investors" — can buy crypto through regulated channels: brokers, trust managers and licensed crypto exchange offices, both on exchanges and over the counter. Second, the list of what they can buy is short: **Bitcoin (BTC), Ether (ETH) and Tether's USDT** — nothing else. Third, every purchase is capped and gated by a test, which we explain below.

Two things do not change. Crypto payments for goods and services remain banned in Russia, so the new rules are about investment access, not about spending crypto. And peer-to-peer transactions between individuals will no longer be permitted from **1 July 2027**, according to the Bank of Russia's framework — a detail that matters because it closes the informal route that many Russians have used until now.

## Only Three Coins: Why Bitcoin, Ether and USDT?

The short list is the substance of the story. The Bank of Russia said it picked the three assets using three criteria: market capitalisation, average daily trading volume, and at least five years of price history on foreign exchanges. As the central bank put it, "only the most liquid assets made the list."

That filter is doing real work. It excludes the thousands of smaller tokens that trade on global exchanges, and it excludes every new or thinly traded asset. Bitcoin and Ether are the two largest cryptocurrencies by market value, and USDT is the largest stablecoin — a token designed to track the US dollar. The choice is deliberate: the regulator is admitting only the assets it considers deep and liquid enough that an ordinary buyer is unlikely to be trapped in a market with no buyers.

It is also worth noting how USDT is classified. Under the directive, USDT is treated as a "foreign digital instrument" rather than a digital currency — a legal distinction that reflects the fact that it is issued by a foreign company, Tether, rather than being a decentralised asset like Bitcoin. For the retail buyer the practical effect is the same: USDT is on the approved list, and it is the only stablecoin on it.

Qualified investors — a legal status in Russia roughly comparable to a sophisticated or professional investor elsewhere — are not bound by the list at all. They can access any crypto asset without restrictions, and they face no purchase cap. The three-coin list applies only to ordinary retail buyers.

## The ₹3.46 Lakh Cap: How It Actually Works

The headline number is **300,000 rubles per calendar year**, and the conversion to rupees is where Indian readers should pay attention. At the Bank of Russia's official dollar rate for 7 November 2026, 82.1386 rubles to the dollar, the annual allowance is worth about **$3,652**. At the 7 November rupee rate of 1.1547 rubles per rupee, that is about **₹3,46,405** — roughly ₹3.46 lakh. Some reports round the dollar figure to about $3,700; the exact rupee value moves with the exchange rate, so treat ₹3.46 lakh as an approximation, not a fixed number.

To put the cap in perspective: at Bitcoin's price of about $75,988 on 6 November, a full year's allowance buys roughly **0.048 BTC** — less than a twentieth of one Bitcoin. At Ether's price of about $2,226 the same day, it buys about **1.64 ETH**. Divided by twelve, the cap works out to 25,000 rubles a month, or about $304. This is not a limit designed to let ordinary Russians build large crypto portfolios. It is a limit designed to let them participate while keeping the amounts small.

The cap has three mechanics that matter:

**It applies per intermediary, not per person.** The limit is 300,000 rubles of purchases through each individual broker in a calendar year. A buyer with accounts at several brokers could in principle buy more than 300,000 rubles in total. That is a genuine loophole in the design, and it is one reason the cap should be read as a starting point for regulation rather than a hard national ceiling.

**It counts the ruble cost of purchases, not holdings.** The directive defines the limit as the total ruble cost of digital currency already bought and being bought through that broker during the calendar year. That means the cap bites on what you spend, not on what your holdings are worth afterwards. If the price of what you bought rises, that does not eat into your allowance — but it also means a buyer who spends the full 300,000 rubles through one broker in January has reached that broker's limit for the rest of the year.

**Pending orders count too.** According to CryptoSlate's reporting on the directive, the cap includes both completed purchases and pending orders through that broker. You cannot get around the limit by stacking up open buy orders.

## The Mandatory Test: No Purchase Without Passing

Before any purchase — whether the buyer is qualified or not — the investor must pass a knowledge test and confirm they have read the risk information. The test is free, and a pass stays valid for one year, according to the framework's testing rules. Qualified investors skip the ceiling and the asset list, but they do not skip the test.

This is the part of the Russian approach that most resembles investor-protection rules in other markets. A test before buying is a gate, not a guarantee: it checks that the buyer understands what they are buying, but it cannot make a volatile asset safe. What it does do is force a pause. A first-time buyer cannot open an app and buy crypto on impulse; they must first demonstrate some understanding of the risks and acknowledge them in writing.

For Indian readers, the test is worth noting because India has nothing equivalent. No Indian exchange is required to test your knowledge before letting you buy crypto. The protection Indian buyers get comes from elsewhere — from the exchange's own disclosures, from SEBI-registered investment adviser rules that do not cover crypto, and from the tax system, which at least forces a paper trail.

## What Happens Next: 17 November and 1 December

The directive takes effect on **17 November 2026**. From that date, exchanges have the right to admit Bitcoin, Ether and USDT to public trading — but the directive does not require every platform to start offering them immediately. The right to list is not the same as a launch.

The next date to watch is **1 December 2026**, when the Moscow Exchange plans to begin cryptocurrency transactions, starting with pilot trades and gradually onboarding participants as they become ready. The Moscow Exchange is Russia's largest exchange group, and its pilot is the practical test of whether the new framework works: brokers, custody, settlement and the test itself all have to function together before ordinary buyers can actually use the system.

A third date sits further out: **1 July 2027**, when direct peer-to-peer transactions between individuals will no longer be permitted. That is the date the informal route closes, and it is the clearest signal of the regulator's direction — activity should move onto regulated venues, where the cap and the test apply, rather than happening between individuals where neither applies.

## Russia vs India: Two Opposite Approaches

The comparison between the two countries is the reason this story matters for Indian readers. Russia and India both regulate crypto heavily, but they regulate opposite ends of the transaction. Russia controls **how much and what** you can buy. India controls almost nothing about buying, and instead takes its share when you sell.

| Rule | Russia, retail (from 17 Nov 2026) | India, resident |
|---|---|---|
| Coins you can buy | BTC, ETH, USDT only | Anything listed on an FIU-IND-registered exchange |
| Yearly purchase cap | ₽300,000 (~₹3.46 lakh) per intermediary | None |
| Knowledge test before buying | Yes, free, valid one year | No |
| Tax on gains | Not covered by this directive | 30% + 4% cess = 31.2%, no loss set-off |
| TDS on sale | Not applicable | 1% of sale value |
| Crypto payments for goods | Banned | Not a legal payment method |
| Peer-to-peer trading | Banned from 1 July 2027 | Not banned, but FEMA and tax rules still apply |

The table makes the trade-off visible. A Russian retail buyer faces a hard ceiling — about ₹3.46 lakh a year per broker — but pays no crypto-specific purchase tax under this framework. An Indian buyer faces no ceiling at all, but every gain is taxed at 31.2% with no set-off of losses, and every sale attracts 1% TDS. Neither system is "free" crypto; they simply charge for it in different ways.

Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

There is a second, subtler difference. Russia's approach is a **permission system**: the regulator publishes a list of what is allowed, and everything else is off-limits to retail buyers. India's approach is a **tax-and-register system**: the government has not published an approved-coin list, but exchanges must register with the Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU-IND), follow know-your-customer and anti-money-laundering rules, and report transactions. The control in India comes through the exchange and the tax return, not through a whitelist of coins.

## What This Means for Indian Investors

The Russian rules do not change anything for an Indian resident directly. They apply to investors buying through Russian intermediaries, and an Indian buyer cannot use them to escape Indian law. But the story carries four practical lessons.

**1. Other countries are choosing whitelists, and India has not.** Russia's three-coin list is a whitelist: only what is named is allowed. India has so far avoided that approach, and Indian buyers can access hundreds of coins on registered exchanges. That breadth is a feature of the Indian system, but it is also a risk: with no whitelist, the responsibility for judging whether a coin is legitimate falls entirely on the buyer. If India ever moved toward a whitelist model, the coins available to Indian retail buyers could shrink sharply — and the Russian example shows what that would look like.

**2. Caps and taxes are two ways of saying the same thing.** A ₹3.46 lakh annual cap and a 31.2% tax on gains both discourage large, speculative positions — one by limiting the input, the other by taxing the output. Indian buyers sometimes describe the 30% tax as the government "punishing" crypto. The Russian comparison reframes it: at least India lets you decide how much to buy. A cap is arguably more restrictive than a tax, because a tax leaves the choice with you and takes a share of the result, while a cap removes the choice entirely above a small threshold.

**3. The offshore-routing crackdown is the Indian version of the same instinct.** In the same week Russia published its directive, India's Enforcement Directorate searched premises linked to CoinDCX and Delta Exchange, alleging that Indian users' rupee deposits were converted into USDT and routed to offshore exchanges such as Binance, KuCoin, Gate.io and Bybit. The allegation, which both companies dispute and which is unproven, is that trades worth thousands of crores were moved offshore in breach of FEMA. The parallel with Russia is real: both regulators are trying to keep crypto activity inside venues they can see. Russia does it with a cap and a whitelist; India does it with FEMA enforcement, FIU-IND registration and the tax net. The lesson for Indian users is practical: trade on FIU-IND-registered Indian exchanges, keep records, and do not assume that routing trades through an offshore platform puts you outside Indian law.

**4. The USDT premium is a reminder that Indian access is not free either.** Indian buyers routinely pay more for USDT than the global dollar rate — a premium that has run to roughly 6% at times in 2026, driven by rupee conversion friction and demand on Indian platforms. Russia's cap does not apply to Indian buyers, but the premium is the Indian market's own version of a cost of access. When you compare systems, compare the total cost: Russia's cap, India's tax and TDS, and the premium you pay to convert rupees into stablecoins.

## What to Watch Next

Three dates matter from here. **17 November 2026** is when the directive takes effect and exchanges gain the right to admit the three assets. The first question is whether any exchange actually lists them on day one, or whether the practical launch waits for the Moscow Exchange pilot. **1 December 2026** is when that pilot is scheduled to begin, and it will show whether the test, the cap and the custody arrangements work in practice. **1 July 2027** is when peer-to-peer transactions are banned, and it will show whether the regulator can actually move activity onto regulated venues.

Beyond Russia, the question is whether other countries copy the model. A three-coin whitelist with a small annual cap is a template that other cautious regulators could adopt, and it is very different from the approach of countries that have chosen either full prohibition or open access with taxation. India sits in the middle of that spectrum today, and the Russian example is a useful reference point for where the spectrum's restrictive end actually looks like in practice.

For Indian readers, the more immediate watch item is domestic: whether the ED's FEMA investigations lead to new rules for Indian exchanges, and whether the government clarifies its long-pending position on crypto regulation. Those developments would affect Indian buyers directly, in a way the Russian directive does not.

## The Bottom Line

From 17 November 2026, ordinary Russians can buy crypto on regulated exchanges for the first time — but only Bitcoin, Ether and USDT, only after passing a knowledge test, and only up to about ₹3.46 lakh a year through each broker. Qualified investors face no cap and no asset list, the Moscow Exchange plans a pilot from 1 December, and peer-to-peer trading will be banned from 1 July 2027. It is a system that controls what you can buy and how much of it, rather than taxing what you earn.

India has chosen the opposite control point. There is no purchase cap and no approved-coin list, but gains are taxed at 31.2% with no loss set-off, every sale attracts 1% TDS, exchanges must register with FIU-IND, and FEMA enforcement is actively targeting offshore routing. Neither system is light-touch; they simply place the friction in different places.

For an Indian investor, the Russian story is not a signal to buy or sell anything. It is a comparison that makes India's own rules easier to understand — and a reminder that the freedom to buy any coin, in any amount, comes with the responsibility to pay the tax, keep the records, and use a registered exchange. That is the deal India offers. Russia has now offered a different one.
