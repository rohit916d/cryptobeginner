# Why Bitcoin, Ethereum and XRP Cost 11.6% More in India

If you checked the price of Bitcoin in India on 19 November 2026, you may have noticed something strange. The world price was about $63,461 — roughly ₹59.3 lakh at the rupee rate. But on Indian exchanges, one Bitcoin cost ₹66,18,797. That is a gap of about ₹6.9 lakh on a single coin, or 11.61% more than the world price. The same gap showed up across the market: Ethereum cost 11.59% more in India than abroad, and XRP cost 10.96% more. These are the highest readings since The Crypto Times began publishing its India premium index on 1 October 2026 — and the gap was still widening on the morning of 20 November, with live readings near 12%.

This guide explains what the India crypto premium is, how it is measured, why it has climbed to a record, and what it actually costs an Indian buyer — without predicting where any price goes next.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a market phenomenon; it does not recommend buying, selling or holding any asset.

## What Happened: The Numbers at a Glance

The figures below come from The Crypto Times India Premium Index, which fixes the premium once a day between 11:30 and 12:30 IST, using prices from CoinDCX, Coinbase and WazirX. The 19 November 2026 fix is the latest completed daily reading at the time of writing.

| Coin | India price at 19 Nov fix | World price | Premium |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bitcoin (BTC) | ₹66,18,796.61 | $63,461.45 | **+11.61%** |
| Ethereum (ETH) | ₹1,87,905.85 | $1,801.97 | **+11.59%** |
| XRP | ₹101.48 | ~$0.98 (implied) | **+10.96%** |
| USDT (stablecoin) | ₹103.18 | ~$1.00 | +10.54% |
| USDC (stablecoin) | — | ~$1.00 | +11.46% |

The dollar reference rate used for the fix was ₹93.4450, derived from the European Central Bank's euro reference rates of 18 November. The index is careful about what it measures: it compares the local order-book price with the global dollar price at a fixed time each trading day, so the number is a snapshot of the same moment, not a comparison of prices taken hours apart.

Two details make the 19 November reading stand out. First, the Bitcoin premium of 11.61% is a new series high — the 30-day range runs from a low of 2.68% on 12 October to this 11.61% figure, against a 30-day average of 5.91%. Second, the premium has risen fast: it was 10.75% the day before, and a week earlier, on 10 November, it was 7.09% for Ethereum. In other words, the gap has roughly doubled in about ten days.

The morning of 20 November brought no relief. Live indicative readings from the same index showed Bitcoin at +11.97% and Ethereum at +12.05% around 05:30 IST, with XRP at +11.41% at 05:29 IST. These are intraday samples, not daily fixes, but they show the premium holding at record levels rather than snapping back.

The stablecoins tell the same story. USDT fixed at +10.54% on 19 November, and USDC at +11.46%. Three days earlier, on 16 November, the USDC premium had crossed 11% for the first time and USDT had reached 10.29% — both records at the time. The Crypto Times noted that Indian stablecoin premiums were running about four times those in other markets it tracks. The premium is no longer a stablecoin story. It now covers the whole market.

## What Is the India Crypto Premium?

The premium is a simple idea with a precise definition. Take the global dollar price of a coin. Convert it to rupees at the dollar-rupee reference rate. Compare that with what the same coin actually costs on an Indian exchange. The percentage difference is the premium.

If Bitcoin trades at $63,461 globally and the rupee rate is ₹93.4450, the implied rupee price is about ₹59.3 lakh. If Indian exchanges charge ₹66.19 lakh, the buyer is paying 11.61% more than the world price implies. The premium is positive when Indians pay more than the global price, and it would be negative if they paid less.

It helps to understand what the premium is not. It is not an exchange fee, and it is not a tax. Exchange fees and the 1% TDS sit on top of the price; the premium is inside the price itself — it is the difference between the Indian market price and the global market price. It is also not a statement about where Bitcoin's price is going. The Crypto Times is explicit on this point: the index measures the plumbing of India's crypto market, not the direction of the market. A high premium does not mean Bitcoin will rise or fall. It means dollars and crypto are scarce onshore in India right now.

The premium also differs from coin to coin. On 19 November, Bitcoin and Ethereum were almost identical at 11.61% and 11.59%, while XRP was a little lower at 10.96%. Stablecoins, which are designed to track the dollar, had their own readings — USDT at 10.54% and USDC at 11.46%. The small differences reflect how deep each coin's Indian order book is. Bitcoin has the deepest books; thinner coins can swing further. But on 19 November, the striking fact was how similar they all were: everything traded roughly 11% above the world price.

## How the Premium Got to a Record

The premium did not jump to 11.61% overnight. It climbed in stages through November, and each stage was visible in the daily fixes.

On 12 October, the Bitcoin premium was 2.68% — the low of the series. Through late October it stayed in the low single digits. By 5 November, the USDT premium had reached 5.97%, with one USDT costing ₹100.18 against a dollar reference of ₹94.64. That was the subject of our earlier guide on the USDT premium, when a 6% gap already looked large.

Then the pace quickened. On 16 November, the USDC premium crossed 11% for the first time and USDT reached 10.29%, both new records. The same day, intraday readings showed Bitcoin at 11.41%, XRP at 11.32% and Ethereum at 10.89% — but those were only indicative samples, not official fixes, and the published daily fixes for Bitcoin and XRP at that point were still dated 6 and 5 November. The gap between the intraday samples and the older fixes was itself a sign of how fast the market was moving.

Independent reporting confirmed the same picture from a different angle. On 17 November, the Sunday Guardian reported that Bitcoin was trading near $64,527 globally — about ₹60.38 lakh on conversion trackers — while domestic Indian exchanges were listing it between ₹66.35 lakh and ₹67.86 lakh. Its explanation matched the index's own: domestic platforms run on local rupee liquidity pools, where supply constraints, TDS rules and local demand push prices above the global conversion. Two different sources, measuring the same market in different ways, found the same roughly 11% gap.

On 18 November, the Bitcoin premium fixed at 10.75%. On 19 November, it fixed at 11.61% — a new series high. Ethereum followed the same path: 7.09% a week earlier, 10.88% on 18 November, 11.59% on 19 November. The 30-day average of 5.91% now looks like a distant memory. The premium has roughly doubled in ten days, and the whole market — Bitcoin, Ethereum, XRP and both major stablecoins — is trading at a double-digit premium at the same time.

## Why Indians Pay More: The Four Drivers

The Crypto Times' own explanation for the premium is straightforward: India buys more crypto than it sells, and a 1% tax on every transfer, a 30% tax on gains and cautious banking limit how much new supply arrives. That is the core of it, but it helps to break the mechanism into its parts.

**1. More buyers than sellers onshore.** India is a net buyer of crypto. When demand on Indian exchanges exceeds the crypto actually available there, the local price rises above the world price until buyers stop buying or sellers bring in more supply. The premium is the price of that imbalance. It is the market's way of rationing a limited local supply among many local buyers.

**2. The 1% TDS and the 30% tax.** Every covered crypto transfer in India attracts 1% tax deducted at source, and gains are taxed at a flat 30%. These rules do not just take money — they change behaviour. Professional traders who once supplied liquidity to Indian exchanges moved much of their activity offshore after the TDS was introduced, because the tax applies to every transaction, not just to profits. Fewer professional traders means thinner order books, and thinner order books mean a bigger gap between the Indian price and the world price. The tax rules are the same as they were a month ago, but their effect grows when the market is stressed.

**3. Cautious banking and limited on-ramps.** Indian banks have long been cautious about crypto, and the routes for moving rupees into crypto — and crypto back into rupees — are narrow. When the banking channel is strained, fresh supply cannot flow in quickly to close the gap. Recent weeks have underlined the fragility: Coinbase's INR withdrawals were halted for days in November because of an outage at its banking partner, and only partially resumed on 17–18 November through an alternative route. When the pipes are narrow, any surge in demand shows up as a premium rather than as volume.

**4. Stress widens the gap.** The premium tends to grow when markets are stressed, and November has been stressful. Bitcoin fell from around $75,000 in early October to about $62,000 by mid-November, and the US Federal Reserve's October meeting minutes, released on 19 November, showed officials expecting more rate hikes. In a sell-off, Indian holders who want to exit and global traders who want to buy both need the same narrow channels, and the premium is what clears the queue. The 15 November low of $62,006 — Bitcoin's weakest in four months — came with the premium already elevated, and the gap has widened further since.

None of these drivers is new on its own. What is new is that all four are pointing the same way at the same time, and the result is a record premium across every major coin at once.

## What the Premium Actually Costs You

A percentage is abstract. The rupee cost is not. At the 19 November fix, one Bitcoin cost ₹66,18,797 in India against an implied world price of about ₹59,30,000. The difference is roughly ₹6.9 lakh on a single coin. For Ethereum, one ETH cost ₹1,87,906 against an implied price of about ₹1,68,385 — roughly ₹19,500 extra per coin.

Most Indian buyers are not buying a whole Bitcoin. The maths scales down, and it is worth doing before any purchase:

| Amount you spend | Crypto you get at world price | Crypto you get at 11.61% premium | What the premium costs you |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ₹10,000 | ₹10,000 worth | ~₹8,960 worth | ~₹1,040 |
| ₹50,000 | ₹50,000 worth | ~₹44,800 worth | ~₹5,200 |
| ₹1,00,000 | ₹1,00,000 worth | ~₹89,600 worth | ~₹10,400 |
| ₹5,00,000 | ₹5,00,000 worth | ~₹4,48,000 worth | ~₹52,000 |

The calculation is simple: divide your rupees by 1.1161 to find what they buy at the world price. At a 6% premium — the level the USDT premium sat at in early November — ₹1,00,000 bought about ₹94,300 worth of crypto. At 11.61%, it buys about ₹89,600. The premium has nearly doubled the hidden cost of buying in two weeks.

That cost sits on top of everything else. Exchange fees, the spread between buy and sell prices, and the 1% TDS all apply as well, and the 30% tax on gains applies when you sell at a profit. The premium is also paid on the way in and, in reverse, on the way out: if you sell when the premium is high, you receive more rupees than the world price implies, which partly offsets the cost of buying at a premium. But that only helps if you sell while the premium is still high, and the premium moves every day. It was 2.68% on 12 October. Nobody can count on it staying at 11.61%.

## How This Differs From the USDT Premium

Readers who followed our 6 November guide on the USDT premium may wonder whether this is the same story. It is related, but it is not the same.

The USDT premium measures the price of digital dollars in India: how much more than ₹94.64 you pay for one USDT. It matters because USDT is the on-ramp most Indian traders use — you buy USDT, then swap it for Bitcoin or Ethereum. When the USDT premium was 6%, every trade that passed through USDT carried a 6% hidden cost, whatever coin you ended up holding.

The new story is broader. The 19 November fixes show that Bitcoin, Ethereum and XRP themselves now carry premiums of 11% or more, on top of whatever the USDT premium is. The gap has spread from the dollar substitute to the coins themselves, and the premium on the coins is now larger than the premium on USDT was two weeks ago. The whole market is trading at a premium, not just the entry point.

There is one more difference worth noting. The USDT premium is partly a story about dollars: it measures how scarce dollars are onshore in India. The Bitcoin and Ethereum premiums are partly a story about the coins themselves: they measure how scarce those coins are on Indian exchanges, given local demand, local order books and local rules. The two premiums usually move together, because the same forces — taxes, banking caution, capital controls — drive both. But they are not the same number, and on 19 November the coin premiums were the bigger of the two.

## What to Check Before You Buy

The premium is a cost, not a verdict. Here is how to handle it practically.

**Compare before you buy.** Before placing an order, check the global dollar price of the coin and multiply it by the current rupee rate. Then compare that figure with the price on your exchange. The difference, as a percentage, is the premium you are about to pay. If the premium is 11%, you are paying 11% more than the world price. The Crypto Times publishes a daily fix for Bitcoin, Ethereum, XRP, USDT and USDC; the Sunday Guardian and other outlets also publish Indian exchange prices alongside global ones.

**Check more than one exchange.** The index uses three exchanges — CoinDCX, Coinbase and WazirX — and caps each one's weight at 40%, precisely because prices differ between platforms. At a 02:19 IST sample on 20 November, CoinDCX was the cheapest place to buy Bitcoin at ₹65,03,116, about 0.3% below Coinbase. A few minutes of comparison can save real money, especially on larger orders.

**Remember the round trip.** The premium is a cost when you buy, and a benefit when you sell — but only if it is still high when you sell. If you buy at an 11.61% premium and sell when the premium has fallen to 5%, you have paid the difference. Factor that into any plan, and do not assume the premium will stay where it is.

**Do not chase it, and do not pay to escape it.** A high premium is not a reason to buy quickly, and it is not a reason to use unregulated workarounds. Peer-to-peer deals, cash transactions and offshore accounts that promise to dodge the premium carry their own risks — fraud, frozen funds and tax complications — and recent enforcement cases show that Indian agencies are actively tracing exactly these routes. Use FIU-IND registered exchanges and platforms, pay through traceable channels, and keep records of every transaction.

**Keep the tax in mind.** Buying crypto in India has tax consequences that do not change with the premium. Gains are taxed at a flat 30% plus a 4% cess, with 1% TDS on covered transfers, and losses cannot be set off against other income. The premium changes what you pay for the coin; the tax rules decide what you owe on the gain. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.
