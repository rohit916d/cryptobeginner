## DOJ's Largest Scam Crackdown: 800+ Crypto Fraud Defendants

The US Justice Department has announced what it calls the largest-ever crackdown on scammers — and cryptocurrency investment fraud is at the centre of it. On 24 November 2026, the department said prosecutors have taken action in more than 400 cases against more than 800 defendants since 6 March 2026, when Executive Order 14390 ordered a government-wide crackdown on predatory schemes. The cases span 85 federal districts, and the crypto line items in the announcement are striking: more than $1 billion in stolen cryptocurrency frozen, more than 1.4 million fraudulent websites and accounts taken down, and a dedicated strike force that has restrained about $1.08 billion in crypto from Southeast Asian scam centres alone.

The announcement, carried the same day by the Justice Department itself and reported on 25–26 November by Bitcoin Magazine, CryptoNews, Infosecurity Magazine and BlockchainReporter, is not a single raid. It is a scoreboard for eight months of coordinated enforcement — new charges, extraditions, guilty verdicts and sentences, counted together. That distinction matters, and this guide explains what was actually announced, how the scam centres behind the crypto cases work, why the same model keeps appearing in Indian fraud cases, and what Indian users should do if they are targeted.

## What the DOJ Announced on 24 November

The surge began with Executive Order 14390, signed on 6 March 2026, which directed US agencies to coordinate against criminal scam networks and online fraud. The Justice Department's Criminal Division put together a dedicated team of prosecutors, and the results announced on 24 November 2026 (Justice Department release 26-1404) cover the period from March to the present. The headline figures, all attributed to the department's announcement, are:

| Figure | What it covers |
|---|---|
| 400+ cases | Cases in which prosecutors took action — new charges, extraditions, guilty verdicts or sentences |
| 800+ defendants | People charged, extradited, convicted or sentenced across those cases |
| 85 federal districts | The districts where the actions were taken |
| 30+ defendants | Charged in the month leading up to the announcement alone |
| 30 defendants | Extradited to the United States |
| ~$17 billion | Illicit funds seized by the FBI under Operation Blackout |
| $1 billion+ | Stolen cryptocurrency frozen with help from US-supported operations in Southeast Asia |
| 1.4 million+ | Fraudulent websites and accounts taken down |
| 29+ scam compounds | Disrupted or dismantled across the Middle East, Africa and Asia |
| 2,200+ people | Freed and returned after being trafficked into scam compounds |

The announcement names several kinds of scam: cryptocurrency investment scams, financially motivated sextortion, government imposter schemes, and other predatory schemes that, in the department's words, collectively cost Americans billions of dollars, with many families losing their life savings. The crypto cases are the ones with the biggest numbers attached, and they are the ones this guide focuses on — but the crackdown is broader than crypto, and the same networks often run several scam types from the same compounds.

One caveat belongs at the top, because the headline number invites misunderstanding. The "800+ defendants" figure counts people at different stages — some newly charged, some extradited, some convicted, some sentenced — not 800 convictions. An indictment is an allegation, and every defendant is presumed innocent until proven guilty. The release is, as one analysis of it put it, a scoreboard rather than a case file: it totals eight months of enforcement, and the crypto-specific freeze is only one line inside a much larger set of figures.

## Operation Blackout: The FBI's Scam-Centre Demolition Drive

The enforcement engine behind the numbers is Operation Blackout, the FBI's global counter-scam demolition initiative. FBI Director Kash Patel said the bureau has made dismantling predatory scam enterprises a top priority, using what he called a whole-of-government approach. Under Operation Blackout, the FBI says it has seized nearly $17 billion in illicit funds, taken more than 14,000 digital devices, directly warned more than 11,000 Americans that they were being targeted by a scammer, and prevented more than $690 million in potential victim losses.

The operation also works on the ground. The FBI says it has helped disrupt or dismantle more than 29 scam compounds across the Middle East, Africa and Asia, and helped free and return more than 2,200 people who had been trafficked into those compounds. That last figure is a reminder of what these centres are: not offices full of willing employees, but compounds where trafficked workers are forced to run scams under threat, contacting victims around the world from behind fake profiles.

Two supporting programmes feed into the same effort. Operation Level Up, the FBI's victim-warning programme, has prevented more than $143 million in victim losses since March, warned thousands of Americans that they were being targeted, and frozen more than $350 million in assets through the FBI's Recovery Asset Team. And the Justice Department's Scam Center Strike Force, formed in 2025 specifically to go after Southeast Asian crypto fraud, says its crypto seizure team has restrained about $1.08 billion in cryptocurrency from these schemes.

The State Department's contribution is the other crypto-specific line item. US-funded advisers train foreign investigators, and the department credits that programme with helping freeze more than $1 billion in stolen cryptocurrency, taking down more than 1.4 million fraudulent websites and accounts, and analysing more than 10,000 devices and hard drives in Thailand alone. The Secret Service reports more than $200 million in restrained illicit funds and more than $119 million recovered across more than 1,540 business email compromise investigations, plus 1,780 disrupted scam domains. Homeland Security Investigations counts about $120 million in seizures, restraints, freezes and recoveries.

## How the Southeast Asian Crypto Scam Centres Work

The crypto cases in the crackdown follow a pattern investigators call pig-butchering — fattening a victim up with trust before the slaughter — and it is worth understanding step by step, because recognising the script early is the only reliable defence.

**Step one is contact.** A stranger reaches out on WhatsApp, Telegram, Instagram, a dating app or a job site. Sometimes the approach is romantic; sometimes it is a job offer or a friendly wrong-number message that turns into a conversation. The person behind the profile is usually a trafficked worker in a scam compound, following a script and handling dozens of victims at once.

**Step two is trust.** Over days or weeks, the scammer builds a relationship. They share photos, daily life, small personal details — all fabricated. Eventually they mention, casually, that they invest in crypto and have been doing very well, and offer to show the victim how.

**Step three is the fake platform.** The victim is directed to a professional-looking trading website or app. The victim deposits crypto — often USDT — and the platform shows impressive, steadily rising profits. Small early withdrawals are sometimes allowed, precisely to build confidence. The numbers on screen are fake; the deposits go straight to wallets controlled by the scam centre.

**Step four is the squeeze.** When the victim tries to withdraw a large amount, the platform invents a problem: a tax payment, a verification fee, a margin call. Each payment is presented as the last one needed before the full balance is released. Victims who keep paying lose more; victims who stop lose everything they already deposited. Then the platform, the website and the contact all disappear.

The Secret Service's most detailed case in the announcement follows exactly this script: a cryptocurrency investment conspiracy run from scam centres in Cambodia that targeted at least 174 US victims. Other cases named in the announcement show the range of the fraud. In the Northern District of Illinois, one indictment accuses the creator of a digital asset of operating a $9 million "rug pull" fraud scheme. Another man was sentenced to 23 years in federal prison for defrauding investors of more than $20 million in a cryptocurrency scam. And Brent C. Kovar was convicted by a jury on 24 August 2026 of fraudulently obtaining $24 million from at least 400 investor victims; his sentencing is scheduled for 30 November 2026.

## Why This Matters for Indian Users

This is an American announcement about American prosecutions, so it is fair to ask what it has to do with Indian readers. The answer is that the scam centres it targets are the same networks that run the frauds Indian police keep busting — and the Indian cases of the last month read like a local edition of the same story.

In Chennai, the Enforcement Directorate arrested two people on 30 October 2026 in a cyber-fraud case involving more than ₹400 crore, over 600 complaints, mule bank accounts and cryptocurrency seized from the accused. In Andhra Pradesh, the CID busted a ₹15 crore fraud in which a Tenali victim was cheated through 27 transactions in Bitcoin, Solana and Dogecoin, with funds routed through a website called Honeycombapi.org and 15 Bitcoin transactions sent to four Gate.io accounts; eight people were arrested across Hyderabad, Maharashtra, China, Malaysia and Portugal. And on 24 November 2026 — the same day as the DOJ announcement — Delhi Police announced the arrest of four people, including an alleged mastermind from Alwar, in a Binance P2P phishing bust.

The pattern in all three Indian cases is the same one the DOJ describes: victims lured online, money moved in crypto, mule accounts used to convert it, and a network that spans borders. Indian users are targeted by the same Southeast Asian centres, not just by local operators. The centres run their scripts in many languages, and India — with a large English-speaking, crypto-curious population and a fast-growing base of first-time investors — is a prime target market.

There is also a practical connection. The same enforcement tools the DOJ is celebrating — freezing crypto at exchanges, tracing funds across chains, taking down fake websites — are the tools Indian investigators use when they act on a complaint. Every Indian complaint that includes wallet addresses and transaction IDs adds to the trail. The crackdown does not make Indian users safe on its own, but it does mean the networks targeting them are under more pressure than they have been in years, and that the fake platforms and websites victims report are more likely to be taken down.

On the tax side, nothing about this announcement changes Indian rules. Crypto income is taxed at a flat 30% (plus applicable surcharge and 4% cess) under Section 115BBH, with 1% TDS under Section 194S on transfers above the specified thresholds, and losses cannot be set off against other income. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. Victims should also know that losses from a scam do not become tax-deductible simply because the money was stolen; keep records and consult a tax professional rather than assuming.

## How to Spot the Scam Before You Pay

The crackdown is good news, but enforcement alone will not stop these scams. New compounds open, new websites appear, and the same scripts get reused with different names. The defence that actually works is recognising the pattern early. These are the red flags investigators repeat most often:

- **A stranger brings up crypto investing first.** Real investment opportunities do not arrive as a direct message from someone you have never met, however friendly the conversation has been.
- **Fixed daily profits that never fall.** Any platform promising fixed daily profits, or showing returns that never fall, is lying. Crypto markets are volatile; a chart that only goes up is a drawing, not a market.
- **Pressure to move the conversation off the platform.** Scammers want you on WhatsApp or Telegram, where there is no moderation and no record the dating app or job site can see.
- **A platform you cannot verify independently.** If the exchange or trading site is not a well-known, FIU-IND-registered platform in India, and you cannot find independent reviews or a real company behind it, do not deposit anything.
- **Withdrawal fees, taxes or "verification" payments.** A genuine platform never asks you to pay a fee to release your own money. Every such demand is the scam's final step, not a real requirement.
- **"Recovery" offers after a loss.** If someone contacts you offering to recover stolen crypto for an upfront fee, that is a second scam targeting the same victims. Legitimate recovery happens through police and exchanges, not through a stranger on Telegram.

A simple rule covers most cases: never send crypto to a person or a platform you found through an unsolicited message, and never let a stranger — however charming, however knowledgeable they seem — choose where your money goes.

## What to Do If You Have Been Targeted

If you have already sent money, speed matters. The faster a complaint is filed, the better the chance that an exchange freezes the receiving wallet before the funds move on.

First, stop sending money immediately and preserve everything: screenshots of the chats, the platform's web address, wallet addresses, transaction IDs and any payment receipts. Second, call the National Cyber Crime helpline **1930** or report at **cybercrime.gov.in**, and file a complaint at your local police station with the evidence you preserved. Third, tell your bank and the exchange you used, and ask them to flag the receiving accounts. Fourth, warn the people around you — these scams spread through referrals, and the scammer may already be talking to your contacts.

Do not pay anyone who promises to recover the funds for a fee, and do not trust "recovery" services advertised on social media. If an exchange or investigator contacts you, verify their identity through official channels before sharing any information. And if the amount is large, consider speaking to a lawyer who handles cybercrime cases — the legal routes available, including the property and trust arguments Indian courts have begun to recognise for crypto holdings, depend heavily on the facts of the individual case.

## What the Crackdown Does Not Change

It is worth being honest about the limits of this announcement, because the headlines can mislead in both directions.

First, the crackdown is a prosecution programme, not a new regulation. It does not change the rules for crypto in India or anywhere else, and it does not make any platform safer by itself. Second, the 800-defendant figure counts people at very different stages, and many cases will take months or years to resolve; some defendants may be acquitted, and some may never be extradited. Third, the scam centres are adaptable. The FBI says it disrupted 29 compounds, but the model — trafficked labour, fake platforms, crypto payments — has survived every crackdown so far, and new centres can be set up faster than old ones are closed. Fourth, the $1 billion-plus in frozen crypto is a fraction of what these networks have stolen over the years, and most victims will never see their money again.

None of that makes the announcement unimportant. It is the largest coordinated action against these networks ever reported, and the figures — $17 billion seized by the FBI, $1 billion in crypto frozen, 1.4 million fake sites taken down — show that enforcement has moved from individual cases to dismantling the infrastructure itself. But the honest reading is that this is a blow against the networks, not their end. The practical lesson for Indian users is unchanged: the scams will keep coming, the scripts will keep working on new victims, and the only reliable protection is recognising the pattern before any money moves.
