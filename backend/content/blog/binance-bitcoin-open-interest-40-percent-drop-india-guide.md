## Bitcoin's Biggest Leverage Unwind Ever — $4.5 Billion Gone

Bitcoin's speculative engine is losing steam, and the scale of the retreat is historic. Over the past year, open interest in Bitcoin futures on Binance — the world's largest crypto exchange by trading volume — has fallen by roughly 40%, or about $4.5 billion, according to CryptoQuant analyst Darkfost. In dollar terms, it is the biggest deleveraging event in Bitcoin's history.

The numbers were reported on 24 November 2026 by TradingKey, CryptoNews and CoinMarketCap, all drawing on the same CryptoQuant data. And the unwind accelerated on Monday 23 November, when the figure rose to almost $4.7 billion as positions unravelled after Nvidia announced that co-founder Jensen Huang would be returning as president and CEO — the news that briefly pushed Bitcoin above $64,000 before it fell back below $61,000.

This guide explains what open interest actually measures, how the $4.5 billion unwind happened, how it compares with the great deleveraging of 2022, and what Indian crypto users should take from it — without pretending anyone can predict what happens next.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. Leveraged trading can wipe out your entire margin in minutes.

## What Open Interest Actually Measures

Open interest is one of the most misunderstood numbers in crypto. It is not trading volume, and it is not the amount of Bitcoin held on an exchange. It is the total value of all outstanding futures contracts — bets on Bitcoin's future price, made with borrowed money — that have been opened but not yet closed or settled.

Every futures trade has two sides: a buyer and a seller. When a new contract is opened, open interest rises by the value of that contract. When a contract is closed — because the trader took a profit, cut a loss, or was liquidated — open interest falls. So a rising open interest means new leveraged bets are being placed faster than old ones are being closed. A falling open interest means traders are stepping back, closing positions, or being forced out.

That is why open interest is best read as a gauge of speculative appetite, not of price direction. High open interest means a lot of borrowed money is riding on Bitcoin's next move, which makes the market more explosive in both directions: a sharp move can trigger a chain of liquidations that pushes the price further, in what this site has previously explained as a liquidation cascade. Low and falling open interest means the opposite — fewer leveraged positions to liquidate, so less fuel for a cascade, but also fewer buyers standing by with borrowed money.

Binance matters here because of its sheer size. The exchange accounts for more than 34% of total Bitcoin open interest worldwide, according to the same CryptoQuant data, which makes its numbers a reliable stand-in for speculative demand across all venues. When Binance's open interest falls 40%, it is not one exchange's story — it is the global market stepping back from leverage.

## How the $4.5 Billion Unwind Happened

The decline did not happen in a single day. Darkfost's data, published on X on 22 November, shows a steady year-long retreat: Binance's Bitcoin open interest fell from about $10.6 billion to about $8.8 billion in the month before the post alone — a 17% monthly decline — and the one-year change reached roughly minus $4.5 billion, or about 40%.

In rupee terms, that is roughly ₹42,300 crore of leveraged exposure removed from a single exchange in twelve months, at an exchange rate of about ₹94 to the dollar. The July peak of the contraction was even larger: at one point the decline from the peak reached $6.25 billion, exceeding 45%, which CryptoQuant describes as Bitcoin's largest deleveraging event in dollar terms.

The unwind has been punctuated by violent days rather than a smooth slide. At least eight trading days in 2026 have seen more than $1.5 billion in liquidations across the crypto market. At Binance alone, liquidations totalled $1.4 billion in August, $1.1 billion in September and $1.3 billion in October — each month's forced selling feeding the next leg of the open-interest decline, because every liquidation closes a contract and removes it from the open-interest total.

Monday 23 November showed the mechanism in miniature. Nvidia's announcement that Jensen Huang would return as CEO sent global stock markets soaring — the Nasdaq rose more than 6% — and Bitcoin briefly surged above $64,000. But the rally did not hold. Within an hour, about $377 million worth of crypto short positions were liquidated, according to Coinglass data, with Bitcoin shorts accounting for roughly $180 million. Then the price slid back below $61,000, the shorts that had just been wiped out were replaced by fresh selling, and the open-interest decline pushed towards $4.7 billion.

The same day's derivatives data tells the rest of the story. Total crypto liquidations on 24 November reached about $725.8 million in 24 hours, with Bitcoin accounting for about $368.5 million of it, according to derivatives tracking — and the Fear and Greed Index stood at 11, classified as Extreme Fear. Traders are not just closing positions; they are doing so in a market where confidence is badly damaged.

## Why This Unwind Is Different From 2022

The natural comparison is the last great deleveraging, in 2022, when the Terra stablecoin collapsed in May, lender Celsius failed in June, and hedge fund Three Arrows Capital followed in July. That episode is still the benchmark for how fast leverage can leave crypto — and, by one measure, it remains worse than today.

According to Darkfost, during the height of the 2022 bear market, Bitcoin's one-year open interest change was more than minus 53% — a bigger percentage fall than the roughly 40% recorded now. The difference is the dollar scale: Bitcoin's market is far larger today than it was in 2022, so a smaller percentage fall now removes more dollars of leverage than the deeper percentage fall did then. That is what makes the current unwind the largest in dollar terms even though it is not the largest in percentage terms.

There is a second difference, and it cuts the other way. In 2022, the deleveraging was driven by the collapse of household-name crypto firms — lenders and funds that blew up and took their leverage with them. In 2026, the drivers are different: a $6 billion hack of Binance itself on 29 October, which knocked confidence across the market; accelerating outflows from US spot Bitcoin ETFs, with about $1.8 billion drained last week alone and roughly $4.8 billion pulled since 30 October, according to Bloomberg data; and a broader November correction that has taken Bitcoin down about 20% in the month, to a two-year low of about $55,254 over the weekend, and to its seventh consecutive weekly decline.

In other words, 2022 was a deleveraging caused by crypto's own failures. The 2026 unwind is being driven by a security shock, institutional selling and macro pressure arriving together — which is why it has been steadier and longer, rather than concentrated in a few catastrophic weeks.

## The Other Numbers Pointing the Same Way

Open interest is not the only gauge flashing the same signal. The rest of the market's plumbing shows the same pattern of money leaving the system:

**Stablecoin supply is shrinking.** Glassnode's "Running on Empty" report, covered on this site on 12 November, found stablecoin supply — the dollars sitting on the sidelines in crypto, ready to buy — had contracted by $14.2 billion in 30 days, the fastest since June 2022. Holders were converting stablecoins back into dollars and leaving the system, rather than rotating into other coins.

**ETF outflows are accelerating.** US-listed spot Bitcoin ETFs recorded roughly $1.8 billion in net redemptions last week alone, the most since June, bringing total outflows since late October to nearly $4.8 billion. This site covered the record $1.79 billion weekly outflow on 23 November, and the BlackRock IBIT fund alone saw a record $541 million single-day outflow. ETFs were the main source of new buying in 2024 and 2025; their reversal removes a key source of price support.

**Funding rates have turned negative.** The annualised funding rate — the fee leveraged traders pay to keep positions open — averaged about minus 1.1% across the major exchanges on 24 November, meaning short sellers were paying long holders, a sign that bearish positioning dominates. On Binance, about 73.46% of accounts were long, yet liquidations since the start of the day had mostly hit longs — the classic sign of a market where the crowd is positioned one way and the price keeps going the other.

**The price itself tells the story.** Bitcoin has lost more than half its value since reaching a peak above $126,000 in October 2025, and it failed to hold its Nvidia-driven rally even as global stocks soared, falling as much as 3% to $59,404 in Asia on Tuesday 24 November. At Indian prices, Bitcoin was trading near ₹56 lakh, with local exchanges showing a premium above the global spot conversion — the same India premium this site has tracked through the correction.

None of these numbers predicts what happens next. But together they describe a market where speculative money is leaving, buyers are scarce, and the leverage that once amplified every rally has largely been removed.

## What Less Leverage Means for the Market

For beginners, the practical question is what a deleveraged market actually behaves like. The honest answer is that it cuts both ways, and both sides matter.

On the positive side, a market with less leverage is a market with less fuel for liquidation cascades. The violent chain reactions this site has covered — the $356 million liquidation wave in late October, the short squeeze that wiped out $974.8 million in leveraged positions in early November — happen because leveraged positions get closed automatically when prices move against them, and each forced sale pushes the price further. With roughly $4.5 billion less leverage on Binance alone, the next sharp move has fewer automatic sellers behind it. In that sense, the unwind is the market healing itself: the excess is being removed, painfully but thoroughly.

On the negative side, leverage is also buying power. When traders borrow to buy, they add demand; when they close those positions, that demand disappears. A deleveraged market is typically a thinner market — wider spreads between buy and sell prices, sharper moves on modest selling, and less cushion when bad news arrives. Market strategist Scott Freeman of Delphi Digital made the same point about the broader sell-off, arguing it reflects a shortage of liquidity: "This isn't just a crypto story or an AI story. It's a liquidity story. The margin of safety offered by liquidity is low, so when something breaks there's no cushion."

There is also a subtlety worth understanding: falling open interest does not mean traders have stopped trading. Volume on 24 November was up more than 70% on the day even as open interest fell, according to CoinMarketCap's snapshot — traders were active, but they were closing positions and trading spot rather than opening new leveraged bets. Activity and leverage are different things, and right now the market has plenty of the first and much less of the second.

## What This Means for Indian Crypto Users

For Indian users, the Binance deleveraging story carries three practical lessons — none of which involves predicting the price.

**First, understand the leverage you are actually using.** Many Indian traders use futures and perpetual contracts on offshore exchanges, where leverage of 10x, 20x or even 50x is offered with a few taps. At 10x leverage, a 10% move against you wipes out your entire margin. The $4.5 billion unwind is, in large part, the bill for exactly that maths: thousands of traders whose positions were closed automatically as the market fell. If you trade with leverage, know your liquidation price before you open the position — not after.

**Second, the India premium and the tax bill do not care about leverage.** At Indian prices, Bitcoin was trading near ₹56 lakh on 24 November, with local exchanges showing a premium of more than 12% above the global spot conversion — a gap driven by isolated INR liquidity pools. And whatever you gain or lose, the tax treatment is the same: gains from transferring virtual digital assets are taxed at a flat 30% (plus applicable surcharge and 4% cess) under Section 115BBH, with 1% TDS under Section 194S on transactions above the specified thresholds. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. Leverage does not change the tax rate; it only changes how fast the gain or loss arrives.

**Third, exchange risk is not just about hacks.** The Binance story of the past month has been dominated by the 29 October hack, but the deleveraging data shows a quieter risk: when an exchange's derivatives market shrinks by 40%, the exchange itself is under stress, and stressed exchanges can change margin rules, raise fees or restrict withdrawals with little notice. Indian users learned a version of this lesson with CoinDCX's futures curb in late October, when certain contracts went reduce-only. Keeping only trading capital on an exchange, and moving the rest to a wallet you control, is the standard protection — and it matters more, not less, when the market is deleveraging.

One more thing worth noting: the US regulator moved the same week. On 23 November, the Commodity Futures Trading Commission issued staff guidance allowing some regulated derivatives firms to count Bitcoin, Ether and qualifying stablecoins towards the resources covering twelve months of operating expenses, with important liquidity limits. It is a narrow, technical step — not a green light for retail leverage — but it shows regulators are now treating crypto as part of the plumbing of regulated markets rather than something outside them. For Indian users, the practical takeaway is that the rules around crypto derivatives are still being written, in India and abroad, and they can change quickly.

## The Bottom Line

Bitcoin's leverage is unwinding at a historic pace in dollar terms: about $4.5 billion gone from Binance in a year, a 40% fall in open interest, with the decline reaching almost $4.7 billion after Monday's Nvidia-driven whipsaw. The percentage fall is smaller than the 2022 wipeout, but the dollar amount is larger — because the market itself is larger, and because the drivers this time are a security shock, institutional ETF selling and macro pressure arriving together.

What that means is not a prediction. It means the market has less borrowed money riding on the next move — less fuel for liquidation cascades, but also less buying power and thinner liquidity. For Indian users, the lesson is practical rather than speculative: understand your leverage and your liquidation price, remember the 30% tax and 1% TDS apply regardless of how the trade was funded, and keep only trading capital on exchanges.

The deleveraging will end when traders decide the risk is worth taking again. Until then, the most useful number to watch is not the price — it is how much borrowed money is standing behind it.
