A stablecoin is supposed to be the calm corner of crypto: one token, meant to be worth about one dollar, day after day. A stablecoin depeg is the moment its market price drifts away from the value it is designed to track. Most depegs are tiny and temporary; a few have been large, chaotic, and permanent. Understanding the difference is essential before you hold, send, or trade any stablecoin.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. Stablecoins are not bank deposits, and a peg is a target, not a guarantee.

## What a Peg Actually Is

A peg is a target price. A dollar-pegged stablecoin aims to trade at one US dollar through a mechanism, not magic. Fiat-backed stablecoins, such as USDT and USDC, are issued by companies holding reserves — cash, bank deposits, or short-term government bonds — and promising redemption at about one dollar each. Crypto-backed stablecoins are backed by other cryptocurrencies locked in smart contracts, usually overcollateralised to absorb price swings. Algorithmic stablecoins hold little or no collateral and rely on software that expands or shrinks supply to push the price back toward the peg.

None of these designs makes the peg automatic. It holds only while people believe redemptions will work, the backing is there, and the market stays liquid enough to close small gaps. When any pillar cracks, the price moves — and that movement is a depeg.

## Small Wobbles Versus a Real Depeg

Stablecoins rarely trade at exactly one dollar. A price of 99.9 cents or 100.1 cents is normal market noise, caused by fees, order-book gaps, and the time arbitrage traders take to act. Arbitrage normally restores the peg: if a token trades at 99 cents, a trader can buy it cheaply and redeem or sell it near one dollar, and that buying pressure pushes the price back up — and vice versa above the peg.

A real depeg looks different. The gap is larger, lasts longer, and widens instead of closing. Warning signs include a price several percent away from the peg, falling volume or widening spreads, and news that redemptions are delayed, capped, or paused.

| Situation | Typical price behaviour | What it usually means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Normal wobble | Within a fraction of a percent, recovers quickly | Ordinary friction, arbitrage working |
| Stress dip | A few percent below the peg, volatile | Market nervousness, thinning liquidity |
| Serious depeg | Well below the peg for days | Confidence in backing or mechanism has broken |

## Why Pegs Break: Five Common Causes

**1. Doubts about the reserves.** For a fiat-backed stablecoin, the peg rests on trust that the issuer really holds the reserves it claims. If an audit is delayed, a reserve report looks thin, or a partner bank runs into trouble, traders sell and more holders follow, pushing the price well below the peg until the issuer proves redemptions work.

**2. Redemption freezes or limits.** A stablecoin is only as good as its exit door. If the issuer pauses redemptions, limits them to large institutions, or faces banking problems that slow payouts, ordinary holders can only sell on exchanges, where buyers demand a discount for the uncertainty. A redemption announcement — even a temporary one — can trigger a depeg all by itself.

**3. Collateral crashes.** Crypto-backed stablecoins depend on collateral that is itself volatile. If that collateral falls sharply, the system must sell or liquidate positions to stay solvent. In a fast crash, liquidations cascade: falling collateral triggers more selling, which pushes prices lower still. Overcollateralisation buffers help, but no buffer is infinite.

**4. Algorithmic death spirals.** Algorithmic stablecoins are the most fragile design. They keep the peg by minting or burning a companion token as prices move. Under stress, this can invert: holders sell the stablecoin, the system mints ever more of the companion token, that token's price collapses, confidence evaporates, and both fall together. Some algorithmic stablecoins have lost their pegs entirely and never recovered.

**5. Liquidity panics.** Sometimes the trigger is not the stablecoin itself. A large exchange failure, a platform freezing withdrawals, or forced selling elsewhere can send everyone rushing for the exit at once. Buyers disappear, order books thin out, and even a sound stablecoin trades at a discount because nobody wants to buy mid-panic. The peg often recovers once panic passes — but sellers at the bottom have locked in the discount.

## What Holders Actually See During a Depeg

A depeg shows up in practical ways. On an exchange, the stablecoin's price falls below its intended value: USDT/INR may trade below the level implied by the dollar-rupee rate, withdrawals may slow or cost more, and spreads widen, so even a small trade costs more than usual.

Two prices matter here: the market price on an exchange right now, and the redemption price the issuer pays directly, usually about one dollar where redemptions are open. Normally they sit close together; during a depeg they can split apart, so checking both tells you far more than either alone.

## The Indian Angle: USDT, INR Pairs, and UPI

For Indian users, stablecoins usually arrive through INR pairs on domestic exchanges: deposit rupees via UPI or bank transfer, buy USDT or another stablecoin, and later sell it back for rupees. The USDT/INR price reflects not only the global dollar peg but also local demand, the rupee-dollar rate, and the exchange's own liquidity, so during stress the Indian price can deviate from the global price.

If you receive freelance payments in stablecoins, a depeg changes the rupee value of what you hold even if you never intended to speculate. If you send stablecoins elsewhere, double-check the network, address, and amount — a depeg is a bad moment to compound a price loss with an irreversible wrong-network transfer. Unusually high returns for holding a particular stablecoin are a question, not a selling point.

Converting a stablecoin back to rupees is a transfer of a virtual digital asset, so keep records of purchase price, sale price, dates, fees, and TDS deducted. Losses on crypto generally cannot be set off against other income under current rules; for complex cases, speak to a qualified tax professional.

## A Five-Minute Peg Check

Before holding a meaningful amount of any stablecoin, run through this quick check. Compare prices across two or three reputable sources — a gap everywhere is more serious than a gap on one platform. Check whether ordinary holders can still redeem; an open redemption channel is the strongest peg support there is. For fiat-backed tokens, check the latest reserve report and its auditor — vague or stale reporting is a warning sign. Check liquidity too: thin order books and wide spreads mean even a modest sale moves the price. Watch the issuer's communication, since silence during a crisis is itself information. Finally, confirm you hold the genuine token on the correct network, as fake tokens with similar names appear during every crisis. If several checks flash red, do not add new funds or chase a rebound; wait for clearer information.

## What Not to Do During a Depeg

Panic is the most expensive reaction. Selling into a thin, panicked market locks in the worst price of the day, and buying a deeply depegged token because it looks "cheap" is a bet that the peg will recover — a bet that has failed completely for some designs. Avoid borrowing to buy the dip, unfamiliar platforms promising rescue yields, and accounts claiming insider knowledge of a recovery. Scammers thrive during depegs, impersonating issuers with fake "redemption portals" that steal wallet approvals; no legitimate issuer will ask you to connect your wallet to a random link or send tokens to "verify" your holdings.

Instead, verify facts from primary sources: the issuer's announcements, your exchange's status page, and reputable price trackers. If you sell, do it deliberately — check the live price, fees, and spread before submitting. If you wait, review that choice calmly too: how long can you wait, and what would change your mind?

## Conclusion

A stablecoin depeg is a reminder that "stable" describes a design goal, not a guarantee. Small wobbles are normal and self-correcting; serious depegs happen when confidence in reserves, redemptions, collateral, or the mechanism breaks down. Know which design your stablecoin uses, check the peg and redemption channel before trusting it with meaningful money, keep records for tax purposes, and never treat a token's name or intended value as its actual price.
