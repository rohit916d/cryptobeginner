Coinbase — the largest crypto exchange in the United States — now runs a federally chartered trust company. The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) completed the chartering of Coinbase National Trust Company on Friday, 6 November 2026, and Coinbase announced the final approval on Monday, 9 November 2026. The decision puts the exchange's institutional custody business under direct federal banking supervision, and it makes Coinbase one of a small group of crypto companies to hold a US national trust bank charter, alongside Ripple, which received its final approval on 26 October 2026. This guide explains what the charter actually allows, what it does not allow, why the distinction matters, and what Indian readers should — and should not — take away from it.

## What Happened: The Timeline

The charter was not a sudden decision. It was the end of a process that took more than a year, and the dates matter because they show how the approval unfolded.

Coinbase applied for a national trust charter in October 2025. On 2 April 2026, the OCC granted preliminary conditional approval in Corporate Decision 1370, signed by Senior Deputy Comptroller Stephen A. Lybarger. That letter set the conditions the company had to meet before it could open, and it made clear that final authority to commence business would wait until the pre-opening requirements were satisfied. The preliminary approval would have expired if the trust company had not opened within 18 months.

The OCC's corporate applications record now lists control number 2025-Charter-343449 as consummated and effective on Friday, 6 November 2026. Coinbase announced the completed approval on Monday, 9 November, in a blog post by Rick Schonberg, confirming that the company had received final approval to charter Coinbase National Trust Company and could expand its custody business under a federal regulatory framework. The story was reported the same day by crypto.news, Cointelegraph, Unchained, CoinCentral and Stocktwits, all carrying the same dates and the same core facts.

One detail worth noting: the OCC has not published a separate November decision letter. The April conditional-approval letter remains the published decision document, and the November step is recorded in the OCC's applications file as the charter becoming effective. That is normal for this kind of process — the final step is the completion of the conditions set in April, not a new policy decision.

## What Is a National Trust Company?

A national trust company is a federally chartered financial institution whose business is holding and managing assets for other people, rather than taking deposits and making loans the way a commercial bank does. The OCC charters and supervises it, and the institution operates under federal law across the United States, rather than under the law of a single state.

That distinction is the whole point of the charter. Until now, Coinbase's custody business ran through Coinbase Custody Trust Company, a limited-purpose trust company regulated by the New York State Department of Financial Services. That is a serious regulator, but it is a state regulator. A federal charter moves the business under a single federal supervisor — the OCC — and gives it the same kind of standing that traditional trust banks have held for decades.

For an institution, the practical difference is significant. A state trust charter means dealing with state rules and, in practice, with the patchwork of state licensing that comes with serving clients across the country. A federal charter means one supervisor, one set of federal examination standards, and a framework that institutional clients — pension funds, asset managers, ETF issuers — already understand from the traditional finance world.

| Feature | Coinbase Custody Trust Company (New York) | Coinbase National Trust Company (federal) |
|---|---|---|
| Chartering authority | New York State Department of Financial Services | Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) |
| Supervisor | State regulator | Federal banking regulator |
| Scope | State trust charter | National trust charter, federal framework |
| Main business | Digital-asset custody | Fiduciary digital-asset custody, mainly institutional |
| FDIC insurance on client cash | No | No — client cash sits at outside banks |
| Can take retail deposits or make loans | No | No |
| Can issue stablecoins | No | No, under the current approved business plan |

## What the Charter Allows — and What It Does Not

The most important thing to understand about this charter is its limits. The headlines say "Coinbase wins a bank charter", and that is true in the technical sense. But the charter does not turn Coinbase into a conventional bank, and several of the things people assume a bank can do are explicitly excluded.

**What it allows.** Under the charter, Coinbase National Trust Company can provide fiduciary digital-asset custody services — holding crypto assets on behalf of clients, under OCC supervision, with the legal duties of a fiduciary. That is the core of the institutional custody business: exchanges, ETF issuers, asset managers and other large clients need somewhere regulated to hold their crypto, and a federally chartered trust company is one of the strongest answers available in the United States. Coinbase plans to move its existing New York custody business into the federal trust company over three years, while staking, prime trading and prime financing stay with its affiliates rather than moving into the trust company itself.

**What it does not allow.** The charter does not authorise the trust company to accept ordinary retail deposits, to make traditional loans, or to issue stablecoins under its current approved business plan. Client dollars are held in "for benefit of" accounts at outside banks, and those balances are not covered by FDIC deposit insurance through Coinbase National Trust Company. The charter also does not automatically give Coinbase direct access to Federal Reserve payment systems — that requires a separate application. And the OCC's April approval letter states that the company does not propose to issue stablecoins under its current business plan, which means any future stablecoin plans would need a fresh regulatory conversation.

**The conditions attached.** For the first three years, the trust company must hold at least $60 million in tier 1 capital and a separate pool of liquid assets equal to 180 days of wind-down expenses. Those conditions are designed to make sure the institution can keep operating — or wind down in an orderly way — even in a crisis. They are the same kind of prudential cushion that traditional trust banks are expected to maintain, and they are a reminder that a charter is not just a licence: it comes with capital, liquidity and examination obligations that last for the life of the institution.

## Why Custody Is the Heart of This Story

Custody sounds boring. It is the business of holding assets safely on behalf of someone else, and it rarely makes headlines. But in crypto, custody is where the industry's biggest questions live: who holds the keys, who is responsible if something goes wrong, and what happens to customer assets if a company fails.

The reason is simple. Crypto assets are bearer instruments in a practical sense — whoever controls the private keys controls the coins. When an exchange or a fund holds crypto for customers, the safety of those holdings depends entirely on the custodian's controls: how keys are generated and stored, who can move funds, how withdrawals are approved, and what happens if an employee goes rogue or a system is breached. The Binance security incident of 29 October 2026, in which around $6.3 billion of digital assets were stolen from hot wallets, is the most recent reminder of what is at stake — and it is no coincidence that the industry's response to that incident has included a renewed focus on custody standards.

Coinbase's custody business is large. CoinCentral reported that the company held $245.7 billion in custody assets as of 30 June 2025. And Coinbase Custody is the named custodian for most of the US spot Bitcoin ETFs — the exchange-traded funds that let ordinary investors get Bitcoin exposure through a brokerage account. That makes the quality of Coinbase's custody arrangements a matter of direct interest to millions of ETF investors, even if they have never heard of the OCC.

A federal trust charter does not make custody risk disappear. It does change the framework around it: the custodian is now examined by a federal banking regulator, must meet federal capital and liquidity standards, and operates under fiduciary duties that are enforceable in a way that a purely commercial contract is not. For the institutions that use Coinbase's custody — ETF issuers, asset managers, corporate treasuries — that framework is the point. It is what lets their own compliance teams and auditors treat the arrangement as equivalent to the custody they use for stocks and bonds.

## Ripple, Circle and the Wider Charter Wave

Coinbase is not the first crypto company to take this route, and the sequence of approvals tells its own story about where US regulation is heading.

Ripple received final OCC approval for its national trust bank on 26 October 2026, about two weeks before Coinbase's charter became effective. Circle, the issuer of the USDC stablecoin, has also secured an OCC approval for a national trust company, according to the same coverage. The pattern is clear: the largest crypto companies are moving their custody and settlement infrastructure into federally chartered entities, one by one.

That wave is not uncontested. The Independent Community Bankers of America (ICBA) sued the OCC on 2 October 2026, challenging the rules behind national trust bank charters for crypto companies. The community banks' argument, in essence, is that the OCC is stretching a charter designed for traditional trust companies to cover a new kind of business, and that the process should be slower and more restrictive. The lawsuit does not affect the approvals already granted, but it is a live legal challenge that could shape how many more charters are issued and on what terms.

There is also a political backdrop. The US midterm elections of 3 November 2026 produced a Democratic sweep that some investors fear could mean greater scrutiny of the crypto industry, and the CLARITY Act — the market-structure bill that would define how crypto is regulated in the US — remains stalled in Congress. Against that background, a federal charter is a way for a company to lock in a regulated status that does not depend on the next bill passing. It is regulation by charter rather than regulation by legislation, and for the companies involved, that is a feature, not a bug.

## What This Means for Indian Readers

Coinbase's retail exchange does not serve Indian residents today, and the new trust company is aimed at institutional clients, not retail users. So the direct effect on an Indian beginner is small. The indirect effects, and the comparison with India's own rules, are where the story matters.

**The ETF connection.** Coinbase Custody holds the Bitcoin behind most US spot Bitcoin ETFs. Those ETFs are the products that Indian investors read about, research, and sometimes access through global brokers or through Indian platforms that offer international investing. The safety of the custody behind them is therefore relevant to anyone thinking about that route — not because a charter guarantees safety, but because it puts the custodian under the kind of supervision that ETF investors are used to seeing in traditional finance.

**The regulatory comparison.** India has no equivalent of a federal crypto-bank charter. Indian crypto exchanges must register with FIU-IND, the Financial Intelligence Unit, and comply with anti-money-laundering rules — but there is no Indian licence that lets a crypto company operate as a federally chartered trust bank, because India's banking licences work differently and crypto companies are not banks. The contrast is instructive: the United States is building crypto into its existing banking-charter framework, while India regulates crypto platforms as reporting entities under anti-money-laundering law and taxes the activity heavily, without a dedicated licensing regime for the business itself.

**The tax reality is unchanged.** None of this changes what an Indian investor owes. Gains on transferring virtual digital assets are taxed at a flat 30% plus a 4% cess — 31.2% in total — with no set-off of losses, and sales attract 1% TDS under Section 194S. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. A US charter does not reduce Indian tax, does not create an exemption, and does not change which platforms an Indian resident is allowed to use.

**The scam warning.** A high-profile approval like this one attracts impersonators. Expect fake "Coinbase trust company" investment schemes, fake customer-support accounts, and messages promising returns from the new charter. Coinbase's trust company does not take retail deposits and does not offer investment products to the public. If anyone contacts you about investing through Coinbase National Trust Company, treat it as a scam. Report fraud on India's National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal at cybercrime.gov.in or the 1930 helpline, and never share a seed phrase or password with anyone claiming to help.

## What to Watch Next

Three things will show whether this charter is a milestone or just a press release.

The first is the custody migration itself. Coinbase has three years to move its New York custody business into the federal trust company. Watch whether that migration happens smoothly, whether institutional clients follow, and whether the OCC's examinations find problems. A charter is a framework; the test is in the operation.

The second is the ICBA lawsuit. If the community banks' challenge succeeds, it could slow or reshape the OCC's approach to crypto charters. If it fails, expect more applications — and more approvals — from other crypto companies, and possibly from traditional banks that want to offer digital-asset custody under the same framework.

The third is the CLARITY Act. The charter route and the legislation route are two different answers to the same question: how should crypto be regulated in the United States? If the Act passes, it may change the rules under which trust companies operate. If it stays stalled, the charter route becomes the de facto path, and the OCC becomes the de facto regulator for the largest crypto custodians. Either way, the direction of travel is clear — the biggest crypto companies are moving into the regulated banking perimeter, not away from it.

## The Bottom Line

On 6 November 2026, the OCC completed the chartering of Coinbase National Trust Company, and on 9 November Coinbase announced the final approval. The new trust company will provide fiduciary digital-asset custody under federal supervision, with at least $60 million in tier 1 capital and 180 days of wind-down liquidity for its first three years. It will not take retail deposits, make traditional loans, or issue stablecoins, and client cash held through it is not FDIC-insured. It joins Ripple's trust bank, approved on 26 October, in a small but growing group of crypto companies inside the US federal banking framework.

For Indian readers, the lesson is about the shape of regulation, not about a new product to use. The United States is fitting crypto custody into its banking-charter system; India regulates crypto platforms through FIU-IND registration and taxes the activity at 31.2% with 1% TDS, with no equivalent charter on offer. Understanding that difference is worth more than any headline — because it tells you where each country's rules are heading, and what to expect from the platforms you actually use.
