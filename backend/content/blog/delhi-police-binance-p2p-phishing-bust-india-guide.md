## Delhi Police Bust a Binance P2P Phishing Gang

Delhi Police have arrested four men who allegedly ran a cryptocurrency fraud syndicate that targeted traders through fake Binance peer-to-peer (P2P) deals and phishing links, draining a trader's wallet of 4,985 USDT — worth approximately ₹5 lakh. The arrests were announced on Tuesday 24 November 2026, and the case was reported the same day by ANI and carried by Asianet News, MENAFN, Rediff, the Daily Pioneer, Yes Punjab, Hindustan Herald and Coin Edition.

The case matters for Indian crypto users because the method it exposes is one of the most common ways ordinary traders lose money in India: not a hack of an exchange, not a flaw in a wallet, but a simple human trick. A seller lists USDT for sale on a P2P platform. A buyer appears, seems genuine, and then — almost casually — moves the conversation to WhatsApp. A link arrives. One click later, the wallet is empty.

This guide explains what allegedly happened, how the syndicate worked, why P2P trading is such a fertile ground for this kind of fraud, and what Indian sellers can do to make sure the same trick does not work on them.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto transactions are irreversible — once coins leave your wallet, there is usually no way to recall them. Never share your seed phrase, password or one-time codes with anyone.

## What Allegedly Happened: One Click, 4,985 USDT Gone

According to Delhi Police, the case began on 6 June 2026, when a Delhi-based cryptocurrency trader had an active sell order for USDT on Binance P2P. USDT, or Tether, is a stablecoin pegged to the US dollar, and it is one of the most commonly traded coins on Indian P2P markets because sellers use it to convert crypto into rupees.

A person posing as a prospective buyer contacted the trader about the listing. So far, nothing unusual — that is how P2P trading is supposed to work. But then the conversation shifted from the platform to WhatsApp. On the pretext of completing the purchase, the supposed buyer sent the trader a phishing link. A phishing link is a web link designed to look harmless — it may imitate a login page, a payment page or a wallet screen — but it is built to steal access credentials or to trigger an unauthorised transfer.

The trader clicked it. Within moments, the entire balance of his wallet — 4,985 USDT, approximately ₹5 lakh — was transferred out without his authorisation. There was no second chance, no confirmation screen he could cancel, no bank to call and reverse the payment. That is the defining feature of crypto theft: the transfer is final the moment it is confirmed on the blockchain.

The trader reported the fraud, and an e-FIR (No. 337/26) was registered on 2 July 2026 at the Cyber Police Station of Delhi Police's South-West District, under Sections 318(4), 319(2) and 61(2) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS). Section 318(4) covers cheating, Section 319(2) covers cheating by personation — pretending to be someone you are not — and Section 61(2) covers criminal conspiracy. These are allegations at this stage: the four men have been arrested, not convicted, and the case will now proceed through the courts.

## How the Police Traced the Syndicate to Alwar

A dedicated team was set up under the supervision of Inspector Pravesh Kaushik, the station house officer of the South-West District Cyber Police Station, with overall guidance from ACP (Operations) Sandeep Ghai. The investigation followed the digital trail: call detail records, internet protocol detail records and the WhatsApp numbers used to contact the victim.

That trail pointed to a syndicate allegedly operating from Alwar in Rajasthan, coordinated by a man named Atul Meena. Police raided Meena's hideout in Alwar and recovered ₹1.62 lakh in cash, three mobile phones, an iPad, a Jio Wi-Fi router and five SIM cards. Further technical leads took investigators to Jaipur, where three associates were arrested and eight more mobile phones were recovered. Atul Meena, who had been evading arrest, was later tracked down and apprehended near Dhaula Kuan in Delhi.

The four arrested men have been identified as Atul Meena, 21, a resident of Bhanokhar village in Alwar; Jagmohan Meena, 19, from Dausa; Narender Kumar Meena, 22, from Alwar; and Manish Meena, 24, also from Alwar. In total, police recovered ₹1.62 lakh in cash, 11 mobile phones, one iPad, one Wi-Fi router and six SIM cards. Police said the entire cheated amount of 4,985 USDT has been refunded to the complainant — an unusually complete recovery in a crypto fraud case, and one that depended on the funds being traced before they were laundered away.

## How the Syndicate Allegedly Worked

What makes this case instructive is not the amount — ₹5 lakh is small beside the multi-crore frauds that usually make headlines — but the organisation behind it. According to police, this was not one person with a laptop. It was a small business, with recruitment, division of labour and a repeatable playbook:

**Recruitment from villages around Alwar.** Police said the accused recruited young men from villages around Alwar on a profit-sharing basis. The recruits were not necessarily technical experts; they were given specific jobs to do.

**Rented Binance accounts.** The associates allegedly arranged Binance accounts on rent, advertising on Instagram to find people willing to lend their accounts. A rented account lets a fraudster operate under someone else's identity, making it harder for the exchange or the police to connect the account to the real operator.

**SIM cards and messaging accounts.** The group allegedly procured SIM cards and created WhatsApp and Instagram accounts in bulk. Fresh numbers and fresh accounts make it easy to discard an identity after a scam and start again with a clean one.

**Advertisements to find sellers.** The syndicate published online advertisements to attract people selling USDT through Binance P2P. This is the inversion of the usual scam: instead of advertising a too-good-to-be-true investment, the gang advertised itself as a buyer — which is exactly what a genuine seller is looking for.

**The handover.** Once a trader responded to an advertisement, the trader's WhatsApp number was allegedly shared with Atul Meena, who posed as a genuine buyer. He would build a normal-seeming conversation, then send a phishing link — and once the victim clicked it, the wallet was drained.

Each step on its own looks ordinary. A rented account, a WhatsApp chat, a link. The fraud is in the combination: the trust built by appearing inside a legitimate marketplace, the move off that marketplace where its protections no longer apply, and the link that turns trust into theft.

## Why Binance P2P Is a Target for This Kind of Fraud

To understand why this trick works, it helps to understand what P2P trading actually is — and what it is not.

On a P2P platform, you are not buying crypto from the exchange. You are buying it from another person, and the exchange acts as a middleman: it holds the seller's crypto in escrow, the buyer pays in rupees through UPI or a bank transfer, and the exchange releases the crypto once the seller confirms payment. While the trade stays inside the platform, both sides are protected. The buyer cannot take the crypto without paying, and the seller cannot keep both the crypto and the money.

The protection ends the moment the conversation leaves the platform. On WhatsApp, there is no escrow, no payment confirmation, no dispute button and no record the exchange can check. A scammer who can persuade a seller to move to WhatsApp has already won the most important part of the con: the victim is now operating without a safety net.

There are three reasons P2P sellers are especially exposed. First, sellers are the ones with crypto in their wallets, which is what the scammer wants — buyers are usually targeted for rupees instead. Second, P2P deals are often large and time-sensitive, which creates pressure to move fast and skip checks. Third, the Indian P2P market is big and busy, with thousands of listings every day, so a fraudster can run the same playbook against many victims at low cost.

This is also why the case matters beyond its ₹5 lakh figure. The same "move to WhatsApp, click this link" pattern shows up in job scams, investment scams and fake customer-support scams across India. The platform changes; the trick stays the same.

## How This Differs From the ₹55 Lakh Delhi Police Case

Readers of this site may remember a similar story from last week: on 20 November, this site covered a Delhi Police bust in which five people were arrested for a ₹55 lakh USDT scam. It is worth being precise about the difference, because two Delhi Police crypto busts in four days can easily blur into one story in a reader's mind.

That earlier case was investigated by the South-East District's Special Staff, not the South-West District's Cyber Police Station. It involved a different victim — a man from Mangolpuri — a different amount — ₹55 lakh in cash handed over in person on 3 and 4 October — and a different method: a trust-first trap in which the victim was persuaded to hand over cash for USDT that was then withdrawn from his Trust Wallet. Its FIR was No. 0372/2026, and one of the accused posed as "Krishna Agarwal".

This case is a different investigation, a different gang, a different district and a different method: a phishing link sent over WhatsApp during a Binance P2P deal, with the theft happening entirely online on 6 June, and the arrests announced on 24 November. The two cases share a city, a coin and a lesson — that USDT sellers in Delhi are being actively targeted — but they are not the same case, and neither is a follow-up to the other.

If anything, the pair of cases shows a pattern worth noting: Delhi Police's cyber units are now regularly busting crypto fraud gangs, and the gangs are using different methods against different victims. The defence has to be general — good habits that work against any variant — not a warning about one specific trick.

## What Indian P2P Sellers Should Do

The good news is that this particular scam is easy to defeat, because it depends on the victim leaving the platform. Every step that keeps the deal inside P2P's own protections makes the fraudster's job harder:

**Never leave the platform chat.** This is the single most important rule. A genuine buyer has no reason to move to WhatsApp, Telegram or a phone call. The escrow, the payment confirmation and the dispute system all live inside the P2P platform. If a buyer insists on moving the conversation elsewhere, treat that as the warning it is: end the deal.

**Never click a link a buyer sends you.** There is no legitimate reason for a P2P buyer to send you a link. Payment happens through UPI or bank transfer, and the release happens on the platform. Any link — to a "payment page", a "verification page", a "wallet connect" screen — is a phishing attempt until proven otherwise.

**Never share your seed phrase, password or one-time code.** No buyer, no seller, no support agent and no police officer will ever legitimately ask for these. Anyone who asks is a scammer, whatever story they tell. Your seed phrase is the key to your wallet: anyone who has it owns your crypto.

**Check the buyer's history before you trade.** P2P platforms show a counterparty's number of completed trades, completion rate and account age. A brand-new account with no history offering to buy a large amount is a riskier counterparty than an established trader with hundreds of completed deals. This check costs seconds and filters out a large share of bad actors.

**Keep only trading funds in your hot wallet.** The victim in this case lost his entire wallet balance — 4,985 USDT — because everything he held was in the wallet the phishing link could reach. Keeping only the amount you are actively trading in a hot wallet, and the rest in a separate wallet or a hardware wallet, limits the damage if any single wallet is compromised.

**Be careful with account-rental offers.** The gang in this case rented Binance accounts through Instagram advertisements. If someone offers you money to rent your exchange account, do not accept: your account, your identity documents and your bank details would be used for fraud, and you could face police action yourself even though you did not send the phishing link.

**Remember the tax rules apply to P2P too.** Gains from transferring virtual digital assets, including USDT sold on P2P, are taxed at a flat 30% (plus applicable surcharge and 4% cess) under Section 115BBH, with 1% TDS under Section 194S on transactions above the specified thresholds. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. Keeping records of every P2P trade — screenshots of the order, the payment and the release — protects you twice: once for tax filing, and once if you ever need to prove what happened in a dispute or a police complaint.

## What To Do If You Are Targeted

If a P2P buyer has already sent you a phishing link, or you clicked one, act fast — minutes matter, because stolen crypto is typically moved and mixed within the hour:

1. **Do not send any more money or crypto.** Scammers often follow a first theft with a "recovery fee" or a "verification deposit". There is no fee that gets your crypto back.
2. **Move remaining funds immediately.** If any balance is left in the affected wallet, transfer it at once to a fresh wallet whose seed phrase has never touched the compromised device.
3. **Change your passwords and revoke sessions.** Change your exchange password, enable two-factor authentication if it is not already on, and revoke any connected apps or sessions you do not recognise.
4. **Report it the same day.** File a complaint on the National Cybercrime Reporting Portal at cybercrime.gov.in, or call the 1930 cybercrime helpline. Give the transaction hash, the wallet addresses involved, screenshots of the chat and the phishing link. The earlier the report, the better the chance that exchanges or police can flag the receiving addresses.
5. **Tell your exchange.** Report the counterparty's account to the P2P platform's support team with the trade details, so the account can be frozen and other users warned.

The refund in this case — the entire 4,985 USDT returned to the complainant — shows that reporting can work. But it is the exception, not the rule: most phishing victims never see their funds again, because the crypto is moved too fast to trace. Prevention is worth far more than any recovery process.

## The Bottom Line

Four arrests, one refunded victim and a playbook laid bare: that is what Delhi Police's 24 November announcement gives Indian crypto users. The gang allegedly behind it recruited young men from villages around Alwar, rented Binance accounts through Instagram ads, and used WhatsApp chats and phishing links to drain the wallets of USDT sellers on Binance P2P. The victim in this case lost 4,985 USDT — about ₹5 lakh — in a single click on 6 June, and only got it back because the police traced the gang and the funds before they disappeared.

The lesson is simple and cheap to apply. P2P trading is safe when it stays inside the platform: escrow, verified payment, dispute resolution. The moment a buyer asks you to move to WhatsApp and click a link, the deal has stopped being a trade and started being a trap. Keep the conversation on the platform, never click a buyer's link, never share your seed phrase or codes, and keep only trading funds in your hot wallet. Those four habits would have stopped this scam cold — and they will stop the next version of it too.
