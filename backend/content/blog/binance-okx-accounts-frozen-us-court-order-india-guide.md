## A US Court Order Froze 27,304 Crypto Wallets — Including Innocent Ones

Imagine opening your exchange app one morning to find your balance frozen. Customer support cannot tell you why, cannot name the court case, and cannot release the money. Your funds are not lost to a hack and not seized by your own government — they are locked because a judge in another country ordered your exchange to freeze accounts linked to a list of blockchain addresses, and your wallet touched one of them somewhere along the way.

That is the situation facing an unknown number of Binance and OKX users right now. On 24 November 2026, a US federal court in Colorado extended a temporary restraining order that freezes crypto accounts linked to 27,304 addresses on the TRON blockchain — and Binance has publicly acknowledged that accounts belonging to people with no connection to the case can be caught in it. The freeze now runs until 17 December, when a judge will decide whether it becomes permanent.

This guide explains what the order is, who it targets, why innocent users get swept in, what happened the same night Binance itself was hacked, and what Indian crypto users should do — and avoid doing — if they are affected.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose.

## What Happened: The Colorado Court Order

The case is called Parbas v. Huione, and it is being heard in the US District Court for the District of Colorado. The sequence, as reported by BeInCrypto, CryptoCoinShow, BSCN and CoinStats on 23–24 November, runs like this:

**9 September 2026 — Xinbi is sanctioned.** The US Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) designated the Xinbi Guarantee marketplace as a significant transnational criminal organisation, alleging it processed more than $24 billion — about ₹2.26 lakh crore at roughly ₹94 to the dollar — mostly in Tether's USDT stablecoin on the TRON blockchain, according to TRM Labs. The Treasury put Xinbi's total processed volume since 2022 at $36 billion, and said the platform had also been used by North Korean hackers and entities tied to the Prince Group. Two supporting developers were sanctioned alongside it: Cambodia-based Anwen Technology, which built the no-KYC XinbiPay wallet, and Singapore-based SafeW Technology, which built the encrypted messaging app Xinbi's networks migrated to as scrutiny grew. "Scam centers in Southeast Asia steal billions of dollars from American victims each year," Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said in the designation statement.

**29 October 2026 — the court signs the freeze.** A Colorado judge signed a temporary restraining order (TRO) — a court order that blocks access to assets before a judge rules on the underlying claims — targeting Huione Group and Xinbi, two Southeast Asian networks accused of laundering criminal proceeds. The US Treasury says Huione laundered at least $4 billion, roughly ₹37,600 crore, between 2021 and 2025. The order instructed Binance and OKX to freeze accounts linked to specified deposit addresses, and the plaintiffs served notice to 27,304 TRON addresses — far more than the 52 addresses the Treasury had named in its sanctions filing.

**The case was sealed.** For weeks, affected users could not find out why their accounts were frozen. Because the case was sealed, Binance's support team could initially only refer to a "Sealed Case Name" when responding to users, according to BeInCrypto. One trader posting on X claimed to have roughly 400,000 USDT — about ₹3.76 crore at roughly ₹94 per USDT — locked with no visibility into the case. That claim comes from a user post and has not been independently verified, but it illustrates the core problem: people were locked out of substantial sums without being told the reason.

**23 November 2026 — Binance acknowledges the problem.** Binance published a blog post and a post on X acknowledging that unrelated accounts could be affected by court-ordered freezes, and explaining what the exchange can and cannot do. The exchange cannot independently release funds covered by the order, even when it believes a customer is uninvolved. "At Binance, protecting users and complying with applicable legal obligations are both core responsibilities," the company wrote. The plaintiffs' legal notice had asked anyone wishing to claim an interest in the frozen assets, or to object, to contact their lawyers as soon as possible and no later than 24 November 2026.

**24 November 2026 — the freeze is extended.** The Colorado court extended the temporary restraining order until 17 December 2026, after the plaintiffs told the court that service on some defendants is still in progress. A preliminary injunction hearing is scheduled for 8:30 a.m. Mountain Time on 17 December in Denver, where the judge will decide whether the freeze becomes permanent or the wallets are released. Any defendant — or any third party claiming an interest in the frozen assets — who wishes to oppose a permanent injunction must file a response by 10 December 2026, a week before the hearing, or risk having the injunction imposed without their objection under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 65.

## Why 27,304 Wallets? How Innocent Users Get Swept In

The most important number in this story is the gap between 52 and 27,304. The Treasury's sanctions filing named 52 addresses. The court order reaches 27,304 TRON addresses — more than 500 times as many. Understanding why that gap exists is the key to understanding the risk.

Blockchain tracing works by following the flow of funds. When investigators identify a wallet they believe belongs to a criminal network, they do not only flag that wallet — they also flag the addresses it sent money to, received money from, and passed money through. Every exchange deposit address that ever received funds from a flagged wallet can end up on the list, and every user whose funds passed through one of those deposit addresses can be swept in as well.

That is how a freeze aimed at two laundering networks can reach a trader who has never heard of Huione or Xinbi. If you once sold USDT on Binance to someone whose funds had earlier passed through a flagged address, or received a payment that had touched one of those addresses three hops earlier, your account can be linked to the list. The order freezes first and sorts out innocence later — and "later" in a court case can mean weeks or months.

The scale of the underlying networks explains why the list is so long. After Telegram banned Xinbi and competing marketplaces in spring 2025, TRM Labs found that Xinbi's daily inflows nearly doubled between May and December 2025, even as Huione's and Haowang's volumes fell close to zero. Enforcement against one channel simply redirected the flow to the next, and the address list grew with it.

There is also a precedent for pushing back. An English court previously lifted a freezing order after the applicant failed to disclose that a targeted wallet held pooled funds belonging to exchange customers. Judges can and do reverse course once the facts surface — which is why the 10 December objection deadline and the 17 December hearing matter. The open question, as CryptoCoinShow put it, is whether the December hearing will produce a mechanism for unrelated wallet holders to exit the freeze before the case against Huione and Xinbi concludes, rather than after.

## The Same Night Binance Itself Was Hacked

The timing of this story carries an irony that is worth spelling out, because it shows how fast crypto can now be frozen — by courts and by companies alike.

The Colorado judge signed the temporary restraining order on the night of 29 October 2026. That was the same night Binance itself suffered a major security breach, in which about $6.34 billion of cryptocurrency and customer tokens were stolen. Within 57 minutes, Tether had blacklisted 971 million USDT. Within roughly 12 hours, issuers and protocols had frozen about $2.84 billion, according to Recoveris — all before any court ruling. US prosecutors later secured approval to seize $1.2 billion of the stolen funds. And on 18 November, Binance obtained a $6.5 billion worldwide freezing order from an Abu Dhabi court, covering approximately 26,000 wallets, including parties it does not accuse of wrongdoing.

Put the two freezes side by side and the picture is striking:

| Freeze | Who ordered it | Scope | Speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Colorado TRO (Parbas v. Huione) | US District Court, Colorado | 27,304 TRON addresses at Binance and OKX | Weeks of sealed proceedings; extended to 17 Dec 2026 |
| Binance hack asset freeze | Tether, issuers and protocols | 971M USDT blacklisted in 57 minutes; $2.84B frozen in ~12 hours | Minutes to hours, no court ruling needed |
| Abu Dhabi order (Binance hack) | Abu Dhabi court, at Binance's request | ~26,000 wallets, ~$6.5B, incl. parties not accused of wrongdoing | Granted 18 Nov 2026 |

These powers can help recover stolen money — the $1.2 billion seizure approval and the $2.84 billion frozen within hours of the hack are real recoveries. But the same machinery, pointed at a different target, can lock an innocent person's savings for weeks with no hearing yet held. As Erica Stanford, an adviser at law firm CMS, warned in a recent BeInCrypto discussion: "There's definitely good use cases for freezing crypto assets… what you also don't want is a new version of fiat money where at their will governments can freeze money, can seize money, can stop you having money." Patrick Prinz, co-founder of Recoveris, made the same point from the other side: "I just wish there would be a clear seizing and freezing policy."

Crypto has made it possible to move and freeze billions of dollars within minutes. The legal systems governing those assets still operate at a much slower pace — and for an ordinary investor caught in a freezing order, that gap can mean weeks without access to money they need to live, even when they have committed no crime.

The through-line across all of them is the same lesson, stated differently each time: when your crypto sits on a centralised exchange, someone else's legal problem can become your access problem.

## What This Means for Indian Crypto Users

Binance and OKX are among the most widely used offshore exchanges for Indian traders, which makes this a directly relevant story for Indian readers — even though the case is American, the networks are Southeast Asian, and no Indian exchange is named in the order. Here is what to take from it.

**Your exchange balance is a claim, not a vault.** When crypto sits on a centralised exchange, the exchange holds the private keys and your balance is a record in its systems. That is why a US court order can freeze an Indian user's Binance account: the exchange, not you, controls the keys, and the exchange must obey the order. The same logic applied when Indian users faced INR withdrawal delays at Coinbase, Unocoin and ZebPay earlier this month — different cause, same structure. If access matters to you, understand the difference between holding crypto on an exchange and holding it in a wallet where you control the keys.

**Check whether you are affected — and do it through official channels.** If your Binance or OKX account is frozen and support mentions a sealed case or a court order, the plaintiffs' legal notice is published at legalnoticeparbasvhuione.com, with the address list and contact details for the plaintiffs' lawyers. The notice asked affected users to contact counsel by 24 November 2026, and formal objections to the court are due by 10 December, ahead of the 17 December hearing. Read the notice itself rather than relying on social-media summaries, and keep screenshots and records of every transaction that might be relevant.

**Never pay anyone who offers to unfreeze your funds.** A freeze like this attracts scammers who promise to release locked funds for a fee, or who impersonate exchange support or lawyers. No legitimate process works that way: only the court can lift the order, and the exchange has said it cannot release funds on its own. Anyone asking for payment, a seed phrase, or remote access to "unfreeze" an account is running a scam. Report such approaches to the exchange and to the Indian Cybercrime Reporting Portal at cybercrime.gov.in.

**Keep records as if a court might one day ask for them.** If your funds are ever frozen, the fastest way to show you are uninvolved is a clean paper trail: exchange statements, deposit and withdrawal records, the source of the funds, and the purpose of each transaction. Indian users already need these records for tax filing, since profits on virtual digital assets are taxed at a flat 30% with 1% TDS rules applying in many cases — and this case is one more reason to keep them organised, not just for the tax department but for any future dispute.

**This does not change Indian crypto rules.** Nothing about a Colorado court order changes the position in India: crypto is not legal tender, trading on offshore exchanges carries its own FEMA questions, and the 30% tax plus 1% TDS framework still applies to gains. What the case does change is the risk picture for anyone using an offshore exchange — a foreign court can reach into your account without your government being involved at all.

Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

## The Bottom Line

A Colorado judge first signed the order on 29 October, the same night Binance was hacked. Nearly a month later, the freeze has been extended to 17 December, it covers 27,304 addresses — more than 500 times the 52 the Treasury named — and Binance has confirmed that people with no connection to the Huione and Xinbi networks can be caught in it, with no way for the exchange to release their funds on its own.

The story is not that a court froze criminals' money. Courts do that every day, and the $24 billion attributed to Xinbi and the $4 billion attributed to Huione are the reason this case exists. The story is that the machinery built to freeze criminals' money now moves so fast, and reaches so widely, that it can lock an innocent person's savings for weeks before a judge hears their side — and that the only remedy is a formal objection filed with a court in Denver by 10 December.

For Indian users, the practical lesson is simple and does not depend on the outcome of this case: know where your crypto actually sits, who holds the keys, and what would happen to your access if that holder were ordered to freeze it. If you cannot answer those questions for every exchange you use, that is the gap to close first — before the next court order, hack, or outage tests it for you.
