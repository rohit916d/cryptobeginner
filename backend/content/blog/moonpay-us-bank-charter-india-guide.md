# MoonPay Seeks a US Bank Charter: What It Means in India

MoonPay, the crypto payments company behind the buy-crypto button inside dozens of wallets and apps, has applied to become a federally chartered trust bank in the United States. The company filed its application with the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) on Tuesday, 17 November 2026, to establish MoonPay National Trust Bank, a non-depository institution headquartered in Miami Beach, Florida. If approved, the bank would issue and redeem stablecoins, manage the reserves behind them, and hold digital assets in custody for clients — all under direct federal banking supervision. The filing was first flagged publicly in a Bloomberg report on Thursday, 19 November, and was then covered the same day by Cointelegraph, Bitcoin.com, PYMNTS, Bloomberg Law and Crypto Briefing, with the OCC's own licensing-applications table recording receipt of the application on 18 November. This guide explains what the MoonPay bank charter application actually asks for, what a national trust bank can and cannot do, why stablecoins sit at the centre of the filing, and what Indian readers should — and should not — take away from it.

## What Happened: The Timeline

The filing itself is only a few days old, but it lands at the end of a long build-up, and the dates matter because they show the difference between applying for a charter and receiving one.

MoonPay submitted its application to the OCC on Tuesday, 17 November 2026. The OCC's public table of digital-assets licensing applications records the application from MoonPay National Trust Bank as received on 18 November 2026. The filing became public knowledge on Thursday, 19 November, when Bloomberg reported it; Bloomberg Law, Cointelegraph, Bitcoin.com, PYMNTS and Crypto Briefing all carried the story the same day, agreeing on the same core facts: the proposed bank's name, its Miami Beach headquarters, its non-depository trust-bank structure, and the activities it would cover.

The application is the first step, not the last. The OCC has not commented publicly on the filing, no review timetable has been published, and approval is not guaranteed — the company must satisfy the regulator's requirements before the proposed trust bank can begin operations. That distinction is worth stating plainly, because headlines that read "MoonPay becomes a bank" would be wrong. MoonPay has asked to become one. The answer will take months, and the OCC's examination process for a new charter is demanding: it reviews the business plan, the people running the institution, the capital and controls, and the compliance framework before it says yes.

MoonPay is not starting from zero with regulators. The company, founded in 2019 and best known for software that lets users move between traditional currencies and crypto, already holds a BitLicense from New York's financial regulator, granted in June 2025, and New York approved a limited-purpose trust company for MoonPay that November. The federal application builds on those state-level authorisations — the same path Coinbase followed before its own federal charter became effective on 6 November 2026.

Google Trends India shows no measurable search interest in "moonpay" itself — the term read flat at zero through the past 24 hours, and no crypto term appeared in India's top 25 trending searches on the morning of 20 November, when a cyclone and cricket dominated the list. The rising queries around that spike were dominated by casino spam, so they do not name a topic by themselves. The story therefore stands on the reporting, not on the search data: seven independent outlets plus the OCC's own applications table agree on the same facts, and the overnight spike shows Indian crypto attention was elevated on the same night the filing was being reported.

## What Is a National Trust Bank?

A national trust bank is a federally chartered financial institution whose business is holding and managing assets for other people, rather than taking deposits and making loans the way a commercial bank does. The OCC charters and supervises it, and the institution operates under federal law across the United States, rather than under the law of a single state.

That distinction is the whole point of the charter. A state trust charter means dealing with state rules and, in practice, with the patchwork of state licensing that comes with serving clients across the country. A federal charter means one supervisor, one set of federal examination standards, and a framework that institutional clients — asset managers, stablecoin issuers, payment companies — already understand from the traditional finance world.

For MoonPay, the practical difference is about the business it wants to grow: custody and stablecoins. Its on-ramp business already moves money between banks and blockchains for millions of users. A federally chartered trust bank would let it hold the assets behind that business — customer crypto in custody, and the reserves backing the stablecoins it issues and redeems — inside an institution supervised by a federal banking regulator, with the legal duties of a fiduciary. That is a stronger framework than a state licence, and it is the framework the largest crypto companies have been moving toward all year.

## What the Charter Would Allow — and What It Would Not

The most important thing to understand about this filing is its limits. The headlines say "MoonPay seeks a bank charter", and that is true in the technical sense. But the proposed bank would not be a conventional bank, and several of the things people assume a bank can do are explicitly excluded.

**What it would allow.** Under the application, MoonPay National Trust Bank would hold customer assets in a fiduciary capacity, issue and redeem stablecoins, manage the reserves backing those tokens, act as a conversion agent, and provide custody for digital assets. That is the machinery behind dollar-pegged tokens: someone has to hold the dollars and short-term government securities that back each stablecoin, honour redemptions when holders want their dollars back, and keep those reserves segregated and audited. Today that work is done by a mix of state-chartered trust companies, banks and the issuers themselves. A federal charter would let MoonPay do it inside a federally supervised institution.

**What it would not allow.** The proposed bank would be non-depository: it would not accept retail or commercial deposits and would not write loans. It would not seek deposit insurance, so customer money held through it would not carry the FDIC protection that covers ordinary bank deposits. It would operate exclusively through digital channels, with no public-facing offices and no plans to open branches during its first three years. And the filing describes the proposed bank as a member of the Federal Reserve System — a status national banks hold — without seeking deposit insurance, a combination that underlines the point: this is a trust institution, not a place to open a savings account.

**The people and the paperwork.** MoonPay co-founder and chief executive Ivan Soto-Wright would chair the proposed bank's five-member board and serve as its interim president and chief executive officer. The filing also seeks waivers of US citizenship and residency rules for directors — a routine but telling detail, since it shows the company is staffing the bank's board with the people it wants rather than the people the default rules would require. None of this is unusual for a charter application; it is the normal paperwork of setting up a regulated institution, and the OCC will examine it along with the business plan.

## Why Stablecoins Are the Heart of This Application

Custody is part of the story, but stablecoins are the reason the timing matters. The GENIUS Act — the US law that sets federal standards for stablecoin issuers, including reserve backing and redemption requirements — takes effect on 18 January 2027. From that date, the rules for who can issue a dollar-pegged token in the United States, what reserves they must hold, and how redemptions must work will be set at the federal level.

MoonPay's application lands two months before that date, and the connection is direct. The activities the proposed bank would perform — issuing and redeeming stablecoins, managing their reserves — are exactly the activities the GENIUS Act regulates. A federal charter would put MoonPay inside the framework the new law creates, supervised by the same regulator that oversees the traditional banks that hold stablecoin reserves today.

That matters because stablecoins are no longer a niche product. They are the dollars of the crypto economy: the asset traders move into when they sell, the asset Indian users buy when they want a dollar-denominated holding, and the rails behind a growing share of cross-border payments. In India, that demand is visible in the price itself. At the 19 November fix of The Crypto Times' India Premium Index, USDT traded about 10.54% above its global price in India and USDC about 11.46% above — Indians pay a real premium for dollar-pegged tokens, because demand for them outstrips the supply that reaches Indian platforms. A federally chartered issuer and reserve manager does not erase that premium, which comes from Indian banking and supply constraints, not from US regulation. But it does show where the global supply side is heading: into federally supervised institutions, with published reserve rules, rather than into the lightly supervised arrangements of the past.

There is also a competitive logic. Circle, the issuer of USDC, has already secured OCC approval for a national trust company. Coinbase's charter became effective on 6 November. Ripple received its final approval on 26 October. Each of those companies is now inside the federal perimeter for the custody and settlement infrastructure behind its stablecoin or exchange business. MoonPay, whose business is moving users' money into crypto and stablecoins, would be at a disadvantage if its competitors held their reserves and custody inside federally chartered banks while it did not. The application is, in that sense, a catch-up move as much as a growth move.

## The Charter Wave: Who Else Is in Line

MoonPay is the latest in a wave of crypto companies seeking federal banking licences, not the first, and the sequence tells its own story about where US regulation is heading.

Ripple received final OCC approval for its national trust bank on 26 October 2026. Circle, the issuer of USDC, has also secured an OCC approval for a national trust company. Coinbase's charter became effective on 6 November 2026, with at least $60 million in tier 1 capital and a separate pool of liquid assets equal to 180 days of wind-down expenses for its first three years. Those are approvals, not applications — the companies have already cleared the OCC's review.

Behind them, a queue is forming. MoonPay's filing joins applications from companies including Rain and Modern Treasury, and reporting on the filing also names Block among the firms pursuing federal charters. The pattern is the same in each case: a crypto company that already operates under state licences is asking the OCC for a federal charter, so that its custody, settlement or stablecoin business sits under one federal supervisor instead of a patchwork of state rules.

That wave is not uncontested. The Independent Community Bankers of America (ICBA) sued the OCC on 2 October 2026, challenging the rules behind national trust bank charters for crypto companies. The community banks' argument, in essence, is that the OCC is stretching a charter designed for traditional trust companies to cover a new kind of business, and that the process should be slower and more restrictive. The lawsuit does not affect the approvals already granted, and it does not block new applications — but it is a live legal challenge that could shape how many more charters are issued and on what terms. If it succeeds, the queue could slow. If it fails, expect more applications, and more approvals, from other crypto companies and possibly from traditional banks that want to offer digital-asset custody under the same framework.

There is also a political backdrop. The US midterm elections of 3 November 2026 produced a Democratic sweep that some investors fear could mean greater scrutiny of the crypto industry, and the CLARITY Act — the market-structure bill that would define how crypto is regulated in the US — remains stalled in Congress. Against that background, a federal charter is a way for a company to lock in a regulated status that does not depend on the next bill passing. It is regulation by charter rather than regulation by legislation, and for the companies involved, that is a feature, not a bug.

## What This Means for Indian Readers

MoonPay's retail on-ramp does not serve Indian residents as a standalone product in the way a local exchange does, and the proposed trust bank is aimed at stablecoin and custody infrastructure, not retail users. So the direct effect on an Indian beginner is small. The indirect effects, and the comparison with India's own rules, are where the story matters.

**The on-ramp you have already used.** MoonPay is best known as the buy-crypto button inside many wallets and apps: the flow that takes a card payment and delivers crypto to your wallet address. Indian users encounter that button inside global wallets and decentralised apps, even when the company itself is not an Indian exchange. A federal charter would not change that button, but it would change the institution behind it — the custody and stablecoin machinery would sit inside a federally supervised trust bank rather than a state-licensed payments company. That is a difference in the safety framework, not in the product you see.

**The stablecoin connection.** The stablecoins Indians actually buy — USDT and USDC — are the tokens this charter wave is about. Circle, which issues USDC, already holds an OCC approval. If MoonPay's application succeeds, another major stablecoin and custody provider joins the federal perimeter two months before the GENIUS Act's reserve and redemption rules take effect. For an Indian user paying a 10–11% premium for those tokens, the US framework does not change the price — the premium comes from Indian supply and banking constraints. But it does change what stands behind the token: a federally supervised reserve manager with published rules, rather than a private arrangement you cannot inspect.

**The regulatory comparison.** India has no equivalent of a federal crypto-bank charter. Indian crypto exchanges must register with FIU-IND, the Financial Intelligence Unit, and comply with anti-money-laundering rules — but there is no Indian licence that lets a crypto company operate as a federally chartered trust bank, because India's banking licences work differently and crypto companies are not banks. The contrast is instructive: the United States is building crypto into its existing banking-charter framework, while India regulates crypto platforms as reporting entities under anti-money-laundering law and taxes the activity heavily, without a dedicated licensing regime for the business itself.

**The tax reality is unchanged.** None of this changes what an Indian investor owes. Gains on transferring virtual digital assets are taxed at a flat 30% plus a 4% cess — 31.2% in total — with no set-off of losses, and sales attract 1% TDS under Section 194S. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. A US charter does not reduce Indian tax, does not create an exemption, and does not change which platforms an Indian resident is allowed to use.

**The scam warning.** A high-profile filing like this one attracts impersonators. Expect fake "MoonPay bank" investment schemes, fake customer-support accounts, and messages promising returns from the new charter. MoonPay National Trust Bank does not exist yet, it does not take retail deposits, and it does not offer investment products to the public. If anyone contacts you about investing through it, or asks for a seed phrase, password or OTP in connection with it, treat it as a scam. Report fraud on India's National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal at cybercrime.gov.in or the 1930 helpline, and never share a seed phrase or password with anyone claiming to help.

## What to Watch Next

Three things will show whether this application is a milestone or just a press release.

The first is the OCC review itself. No timetable has been published, and the OCC has not commented on the filing. Watch for the OCC's licensing-applications table to update, for a public comment period if one is opened, and for any conditions the OCC attaches — the Coinbase charter came with $60 million in tier 1 capital and 180 days of wind-down liquidity for three years, and MoonPay's approval, if it comes, is likely to carry its own conditions. A charter is a framework; the test is in the operation.

The second is the GENIUS Act's effective date, 18 January 2027. The stablecoin rules take effect then, and the companies that hold federal charters will be the ones best placed to operate under them. Watch whether MoonPay's review moves quickly enough to be inside the framework by then, and whether other applicants in the queue — Rain, Modern Treasury, Block — receive decisions before or after that date.

The third is the ICBA lawsuit. If the community banks' challenge succeeds, it could slow or reshape the OCC's approach to crypto charters. If it fails, expect more applications — and more approvals — from other crypto companies, and possibly from traditional banks that want to offer digital-asset custody under the same framework. Either way, the direction of travel is clear: the biggest crypto companies are moving into the regulated banking perimeter, not away from it.

## The Bottom Line

On 17 November 2026, MoonPay applied to the OCC to establish MoonPay National Trust Bank, a non-depository trust bank headquartered in Miami Beach, Florida, that would issue and redeem stablecoins, manage their reserves and provide digital-asset custody. The OCC recorded receiving the application on 18 November, and the filing was reported on 19 November by Bloomberg, Bloomberg Law, Cointelegraph, Bitcoin.com, PYMNTS and Crypto Briefing. The bank does not exist yet: approval is not guaranteed, no timetable has been published, and the proposed institution would not take retail deposits, make loans or offer FDIC-insured accounts. It would operate digitally, with no branches planned in its first three years, under a board chaired by co-founder and chief executive Ivan Soto-Wright.

For Indian readers, the lesson is about the shape of regulation, not about a new product to use. The United States is fitting stablecoins and crypto custody into its banking-charter system — Coinbase, Ripple and Circle are already inside it, and MoonPay has now asked to join them — while India regulates crypto platforms through FIU-IND registration and taxes the activity at 31.2% with 1% TDS, with no equivalent charter on offer. Understanding that difference is worth more than any headline, because it tells you where each country's rules are heading, and what stands behind the stablecoins you actually buy.
