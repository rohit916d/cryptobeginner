## Spain's Sepblac Can Freeze Crypto Accounts: India Guide

Spain has given its financial intelligence unit the power to freeze a crypto account on its own say-so, without waiting for a judge. Under Royal Decree-Law 44/2026, published in Spain's Official State Gazette on 25 November 2026, Sepblac — the Executive Service of the Commission for the Prevention of Money Laundering and Monetary Offenses — can suspend a crypto, bank or payment account for up to five business days when its own analysis points to money laundering or terrorist financing. A transaction already in progress can be held for up to ten business days. The change was reported on 26 November by CoinDesk, Cointelegraph, FinanceMagnates, CryptoCompass, ForkLog and others, and it takes effect on 25 January 2027.

This guide explains what Sepblac is, exactly what changed, the limits on the new power, why Spain made the move now, and what it means for Indians — whether you live in Spain, hold crypto on a Spanish-authorised platform, or simply want to understand how financial watchdogs around the world are tightening their grip on crypto. It is an explainer, not legal advice, and it makes no prediction about prices or markets.

## What Happened: The Decree at a Glance

The facts, as reported on 25–26 November 2026 by CoinDesk, Cointelegraph, FinanceMagnates, CryptoCompass, ForkLog, BitcoinWorld and crypto.ro, are:

- **Royal Decree-Law 44/2026 was published on 25 November 2026** in Spain's Official State Gazette (the Boletín Oficial del Estado). Its main subject is consumer credit — it transposes an EU directive on consumer credit — but it also carries the amendment that changes Sepblac's powers.
- **A new provision, Article 46 bis, is inserted into Ley 10/2010**, Spain's core anti-money-laundering law. It gives Sepblac the authority to suspend bank, payment and crypto-asset accounts when it detects signs of money laundering or terrorist financing.
- **An account or business relationship can be frozen for up to five business days.** A transaction already in progress can be stopped or held for up to ten business days.
- **No judge is needed for that first step.** Sepblac can act on its own internal analysis, without waiting for a court order and without waiting for a request from a foreign financial intelligence unit.
- **The freeze is temporary by design.** Once the deadline expires, the restriction lapses automatically unless a public prosecutor asks a court to confirm or extend it before the deadline.
- **The powers take effect on 25 January 2027.** Crypto firms operating in Spain have roughly two months to adjust their compliance procedures before direct freeze orders become possible.
- **The decree is provisional until validated.** Under Spain's constitution, Congress has 30 days from promulgation to ratify or repeal a royal decree-law, so the measure still needs that vote before its effective date arrives.
- **Non-compliance is expensive.** Platforms that fail to carry out a Sepblac order face a "very serious infringement", with fines starting at €150,000, according to legal analysis cited in the coverage.

## What Is Sepblac?

Sepblac is Spain's financial intelligence unit, formally the Executive Service of the Commission for the Prevention of Money Laundering and Monetary Offenses. It is both a financial intelligence unit and a supervisory authority for money-laundering prevention. In plain terms, it is the Spanish body that receives suspicious-transaction reports from banks, payment firms and crypto platforms, analyses them, and passes cases to law enforcement and prosecutors.

Every country has an equivalent. In India, that role is split: the Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU-IND) receives suspicious-transaction reports from reporting entities — including crypto exchanges, which must register with FIU-IND — while the Enforcement Directorate investigates and attaches property under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA). In the United States, the equivalent is FinCEN, which in November 2026 used its powers to cut off the exchange MEXC over alleged illicit flows. Understanding Sepblac as "Spain's FIU" makes the rest of the story easy to follow.

## What Exactly Changed: Before and After

The change is narrow but important. It is about who can start a freeze, and how fast.

| | Before the decree | After the decree (from 25 Jan 2027) |
|---|---|---|
| **Who can order a freeze** | Sepblac could suspend an ongoing transaction only when a foreign financial intelligence unit asked it to | Sepblac can act on its own analysis, with no foreign request and no judge |
| **What can be frozen** | An individual transaction in progress, on a foreign request | A whole account or business relationship, plus transactions in progress |
| **How long an account freeze lasts** | Not available on Sepblac's own initiative | Up to five business days |
| **How long a transaction hold lasts** | Limited, and only on a foreign request | Up to ten business days |
| **What happens after the deadline** | The restriction ended | It lapses automatically unless a prosecutor gets a court to confirm or extend it |
| **Who must comply** | Crypto service providers authorised in Spain, plus banks and payment firms | The same, with fines from €150,000 for non-compliance |

The key sentence in the coverage, repeated across outlets, is that Spain is "trading speed for prior judicial sign-off". Sepblac no longer has to wait for a foreign counterpart to flag a problem, and it no longer has to wait for a judge to agree before it acts. The judge still matters — but only at the second step, when the state wants to keep the money frozen beyond five or ten business days.

There are also procedural details worth knowing. When a suspicion comes from a report filed by a bank or exchange under Spain's anti-money-laundering law, Sepblac has three business days from notification to issue a suspension order. When the unit identifies the need through its own analysis, it must act as soon as possible. And Article 46 bis protects Sepblac, its officials and employees, and the institutions that comply with its orders from liability when a suspension is carried out lawfully.

## Why Spain Moved Now

The decree does not explain itself in a single sentence, and the reporting does not claim one single trigger. But the timing fits a pattern that has been building across Europe through November 2026.

First, Europe is in a tightening phase. In early November, the European Union announced a dedicated Crypto Sanctions Task Force to chase sanctions evasion through crypto, targeting networks such as the A7A5 ruble stablecoin. The UK's Financial Conduct Authority said on 26 November that it plans to require DeFi platforms to be authorised under its incoming crypto regime, explicitly citing the $6.3 billion Binance hack as a reason. Spain's move is the same direction of travel: give the enforcement body a faster first move, and let the courts decide later whether the freeze should continue.

Second, the Binance hack in late October 2026 changed the conversation about speed. When billions of dollars can move across chains in minutes — the stolen funds were swapped and bridged within hours — a watchdog that has to wait for a foreign request or a court hearing is, in practical terms, watching the money leave. Sepblac's new power is designed for that reality: five days to hold the account while investigators work out whether the suspicion is justified.

Third, the change closes a gap that was already visible in the old rule. Under the previous Article 48 bis, Sepblac could act only when another country's financial intelligence unit asked. That meant a suspicious account in Spain could sit untouched if no foreign counterpart happened to flag it. The new rule lets the Spanish unit act on what its own analysts see.

None of this makes Spain an outlier. It makes Spain one of the first EU countries to write this particular power into law in this form — and other European regulators will be watching whether the 30-day congressional validation passes and whether the January start date holds.

## What It Means for Indians

For most Indian crypto users, this decree changes nothing directly. It applies to accounts with crypto service providers authorised in Spain, and to bank and payment accounts in Spain. An account on an Indian exchange, held by an Indian resident in India, is governed by Indian law, not by a Spanish decree.

But there are three groups of Indians who should pay attention.

**1. Indians living, studying or working in Spain.** If your crypto account sits with a provider authorised in Spain, Sepblac can now suspend it for up to five business days on its own analysis. Your nationality does not protect the account — what matters is which legal entity holds it, not your passport. The same point came up in November 2026 when Binance told customers in France, Spain, Italy and Poland that their accounts would close at the end of the year: the entity holding the account decides which rules apply. If you live in Spain, check which entity holds your account, keep your KYC documents current, and keep records showing where your funds came from. A freeze is far easier to resolve when you can show the source of funds quickly.

**2. Indians who use European platforms.** Many Indian users hold accounts on global exchanges that operate across Europe. If your account is with a Spanish-authorised entity, the new rule reaches it. If it is with an entity authorised elsewhere, it does not — but the direction of travel is clear, and similar powers are being discussed in other European jurisdictions. Knowing the entity that holds your account is the single most useful piece of homework here.

**3. Anyone watching how India might respond.** India already has strong attachment powers — the Enforcement Directorate provisionally attaches property under PMLA, as it did with a Karaikal hotel in November 2026 in a case involving alleged gold smuggling, hawala and illegal crypto transfers — and FIU-IND already requires exchanges to register and report. What India does not have is a five-day, FIU-initiated freeze of this kind. Spain's experiment gives Indian policymakers a live example to study: a fast first move by the intelligence unit, with a court required only to extend it. Whether India follows is a question for lawmakers, not for this guide — but the comparison is now on the table.

One thing that does not change: Indian tax rules. Crypto gains in India are taxed at a flat 30% with a 4% cess and no set-off of losses, and sales attract 1% TDS. ## What to Do If an Account Is Frozen

A freeze under this decree is temporary and suspicion-based, not a finding of wrongdoing. If it happens to you, the practical steps are straightforward:

- **Do not panic, and do not try to move the funds another way.** Attempting to route around a freeze can itself look suspicious and make the situation worse.
- **Contact the platform first.** Ask which authority ordered the suspension, what it covers, and what documents they need from you. The platform is legally required to carry out the order, but it can usually tell you what happens next.
- **Gather your source-of-funds documents.** Bank statements showing how you funded the account, salary or business income records, and the transaction history that shows the money's path. In money-laundering investigations, the question is almost always "where did this come from?" — and a clear paper trail is the fastest answer.
- **Note the deadline.** An account freeze lapses after five business days and a transaction hold after ten, unless a prosecutor asks a court to extend it. If the freeze is still in place after that, a judge is now involved, and you should get legal advice.
- **Get a lawyer in Spain if the freeze is extended.** Once a court is involved, this is no longer a customer-service problem. A Spanish lawyer who handles financial regulation can challenge the extension and argue for release.
- **Keep records of everything.** Screenshots of the freeze notice, emails from the platform, and the dates of every communication. If the freeze turns out to have been wrongly applied, that record is what you will need.

None of this is legal advice. It is a checklist of practical steps, and the details of any individual case should be handled by a qualified professional.

## The Bigger Picture

Spain's decree is a small piece of a much larger shift. In November 2026 alone, the United States cut off MEXC through FinCEN, the European Union created a Crypto Sanctions Task Force, the UK moved to bring DeFi platforms under authorisation, South Korea proposed reopening ICOs under disclosure rules, and Binance wound down accounts in four European countries after failing to win a MiCA licence. The common thread is that regulators are no longer content to supervise crypto from a distance. They want the ability to act fast — to freeze, to suspend, to cut off — and to let the courts sort out the longer questions afterwards.

For Indian users, the lesson is not about Spain. It is about the direction of regulation everywhere: compliance is becoming the price of access. Exchanges that cannot meet anti-money-laundering rules lose licences, lose banking partners, or get cut off by foreign regulators. Users who keep their KYC current, keep their records clean, and know which legal entity holds their account are the ones least likely to be caught out when the rules change.

Spain's new power takes effect on 25 January 2027, if Congress validates the decree within 30 days. Until then, the story to watch is the validation vote — and whether other European countries follow Spain's lead and give their own financial intelligence units the same fast first move.

## Conclusion

Spain's Royal Decree-Law 44/2026 gives Sepblac something it did not have before: the power to freeze a crypto, bank or payment account for five business days — or hold a transaction for ten — on its own analysis, without a judge and without waiting for a foreign request. The freeze is temporary, the court still decides anything longer, and the decree still needs Congress to validate it within 30 days. But the direction is unmistakable: financial watchdogs want speed, and they are getting it.

For Indians, the practical takeaway is simple. Know which legal entity holds your crypto account. Keep your KYC and your source-of-funds records in order. And remember that a freeze is a suspicion-based pause, not a verdict — the deadline, the documents and the court are the three things that decide what happens next.
