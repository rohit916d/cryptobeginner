On 5 November 2026, Bitcoin climbed back above $80,000, and in the same 24 hours nearly $1 billion in leveraged crypto positions were wiped out. Most of those losses fell on traders who had bet that prices would keep falling. That is the signature of a crypto short squeeze: a rising price forces losing bets to close, and the closing itself pushes the price higher. This guide explains what happened, how a short squeeze actually works step by step, and how to read the next headline about one without being misled by it.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a reported market event and a trading concept; it does not recommend any coin, platform, or trade, and it does not predict prices.

## What Happened on 5 November

According to Coinglass data reported by TradingView and BeInCrypto on 5 November 2026, total crypto liquidations reached about $974.80 million in 24 hours as Bitcoin rose 4.64% to about $80,480. Short positions — bets that prices would fall — made up $575.48 million, or about 59%, of that total. Long positions lost about $399.32 million over the same window. Coinglass counted 128,911 traders liquidated in the period, and Binance carried about 39% of all liquidations, the largest share of any exchange.

Bitcoin itself accounted for about $396.04 million of the liquidations, and shorts made up about 78.2% of Bitcoin's total — a much heavier short skew than the market-wide figure. Ethereum rose about 6.84% over the same 24 hours to about $2,418, with about $241.79 million in liquidations, of which shorts were about 56.5%. Bitcoin Magazine's separate account of the same session put the total at about $950 million, with about $560 million in shorts and about $403 million in Bitcoin positions, about 76% of them shorts. The small differences between these accounts are normal: they reflect slightly different time windows and exchange coverage, not a disagreement about what happened.

For Indian readers, the price levels in rupees help put the move in context. The Sunday Guardian's market roundup on the morning of 5 November put Bitcoin at about ₹75.91 lakh after a 3.4% rise to about $77,661.73, following a dip to a multi-month low near $75,728.70 earlier in the day. In other words, the squeeze unfolded after a sharp fall had already convinced many traders that further declines were the likely outcome. That sequence — a fall, a buildup of short bets, then a sharp reversal — is the classic setup for a squeeze.

The broader backdrop also mattered. The squeeze coincided with the aftermath of the United States midterm elections, in which Democrats took control of Congress, and with easing pressure from United States spot Bitcoin exchange-traded funds: Coinotag reported that ETF outflows fell to about $116 million on 3 November, down from roughly $643 million in the previous session. None of this means the election or the ETF flows "caused" the squeeze in any simple sense. It means the market entered the session with heavy short positioning and then received news that did not confirm the bearish case, which is often all it takes for a reversal to turn violent.

## What "Shorting" Actually Means

To understand a squeeze, you first need to understand a short position. When traders "go long," they buy an asset hoping its price rises. When they "short," they do the opposite: they borrow the asset, sell it at the current price, and plan to buy it back later at a lower price, returning it to the lender and keeping the difference. If the price falls, the short trader profits. If the price rises, the short trader loses — and unlike a long position, where the worst case is the asset going to zero, a short position's losses grow without a natural limit as the price climbs.

Most short squeezes involve leverage, which is borrowed money on top of the trader's own funds. With 10x leverage, for example, a trader controls a position ten times larger than their own money. That multiplies both gains and losses: a 10% move against the position wipes out the trader's own funds entirely. Exchanges protect themselves by liquidating such positions automatically — force-closing them once the trader's margin, the collateral backing the position, falls below a required level. Liquidation is not a choice the trader makes in the moment. It is an automatic sale, or in the case of a short, an automatic buy-back, executed by the exchange.

This is the key mechanical fact behind every squeeze. A liquidated short is not a trader calmly deciding to exit. It is a forced market buy order, executed at whatever price the market offers, and it adds upward pressure to the price at exactly the moment when other short traders are closest to their own liquidation levels.

## How a Squeeze Unfolds, Step by Step

A short squeeze typically follows a recognisable sequence, and the 5 November event fits it closely.

First, a falling market builds up short interest. After Bitcoin's slide toward multi-month lows, many traders concluded that further declines were likely and opened leveraged short positions. Each new short adds to a pool of traders who must eventually buy the asset back, whether they want to or not.

Second, the price turns. The trigger can be almost anything — an election result, a slowing of fund outflows, a large buyer entering the market, or simply the exhaustion of sellers. On 5 November, Bitcoin pushed back through levels where many shorts had placed their stop-losses and liquidation points, including the psychologically important $80,000 mark that it had struggled to hold since late October.

Third, the weakest shorts are liquidated. Their forced buy-backs push the price higher. That rise triggers the next layer of shorts, whose forced buy-backs push the price higher still. This feedback loop is the squeeze itself: rising prices cause buying, which causes rising prices.

Fourth, the loop runs out of fuel. Once most of the vulnerable short positions have been closed, the forced buying stops. Whatever buying remains is voluntary, and if no new buyers step in, the price can stall or fall back. That is why squeezes are often described as fast and violent but short-lived: the fuel is finite, because it consists of a specific pile of losing positions, not an ongoing stream of new demand.

One useful detail from the 5 November data illustrates this. Bitcoin's liquidations were far more short-heavy (about 78%) than the market average (about 59%), and Ethereum's were closer to balanced (about 56% shorts). That pattern suggests the squeeze was concentrated in Bitcoin, where short positioning had built up most heavily, rather than a uniform wave across every asset. Reading the split between longs and shorts, not just the headline total, tells you where the pressure actually was.

## Short Squeeze vs Liquidation Cascade: Two Directions, One Mechanism

Readers of this site may notice a resemblance to the liquidation cascade explained here on 29 October 2026, when a falling market wiped out leveraged long positions. The two events are mirror images of each other, driven by the same machinery running in opposite directions.

In a liquidation cascade, prices fall, leveraged long positions are force-sold, and the forced selling pushes prices lower, triggering more liquidations. In a short squeeze, prices rise, leveraged short positions are force-bought back, and the forced buying pushes prices higher, triggering more liquidations. Both are feedback loops. Both are caused by leverage, not by any change in the underlying value of the asset. And both can happen in the same market within days of each other, because a market can squeeze shorts upward in the morning and cascade longs downward at night.

Understanding this symmetry is the single most useful thing a beginner can take from either event. Headlines that report "nearly $1 billion liquidated" sound like news about value, but they are really news about leverage: about how much borrowed money was positioned the wrong way when the price moved. The size of the liquidation figure tells you how much leverage was in the system, not how much the asset is worth.

## How to Read the Next Squeeze Headline

Liquidation headlines are easy to misread, so it is worth learning to check four things before drawing any conclusion from one.

First, check the time window. Different trackers report different totals because they cover different 24-hour periods and different exchanges. On 29 October, for example, one tracker reported about $150 million and another about $356 million for overlapping but different windows. On 5 November, TradingView's Coinglass-based total of about $974.80 million and Bitcoin Magazine's roughly $950 million describe the same event through slightly different lenses. Neither is "wrong"; they are just measured differently. Always ask which window a figure covers.

Second, check the long-versus-short split. A total dominated by liquidated shorts tells a different story from one dominated by liquidated longs. The first means the market punished bearish bets; the second means it punished bullish ones. A roughly even split usually means a volatile two-sided session rather than a one-directional squeeze.

Third, check whether the move is backed by anything beyond forced buying. A useful clue is open interest, the total value of outstanding futures positions. When prices jump and open interest jumps with them, new money is entering the market. When prices jump but open interest falls, the move may be powered mainly by traders exiting losing shorts in a hurry — a squeeze that can fade quickly once the covering is done. Our earlier guide to open interest explains this distinction in more detail.

Fourth, resist the temptation to treat a squeeze as a forecast. A squeeze says that a lot of traders were positioned the wrong way and were forced out. It does not say that the asset will keep rising, that the worst is over, or that the price has found its "true" level. Prices can and do fall back after squeezes, sometimes within hours, once the forced buying is exhausted. The United States employment report due on 6 November, noted by Coinotag as the next scheduled variable, is a reminder that new information arrives constantly and can reverse a squeeze-driven move just as quickly.

## What This Means for Indian Users

For most Indian crypto users, the practical lesson is about leverage, not about Bitcoin's price. If you buy crypto on a spot exchange with your own rupees and hold it, a price move cannot liquidate you. Your holding may fall in value, but no exchange will force-sell it because of a margin call. Liquidation risk only appears when you trade with borrowed money — through futures, perpetual contracts, or margin products, which are typically offered by offshore platforms rather than by Indian exchanges.

That distinction matters because the 5 November liquidations were overwhelmingly a leveraged-trading story. The 128,911 traders counted by Coinglass were not ordinary holders; they were people with leveraged positions that moved against them. For a beginner, the safest way to stay out of that statistic is simply not to use leverage until you fully understand margin requirements, liquidation prices, and how quickly a leveraged position can be closed automatically.

Indian tax rules are also worth keeping in mind when reading about fast-moving markets. Gains from crypto are taxed at 30%, with 1% TDS on transfers. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. A squeeze-driven gain or loss does not change those rates, but the speed of a squeeze can tempt traders into rapid, repeated trades, each of which carries its own tax and fee consequences. Slow, deliberate decisions are cheaper than fast, emotional ones in more ways than one.

Finally, be sceptical of social media accounts that claim a squeeze "proves" a market direction. The accounts that profit most from squeeze headlines are often those selling leverage, signals, or courses. A squeeze is a fact about positioning, not a recommendation, and anyone presenting it as one is selling something.

## The Bottom Line

The 5 November short squeeze is best understood as a lesson in market mechanics. A falling market encouraged a large pile of leveraged short bets. The price turned, the weakest bets were force-closed by buying, and that forced buying pushed the price higher until most of the pile was gone. Nearly $1 billion in positions vanished in the process — not because the underlying asset changed, but because leverage turns ordinary price moves into forced trades.

If you remember one thing from this guide, make it this: in leveraged markets, price moves do not just reflect what traders think. They also force traders to act, and those forced actions move prices further. That feedback loop is what makes crypto markets so violent, and it is why the least leveraged participant in the room is usually the one who sleeps best.
