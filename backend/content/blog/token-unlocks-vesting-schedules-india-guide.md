When a new crypto project launches, not everyone gets their tokens at once. The team, early investors, and advisers usually receive their share on a timetable — locked first, released gradually. Those release dates are called token unlocks, and the timetable behind them is called a vesting schedule. If you have ever wondered why a token's price suddenly wobbled on an otherwise quiet day, an unlock is one of the first things experienced researchers check.

This guide explains token unlocks and vesting schedules in plain language: what the terms mean, who gets locked tokens and why, how unlocks affect supply, and how to check a schedule before you buy. No jargon, no price predictions — just the mechanics every beginner should understand.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose.

## What Is a Vesting Schedule?

Vesting is a promise with a timer. Instead of handing someone their full token allocation on day one, a project locks those tokens in a smart contract and releases them slowly over months or years. Think of it like a salary paid monthly rather than a lump sum on your first day: the arrangement keeps people committed over time instead of letting them walk away immediately with everything.

A vesting schedule has three basic parts. First, the **total allocation**: how many tokens a group (say, the team) will receive overall. Second, the **cliff**: an initial waiting period, often six or twelve months, during which nothing is released at all. Third, the **release pattern** after the cliff: tokens may drip out steadily each month (linear vesting) or arrive in chunks at set dates (for example, 25% every six months).

Here is a simple example. Suppose a project's team is allocated tokens with a one-year cliff and monthly vesting over three more years. For the first year, the team receives nothing. After the cliff, a small slice unlocks each month for the next three years. By the end of year four, the full allocation has been released. The team cannot sell what it does not yet hold, which is exactly the point: the schedule ties their reward to sticking around and building.

## Who Gets Locked Tokens?

Locked tokens are not a punishment — they are how most serious projects distribute supply fairly over time. The main groups are:

**The team and founders.** Their tokens are usually locked the longest, often with a cliff of a year or more. This reassures everyone else that the people building the project cannot sell everything on launch day.

**Early investors.** People and funds that backed the project before launch typically buy at an early price, but their tokens are locked too, releasing gradually. This prevents early backers from flooding the market the moment trading begins.

**Advisers and partners.** Smaller allocations for people who helped the project, usually with similar lockups.

**The community and ecosystem treasury.** Tokens set aside for future rewards, grants, or development are often released on a schedule controlled by the project or its governance process, rather than all at once.

**The public sale.** Tokens sold to the general public at launch are usually unlocked immediately — buyers can trade them straight away. This is why the freely trading supply at launch is often much smaller than the total supply that will ever exist.

Understanding who holds locked tokens matters because it tells you whose hands the future supply is in, and when those hands become free to sell.

## What Is a Token Unlock?

A token unlock is simply a date on which a batch of locked tokens becomes available. Unlocks happen on a published schedule, and many projects have several each year as different groups reach their release dates. Some unlocks are small — a fraction of a percent of total supply — and barely register. Others are large, releasing a big share of the circulating supply in a single day, and those are the ones that attract attention.

It helps to keep three supply numbers straight. **Total supply** is every token that will ever exist. **Circulating supply** is the number currently free to trade. **Locked supply** is the difference: tokens that exist but cannot yet be sold. An unlock moves tokens from the locked column to the circulating column. It does not create new tokens, but it does increase the amount that could be sold.

This is also where token unlocks connect to tokenomics, the economics of a token's supply. A project can look scarce on day one — small circulating supply, high demand — and then become much less scarce over the following years as scheduled unlocks arrive. Two tokens with the same launch price can have very different futures if one unlocks most of its supply in year one and the other spreads it over a decade. That is why experienced beginners read the vesting schedule before they read the price chart.

## Why Unlocks Can Move Prices

An unlock does not force anyone to sell, but it changes the balance of supply and demand. If a large batch unlocks and many recipients decide to sell at the same time, the extra selling pressure can push the price down. If recipients hold, stake, or sell slowly, the effect may be small or invisible. Context matters enormously: the size of the unlock relative to daily trading volume, whether the market expected it, and the general mood of the market all play a role.

There are a few patterns beginners should know. First, **anticipation**: because unlock dates are public, traders often react before the date itself, so the biggest move sometimes happens in the days leading up to an unlock rather than on the day. Second, **size relative to volume**: an unlock equal to a few days of normal trading volume is easier for the market to absorb than one equal to several weeks of volume. Third, **who is unlocking**: team and investor unlocks draw more attention than treasury unlocks, because the market assumes insiders are more likely to sell than a development fund is.

None of this is a reliable prediction tool. Unlocks are one factor among many, and prices move for countless reasons. The value of watching unlocks is not to guess the next price move, but to avoid being surprised by a known supply event — and to avoid buying into a token whose scarcity story collapses once you look at its schedule.

## How to Check an Unlock Schedule

Checking a schedule takes about ten minutes once you know where to look. Follow these steps before buying any token that is still in its vesting period:

**1. Read the project's own tokenomics page.** A credible project publishes its total supply, allocation breakdown, and vesting schedule in its documentation or whitepaper. Look for the team, investor, and treasury allocations, plus the cliff and duration for each. If you cannot find a schedule at all, that is itself useful information — a project hiding its supply plan deserves extra caution.

**2. Cross-check with an independent unlock tracker.** Several free trackers list upcoming unlocks by date, amount, and percentage of circulating supply. Compare the tracker's numbers with the project's own documents. If they disagree, trust neither until you understand why — the project may have changed its schedule, or the tracker may be out of date.

**3. Work out the next few unlocks, not just the next one.** Note the dates and sizes of the next three or four unlocks. A single small unlock is rarely important; a cluster of large unlocks over a few months is a different story. Ask yourself: how much will the circulating supply grow over the next year, and who will hold those newly unlocked tokens?

**4. Check whether the schedule has changed.** Projects sometimes amend vesting terms — delaying a team unlock, for example. Changes are usually announced, but they are not always easy to find. A quick search for the project name plus "vesting" or "unlock" before buying can surface recent changes.

**5. Watch for unlock-adjacent scams.** Fake "unlock" or "claim" pages are a common scam: a message promises early access to locked tokens if you connect your wallet or pay a fee. Real unlocks never require you to connect your wallet to a random website or send tokens to "activate" a release. If a claim page appears before an official announcement, treat it as a scam.

## Common Beginner Mistakes

The most common mistake is confusing a token's price with its value. A token trading at a low price per unit can still be expensive if billions of locked tokens are about to flood the market — and a token with a high unit price can be reasonable if most of its supply is already circulating. Always compare market capitalisation (price multiplied by circulating supply) alongside the fully diluted valuation (price multiplied by total supply). A wide gap between the two is a sign that large unlocks lie ahead.

Another mistake is assuming unlocks only matter for new tokens. Older projects still have unlocks too — treasury releases, staking reward emissions, and governance-controlled distributions can all add supply years after launch. The schedule matters for as long as locked tokens remain.

A third mistake is panic-selling or panic-buying around an unlock date based on social media hype. Unlock calendars are public information, which means the market has usually priced in at least part of the event before it happens. Making a rushed decision on unlock day itself, without understanding the size of the release or who is receiving it, is speculation, not research.

## Token Unlocks in India: What to Keep in Mind

For Indian users, unlocks do not change the tax rules — but they do affect your record-keeping. When you buy a token on an Indian exchange, the purchase itself is recorded by the exchange, and transfers on Indian platforms attract 1% TDS. Tokens that unlock into your own wallet, however, are a different matter: if you later sell them, you will need a clear record of when you received them, what you paid (if anything), and when you sold, to report the transaction correctly.

Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

There is also a practical point for Indian buyers. Many tokens are bought on Indian exchanges in INR pairs, but unlock schedules and tracker data are usually published in global terms. Before buying, confirm that the token listed on your exchange is the same token whose schedule you researched — contract address, network, and ticker should all match. Copycat tokens with similar names are a real scam risk, and buying the wrong one has nothing to do with unlocks and everything to do with verification.

## A Simple Unlock Checklist

Before buying any token with locked supply still outstanding, run through this short checklist:

- What percentage of total supply is still locked, and when does it unlock?
- Are the next twelve months' unlocks small, or do they add a large share to circulating supply?
- Who receives the unlocked tokens — team, investors, treasury, or community rewards?
- Does the project's own schedule match what independent trackers show?
- Is the gap between market capitalisation and fully diluted valuation wide or narrow?
- Have there been recent changes to the schedule, and were they announced transparently?

If you cannot answer most of these questions, you do not yet understand the token's supply story — and that is a good reason to pause, research more, or choose a simpler asset while you learn.

## Conclusion

Token unlocks and vesting schedules are not exotic insider knowledge. They are the public timetable of a token's future supply, and they explain a surprising amount of what happens in crypto markets: why some tokens stay scarce for years while others quietly inflate, why certain dates bring volatility, and why a low unit price can be misleading.

For beginners, the habit matters more than any single unlock date. Before you buy, spend ten minutes on the schedule: who holds locked tokens, when they unlock, and how much supply that adds. Combine that with the basics you already know — secure storage, scam awareness, and careful record-keeping — and you will be making decisions with far more information than most newcomers.
