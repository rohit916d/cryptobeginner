## Bithumb Data Breach Widened: 15 Days, Passports Exposed

Bithumb, one of South Korea's largest crypto exchanges, has admitted that its October data breach was far worse than it first reported. The attacker was inside the exchange's internal systems for about 15 days, not the single hour Bithumb originally disclosed. More people were affected than the 7,159 first reported, and the exposed data now runs to 19 categories — including passport numbers, bank account numbers, copies of identity documents, transaction histories and crypto wallet addresses.

The revised disclosure, published in an updated security notice on 25 November 2026 and reported the same day by Bitcoin Magazine, Chosun Biz, CoinDesk, crypto.news, Dave Finances, CoinMarketCap and Odaily, is the latest in a month of uncomfortable admissions from exchanges that first minimised an incident and then had to correct themselves. For Indian users, the story matters even though Bithumb is Korean: the same pattern — a vendor or internal system breached, personal data exposed, and a first disclosure that understates the damage — has hit exchanges Indians actually use, from CoinDCX to ZebPay, and the phishing risk it creates does not stop at any border.

This guide explains what Bithumb originally said, what the follow-up investigation found, what was actually exposed, what the exchange is doing about it, and what Indian crypto users should check.

## What Bithumb First Said in October

When Bithumb first disclosed the breach in late October 2026, the picture it presented was contained. An unauthorised outside party had accessed Daou Office, the exchange's internal mobile groupware system, for about an hour — from 11:28 a.m. to 12:27 p.m. Korea time on 27 October — and Bithumb said it had detected the intrusion at 12:14 p.m. that same day, while the attack was still in progress. The number of people affected was put at 7,159: 6,444 customers and 715 employees, including staff of partner companies.

That framing shaped how the incident was understood. An hour-long intrusion, caught within minutes, affecting a few thousand people, reads as a near-miss — a breach that was stopped quickly. It was reported that way, and it was filed away as one more entry in a month already crowded with Korean exchange-security stories: the Gopax halt on 6 November, the South Korean financial regulator finding deficiencies at 130 of 555 inspected firms, and the regulator's decision to let exchanges freeze withdrawals on mere suspicion of a hack.

The follow-up investigation has now rewritten that framing almost completely.

## What the Follow-Up Investigation Found

Bithumb's updated notice, published on 25 November 2026, says a further review of the same incident turned up a different timeline, a wider group of victims and a longer list of exposed data. The key findings, as reported by Bitcoin Magazine, Chosun Biz, CoinDesk and the other outlets carrying the update, are:

- **The intrusion lasted about 15 days, not one hour.** Bithumb now says an unauthorised outside party accessed Daou Office from 1:49 p.m. on 12 October to 3:22 p.m. on 27 October 2026 — a window of 15 days, one hour and 33 minutes. That means the attacker went unnoticed for more than two weeks, not the roughly one hour in the original disclosure.
- **More people were affected than the 7,159 first reported.** Bithumb has not given an updated count, but it says the follow-up review identified additional affected people, including customers and employees of partner companies, as well as current and former staff. The notice lists the affected groups as customers, partner-company employees and Bithumb's own current and former employees.
- **The exposed data now runs to 19 categories.** The list includes member numbers, names, mobile phone numbers, email addresses, dates of birth, resident registration numbers (South Korea's national ID number), passport numbers, driver's licence numbers, bank account numbers, home addresses, gender, nationality, copies of identity documents, occupations, employer names, work contact details, transaction histories and crypto wallet addresses. Bithumb says not every item applies to every person, and that affected users will be told individually by email and text message what was taken from them.
- **The breach was of an internal system, not the trading platform.** Bithumb maintains that Daou Office operates separately from the systems that support customer trading accounts and crypto assets, and that it has found no evidence that its exchange service systems or customer databases were directly breached. It says it has found no sign of direct intrusion into the trading platform.
- **No confirmed misuse has been reported.** According to the reporting of the update, Bithumb has not reported confirmed cases of identity theft, fraudulent withdrawals or account takeovers resulting from the exposed information. That is a statement about what has been confirmed so far, not a guarantee about what could happen next — personal data of this kind is typically used weeks or months after a breach, not hours.

Two points in that list deserve emphasis. First, the revision is not a small correction. The difference between a one-hour intrusion caught within minutes and a 15-day intrusion that went unnoticed for two weeks is the difference between a security team that stopped an attack and a security team that did not know it was happening. Second, the data now listed is not the kind that becomes harmless with time. A password can be changed. A passport number, a resident registration number and a copy of an ID document cannot — they are permanent identifiers, and they are exactly what identity thieves and impersonation scammers need.

## Why the 15-Day Window Changes the Story

The original disclosure invited a generous reading: Bithumb detected the intrusion at 12:14 p.m. on 27 October, while the attacker was still inside, and shut it down within the hour. The revised timeline does not support that reading. If the attacker first entered on 12 October, then the detection on 27 October came more than two weeks late — and the "one hour" figure was not the length of the attack, but the length of the final day's activity that Bithumb initially saw.

That matters for three reasons. The first is the volume of data. Fifteen days of access to an internal groupware system gives an attacker time to explore, to find the files worth taking, and to take them at leisure — which is consistent with the jump from the original data list to 19 categories. The second is the question of what else the attacker saw. Bithumb says the trading systems were not directly breached, and there is no evidence to contradict that — but the claim rests on the same internal investigation that originally missed two weeks of unauthorised access, which is why the reporting on the update is careful to attribute the "no direct breach" claim to the company rather than stating it as established fact. The third is the pattern it fits. This is not the first time this autumn that an exchange's first account of an incident has had to be revised: Unocoin first said no funds were lost in its November breach, then admitted about $200,000 had been stolen from three hot wallets. First disclosures in a crisis are often incomplete. The honest question is how incomplete, and for how long.

Bithumb has not explained why the first disclosure was so far off. It has said only that the new findings came from a follow-up review of the same incident. It has not said whether the attacker was active continuously across the 15 days, or in bursts — the reporting notes that the disclosure does not establish whether access was continuous throughout the period. That is an honest gap, and it is worth stating plainly rather than filling in.

## What Was Exposed: The 19 Categories

The full list of data categories Bithumb now says may have been exposed is worth reading in full, because it shows why this breach is more serious than a leaked email list:

| Category | Examples in the notice |
|---|---|
| Account identifiers | Member numbers |
| Contact details | Names, mobile numbers, email addresses, home addresses |
| Identity documents | Passport numbers, driver's licence numbers, copies of ID documents |
| National identifiers | Resident registration numbers, dates of birth, gender, nationality |
| Financial details | Bank account numbers, transaction histories |
| Employment details | Occupations, employer names, work contact details |
| Crypto details | Wallet addresses |

Three of those rows are the ones that should worry an affected user most. Passport numbers and ID-document copies are the raw material for identity theft: opening accounts, passing verification checks, or building a convincing fake identity. Bank account numbers, combined with names, phone numbers and addresses, are the raw material for social-engineering attacks on banks. And wallet addresses, combined with transaction histories, let a scammer know exactly how much crypto a person holds and where it moves — the information needed to craft a phishing message that looks like it comes from someone who genuinely knows the victim's holdings.

Bithumb has said that not every item applies to every person, and that it will notify each confirmed victim individually by email and text message, telling them what was taken from their records. It has also pointed customers to a dedicated online tool to check whether their own records were exposed, and said customers can file complaints through South Korea's Personal Information Infringement Report Center or seek mediation through the Personal Information Dispute Mediation Committee, both of which operate independently of the exchange.

## What Bithumb Says It Has Done

Alongside the revised disclosure, Bithumb set out the steps it says it has taken and will take. It says it has blocked the attacker's IP addresses, fixed the vulnerability behind the incident, and suspended use of the affected workplace platform. It says it is running a security review of all of its externally exposed services, has stepped up round-the-clock monitoring of unusual trades and withdrawals, and will require additional identity verification for important transactions.

It has also made a compensation pledge. In the words quoted by CoinMarketCap and Chosun Biz, Bithumb said it "will take responsibility for all harm confirmed to have resulted from this personal information breach and provide all necessary support and compensation to affected customers" — with each claim reviewed against the facts and relevant law before any payout. That is a real commitment, but it is also a conditional one: "confirmed" harm, reviewed case by case. Victims will need to prove the link between the breach and any loss, which is the hard part of any data-breach claim.

And it has issued the warning that now accompanies almost every exchange breach. Bithumb warned users that it never asks for passwords, verification codes or OTP details by phone, text or messenger, and urged people to stop responding to any communication requesting those details and to verify requests through the exchange's official app or customer service centre. That warning is not boilerplate. The weeks after a breach disclosure are the most dangerous period for victims, because scammers know the breach is in the news and can use its real details — the name of the system, the dates, the categories of data — to make their messages sound authentic.

## The Korean Context: A Month of Exchange-Security Failures

The Bithumb revision did not arrive in isolation. It is the latest entry in a Korean exchange-security story that has run all month, and it is worth seeing it in that context because the pattern is the same one Indian users have watched at home.

On 6 November, Gopax suspended deposits, withdrawals and then trading after a hack-threat email, and moved all holdings to cold wallets. South Korea's Financial Services Commission then found deficiencies at 130 of 555 inspected firms, held a meeting with chief information security officers on 19 November, and decided to let exchanges freeze withdrawals on mere suspicion of a hack — a power the law previously allowed only once a breach was clearly established. On 15 November, Coinone disclosed that a support-vendor breach had exposed passwords and national ID numbers. The Bithumb update, with its 15-day undetected window, is the same story from a different angle: not a trading-system hack, but a failure of the internal systems and monitoring around it.

That context is also why the revised timeline matters beyond Bithumb. The Korean regulator's crackdown was built on the finding that exchanges' internal controls were weak. A breach that ran undetected for 15 days in a major exchange's internal groupware is evidence for that finding, not against it — and it helps explain why the regulator moved to give exchanges the power to freeze first and ask questions later.

## What This Means for Indian Crypto Users

Bithumb is a Korean exchange, and most Indian readers will not hold an account there. But the risks this breach creates are not limited to Bithumb's customers, and the lessons apply directly to the exchanges Indians do use.

The first lesson is about the kind of breach that matters. The crypto stories that get the most attention are wallet thefts — coins drained from a hot wallet, a bridge exploited, a private key stolen. This breach is different: no crypto was reported stolen, and the trading systems were not reported breached. What was taken was identity. For an Indian user, that is the more common danger. The past month in India has been a run of exactly this kind of incident: CoinDCX disclosed that a messaging vendor, Plotline, had exposed customers' names, emails and linked bank names; ZebPay warned that a breach at its vendor MoEngage had exposed customer contact and device data; and the Delhi Police busted a Binance P2P phishing syndicate that used rented accounts and Instagram ads to drain victims' USDT. In each case, the attack surface was not the blockchain — it was the people, the vendors and the support systems around it.

The second lesson is about first disclosures. Bithumb's original account — one hour, caught within minutes, 7,159 people — turned out to understate the intrusion by a factor of 15 in time and to miss entire categories of data. Unocoin's first account of its November breach — no funds lost — was reversed days later with a $200,000 loss admission. The pattern is consistent enough to be a rule of thumb: when an exchange discloses a breach, treat the first version as the minimum, not the full picture, and check back for the revision. That is not cynicism; it is what the record of the past month shows.

The third lesson is practical, and it is the one that matters most for an Indian user who has ever signed up to a global exchange. If your passport number, your ID copy, your bank account number and your wallet address are in a leaked dataset, the attack that follows will not look like a hack. It will look like a phone call from your bank, a message from your exchange's support team, or an email about a withdrawal you never made — and it will use real details about you to earn your trust. The defences are unglamorous but effective: change passwords on any account that shares a password with the affected exchange, enable two-factor authentication everywhere it is offered, be suspicious of any message that asks for a password, a verification code or an OTP, and verify anything urgent through the exchange's own app or official support channel, never through a link in a message.

Indian users have one more protection worth knowing about. If you are the victim of a crypto-related fraud, or an attempt at one, report it to cybercrime.gov.in or call the 1930 helpline, and keep every message, transaction record and screenshot. Reporting does not guarantee recovery — in most of these cases it does not — but it creates the record that law enforcement and, in a case like Bithumb's, the exchange's own compensation process will ask for. And on the tax side, nothing about this breach changes Indian rules: gains on crypto transfers are still taxed at a flat 30% (plus applicable surcharge and 4% cess) under Section 115BBH, with 1% TDS under Section 194S on transactions above the specified thresholds, and losses cannot be set off against other income. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

## The Bottom Line

Bithumb's revised disclosure does not describe a new hack. It describes an old one that was worse than the exchange first said: 15 days inside its internal systems instead of one hour, more victims than the 7,159 first reported, and 19 categories of personal data — passports, bank accounts, ID copies, transaction histories, wallet addresses — instead of the shorter list in the original notice. No crypto was reported stolen, and Bithumb says its trading systems were not directly breached. What was taken was identity, and identity is the harder thing to replace.

For Indian users, the takeaway is not about Bithumb. It is about the pattern this autumn has repeated too often to ignore: exchanges disclosing breaches late, understating them at first, and correcting themselves only after a follow-up review. CoinDCX, ZebPay, Unocoin and now Bithumb have all followed some version of that arc in the past month. The defence does not depend on which exchange you use. It is the same in every case: assume the first disclosure is incomplete, check whether you are affected through the exchange's own channels, treat every breach-related message as a possible scam, and keep your passwords, your two-factor authentication and your records in order.

The facts to watch next are the ones Bithumb has not yet provided: an updated victim count, confirmation of whether the access was continuous across the 15 days, and the first compensation decisions under its pledge to take responsibility for confirmed harm. Until those arrive, the story stands as the clearest example yet of why a breach disclosure should be read twice — once when it is published, and again when the revision lands.
