Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin has warned that India's crypto tax rules could hold back the country's blockchain industry. Speaking on the sidelines of Devcon — Ethereum's annual global developer conference, being held in Mumbai for the first time — Buterin said the 30% tax on crypto gains and the 1% tax deducted at source (TDS) on transfers could disproportionately burden smaller developers and discourage innovation. His warning, reported by The Times of India on 9 November 2026, lands at a moment when India is trying to position itself as a place where blockchain technology is built, not just traded. This guide explains what Buterin actually said, how India's crypto tax works, why small developers are the ones most affected, what alternatives exist in other countries, and what — if anything — Indian users and builders should expect next.

## What Buterin Said in Mumbai

The Times of India reported on 9 November 2026 that Buterin, speaking on the sidelines of Devcon in Mumbai, said India's cryptocurrency tax regime could hamper blockchain innovation and undermine the country's potential to become a hub for building core blockchain infrastructure. The report was picked up the same day by CoinEdition, NewsBytes and other outlets, all carrying the same core facts.

The heart of the warning is about who pays the price of the tax rules. Buterin's point was not that large traders or wealthy investors suffer most — it was that smaller developers do. A 30% tax on gains and a 1% TDS on every transfer, he argued, can land hardest on the people doing small, experimental work: the developer testing a payment feature with tiny amounts, the student building a first decentralised application, the small team bootstrapping a product with almost no revenue. For them, the tax is not just a cost — it is friction on every transaction, and friction is what kills experiments before they become products.

Buterin also pointed to alternatives. He noted proposals in the United States for a de minimis exemption — a rule that would exempt small-value crypto transactions from tax — and tax exemptions in some countries for assets held for over a year. The underlying idea is the same in both cases: tax policy should distinguish between a large, deliberate investment and a small, everyday or experimental use, and it should not treat the two identically.

It is worth noting what Buterin did not say. He did not call for crypto to be untaxed. He did not predict that India will change its rules. And he did not say that India's tax regime is the only thing holding back its blockchain industry — only that it could become a major hurdle. The distinction matters, because the story is about a warning and a proposal, not about a policy change.

## How India's Crypto Tax Actually Works

To understand the warning, it helps to understand the rules themselves. India taxes crypto under the framework for Virtual Digital Assets (VDAs), and the two numbers that matter most are 30% and 1%.

**The 30% tax on gains.** Income from the transfer of a virtual digital asset is taxed at a flat 30%, plus applicable surcharge and cess. The rate applies regardless of how long you held the asset and regardless of your income slab — a small trader and a large investor pay the same percentage. Only the cost of acquisition can be deducted; no other expenses are allowed. Losses from crypto cannot be set off against other income, and they cannot be carried forward to future years either. So a loss in one year cannot reduce the tax on a gain in the next.

**The 1% TDS on transfers.** Under Section 194S of the Income-tax Act, 1% tax is deducted at source when a resident is paid for transferring a VDA. The TDS applies above certain thresholds — ₹50,000 in a year for specified individuals and Hindu Undivided Families, and ₹10,000 for other payers — and it is deducted even when the transaction itself makes no profit. The TDS is not an extra tax on top of the 30%; it is collected in advance and can be adjusted against the final tax liability. But it is collected at the moment of the transaction, which means it affects cash flow on every qualifying transfer, profitable or not.

**Why the two rules combine into a burden.** For a large, infrequent trade, the 30% rate is the main cost and the 1% TDS is a minor cash-flow timing issue. For a small developer making many small transactions, the picture reverses: the 1% TDS applies to each transfer, the record-keeping multiplies with every transaction, and the 30% rate applies to any gain with no way to offset the many small losses that experimentation inevitably produces. That is the mechanism behind Buterin's warning — it is not the rate alone, but the way the rate, the TDS and the no-set-off rule interact for someone doing many small transactions.

## Why Small Developers Are the Ones Most Affected

Buterin's warning is specifically about innovation, not about trading, and the distinction explains why small developers sit at the centre of it.

Building blockchain applications is not like buying and holding a coin. A developer testing a payment feature might move tiny amounts of crypto dozens or hundreds of times in a day — paying a fraction of a rupee's worth of tokens to test a smart contract, sending test funds between wallets, or settling micro-payments to see whether a design works. Each of those movements is a transfer, and each transfer can trigger tax and record-keeping obligations. When the amounts are tiny, the compliance cost can easily exceed the value of the transaction itself.

The problem is compounded by the no-set-off rule. Experimentation produces losses — most test transactions lose a little money to fees, price movement or failed attempts. Under India's rules, those losses cannot be used to reduce tax on the occasional gain. A developer who makes a small gain on one test and small losses on fifty others is taxed on the gain and gets no relief for the losses. Over a year of experimentation, that asymmetry adds up.

There is also a simpler, human effect. When every small transaction carries tax paperwork, many people simply do not experiment. They build elsewhere, or they build with test networks that never touch real value, or they do not build at all. That is the "discourage innovation" part of Buterin's warning: the tax does not have to be large to change behaviour — it only has to be more complicated than the experiment is worth.

This is also why Buterin's warning is aimed at policymakers rather than at traders. A trader can choose to trade less often, or to hold longer, or to accept the tax as a cost of doing business. A developer building infrastructure does not have those options — the small transactions are the work itself. If the rules make the work expensive, the work moves somewhere the rules are friendlier.

## What Other Countries Do Differently

Buterin pointed to two specific models from other countries, and both are worth understanding because they show what a friendlier version of the rules could look like.

**The US de minimis proposal.** In the United States, proposals have circulated for years to exempt small crypto transactions from capital gains tax. The idea is simple: if you spend a small amount of crypto — the often-cited figure is $200 or less per transaction — you do not owe capital gains tax on it and do not have to report it. The point is to remove the paperwork from everyday use, so that paying for a coffee with crypto is treated like paying with cash rather than like selling an investment. The proposal has been introduced in Congress in various forms but has not become law, which is itself a useful reminder: proposals and laws are different things, in the United States as in India.

**Long-term holding exemptions.** In some countries, crypto held for more than a year is taxed at a lower rate or not taxed at all. Germany, for example, has long exempted private crypto gains after a one-year holding period. The logic is that a long holding period signals investment rather than speculation, and that the tax system should reward patience. India does not make this distinction — the 30% rate applies whether you held for a day or a decade.

Neither model is a perfect fit for India, and neither was presented by Buterin as a ready-made solution. They are examples of a principle: that tax rules can be designed to distinguish between different kinds of use, and that a flat rule applied identically to a large investor and a small developer will burden the developer more. Whether India adopts any version of that principle is a decision for the Indian government, and it will be weighed against the government's own priorities — revenue, preventing tax evasion, and the administrative cost of running a more complex system.

## Why This Warning Matters in India Right Now

The timing of Buterin's remarks is part of the story. Devcon 8 is being held in Mumbai from 3 to 6 November 2026, the first time the conference has come to India, at the Jio World Centre in Bandra Kurla Complex. The conference has drawn thousands of developers to India, and Buterin's own keynote on 3 November — about decentralised hardware, local AI and treating personal data carefully — was itself a major story in Indian crypto coverage.

The warning also arrives in a year when India's crypto policy has been under active discussion on several fronts. The Enforcement Directorate has been investigating cross-border crypto transactions, the crypto industry has been debating the tax rules with the government for years, and Indian developers have repeatedly said the 30% rate plus 1% TDS pushes activity offshore. Buterin's voice adds weight to that debate because he is not an industry lobbyist or a trader with a position — he is the co-founder of Ethereum, the platform most Indian blockchain developers actually build on, and he is speaking from inside the conference that brought the world's Ethereum developers to Mumbai.

There is also a practical reason the story has travelled widely in India. The 30% tax and 1% TDS are the two numbers every Indian crypto user already knows, and a warning about them from one of the most recognisable names in crypto is immediately understandable. It does not require explaining a new technology or a new regulation — it takes a rule everyone already lives with and asks whether it is doing more harm than good.

## What Buterin's Warning Does Not Change

It is important to be clear about the limits of this story, because tax warnings are often reported as though they were tax changes.

**Nothing in Indian law has changed.** The 30% tax on crypto gains and the 1% TDS on transfers remain in force exactly as before. No amendment has been announced, no bill has been introduced, and no official has responded to Buterin's remarks with a policy proposal. A warning from a developer, however prominent, is not a change in the law.

**No timeline exists.** Buterin did not say when, or whether, India should change its rules, and he did not predict that it will. The debate about India's crypto tax has been running since the rules were introduced, and it will continue through the government's own processes — budget discussions, industry consultations and parliamentary debate — on a timetable that nobody outside the government controls.

**The warning is about innovation, not about your tax bill.** If you trade crypto in India, your obligations are unchanged: the 30% rate applies to gains, the 1% TDS applies to qualifying transfers, and losses cannot be set off. Buterin's remarks do not create a new exemption, a new threshold or a new way to file. Anyone who changes their tax behaviour because of this story would be acting on a misunderstanding.

**The debate is not settled.** There are serious arguments on both sides. Supporters of the current rules say a flat 30% rate is simple to administer, hard to game, and appropriate for a speculative asset class, and that the TDS gives the tax department visibility into a market that was previously hard to track. Critics say the rules push activity offshore, punish small users and developers, and treat a technology with many uses as though it had only one. Buterin's intervention adds a prominent voice to the critics' side, but it does not resolve the debate.

## What to Watch Next

The story will develop, if it develops, through official channels rather than through further conference remarks. Here are the checkpoints that will tell you whether Buterin's warning is turning into a policy discussion.

**Responses from Indian officials.** If a minister, a tax official or a regulator responds publicly to Buterin's remarks, that response will be the first sign of whether the government is engaging with the argument. Silence is also informative — it usually means the issue is being handled through existing channels rather than in public.

**The Union Budget.** India's crypto tax rules were introduced through the Budget, and any change to them would most likely come through the same route. The Budget is the natural moment for a de minimis exemption, a rate change or a holding-period distinction to appear — or not to appear.

**Industry submissions.** Indian crypto industry bodies have been making representations to the government about the tax rules for years. If Buterin's remarks prompt a new round of submissions, or a new proposal built around the de minimis idea, that will be visible in industry statements and reporting.

**Developer activity.** The most practical test of Buterin's warning is whether Indian developers keep building in India. Conference attendance, hackathon participation and the number of Indian-built projects are all public signals, and they will tell the story more reliably than any single speech.

For now, the most useful thing an Indian reader can do is the least dramatic one: understand the rules as they stand, keep proper records of every transaction, and follow the official channels for any change. Tax policy changes slowly, and it changes through announcements, not through headlines.

## The Bottom Line

On 9 November 2026, speaking on the sidelines of Devcon in Mumbai, Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin warned that India's 30% tax on crypto gains and 1% TDS on transfers could hamper blockchain innovation and undermine the country's potential to become a hub for building core blockchain infrastructure. His argument is that the rules fall hardest on small developers, whose work involves many tiny transactions, and he pointed to a US proposal for a de minimis exemption on small-value transactions and to long-term holding exemptions in other countries as friendlier models.

Nothing in Indian law has changed. The 30% rate and 1% TDS remain in force, losses still cannot be set off, and no official response or policy proposal has followed Buterin's remarks. But the warning matters because it comes from the co-founder of the platform most Indian blockchain developers build on, at the first Devcon ever held in India, and because it puts a specific, testable idea — exempt small transactions — into a debate that has so far been mostly about the headline rate. Whether that idea goes anywhere will be decided in New Delhi, not in Mumbai, and the checkpoints to watch are official responses, the Union Budget and the industry's own submissions.
