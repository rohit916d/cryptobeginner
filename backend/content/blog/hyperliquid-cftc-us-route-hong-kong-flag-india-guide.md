# Hyperliquid Squeezed: Hong Kong Flags It, CFTC Sets US Route

Hyperliquid, the on-chain exchange where traders buy and sell perpetual futures — contracts that track a crypto price without an expiry date — had a rough regulatory week in mid-November 2026, and both blows landed within about 48 hours of each other. On 18 November, Hong Kong's Securities and Futures Commission added Hyperliquid to its list of suspicious virtual asset trading platforms, saying it is not licensed in the city. A day later, on 19 November, the chair of the US Commodity Futures Trading Commission, Michael Selig, told a Washington forum that on-chain platforms like Hyperliquid must either register as an exchange or partner with an already registered one if they want to serve retail traders in the United States. Neither announcement shuts Hyperliquid down. Together, though, they draw a clear line around the world's largest on-chain derivatives venue: the two biggest financial centres it is not licensed in have now both said so publicly, in the same week. This guide explains what each regulator actually said, what the US route Selig described looks like, and what Indian readers — who make up a large share of Hyperliquid's global user base — should and should not take away from it.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains reported regulatory statements; it does not recommend any exchange, token or investment, and it does not predict any price movement.

## What Happened: Two Fronts in 48 Hours

The two developments are separate — different regulators, different countries, different legal questions — but they arrived back to back, and they both concern the same platform.

| Date (2026) | Development | Source of the facts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 18 Nov | Hong Kong's SFC adds Hyperliquid and "Hyperliquid Hong Kong" to its suspicious virtual asset trading platforms alert list, naming app.hyperliquid.xyz/trade | SFC alert list; reported 19 Nov by CoinMarketCap, BeInCrypto, TradingView/FinanceMagnates, The Coin Republic, Bitcoin.com |
| 19 Nov | CFTC Chair Michael Selig says on-chain platforms like Hyperliquid must register as an exchange or partner with a registered one to serve US retail traders | Capitol Crypto Forum remarks, reported 19–20 Nov by The Block, CoinDoo, Digital Today, Newisty, Coinfomania |
| 21 May 2026 | UK Financial Conduct Authority adds Hyperliquid and the Hyper Foundation to its warning list | FCA warning list, as reported in the same coverage |
| 26 Jun 2026 | Singapore's Monetary Authority of Singapore places Hyperliquid on its Investor Alert List | MAS Investor Alert List, as reported in the same coverage |

The Hong Kong listing is the freshest of the four: the SFC entry is dated 18 November and carries a red "(New)" tag at the top of the regulator's suspicious-platforms page. The CFTC remarks are the freshest statement: Selig was speaking at The Hill and The Block's Capitol Crypto Forum in Washington on Thursday, 19 November, and was asked directly how a decentralised platform like Hyperliquid could enter the US market. His answer is the news — because until that moment, the CFTC had not spelled out Hyperliquid's route in public.

## What the CFTC Chair Actually Said

Selig's answer came down to two choices. As reported by The Block and several other outlets covering the forum, he said on-chain venues can enter the US retail market by registering as an exchange or by partnering with an already registered exchange, and that platforms can keep using blockchain technology while doing so — but they must follow the same rules as any other exchange. In the words quoted by Newisty from the forum: "You're going to have to come in and partner with an exchange or obtain your own exchange, which doesn't change the ability and the great features of crypto and blockchain."

Three details in the remarks matter for understanding them.

**The Backpack template.** Selig pointed to Backpack, a crypto exchange, as the example of the first route. Backpack's group obtained three US licences in the space of a week: its clearinghouse, TLD Clearing, Inc., was registered as a derivatives clearing organization on 13 November; its exchange, TLD Markets, Inc., was designated as a designated contract market on 16 November; and it received a futures commission merchant licence on 18 November. Selig had issued a statement on 16 November welcoming the exchange and clearing approvals for a firm using blockchain-based architecture to execute and settle derivatives — a statement that did not name the firm, but was issued the same day TLD Markets was designated. Backpack did not get a ruling that its technology is acceptable in the abstract. It got a separate licence for each regulated job — exchange, clearinghouse, broker — and each licence carries its own obligations, including anti-money-laundering programmes, customer protection and segregation of funds, market surveillance, financial resources, risk management and system safeguards. That is the first of Selig's two routes in practice.

**The partnership route already has a candidate.** On 29 October, Intercontinental Exchange — the company behind the New York Stock Exchange — and TradeXYZ announced a plan to build a new US exchange using Hyperliquid's HIP-3 technology, with trading only for users who have completed identity verification. That is the second of Selig's two routes: a regulated firm holding the licences, with Hyperliquid's technology underneath. But the plan still awaits regulatory sign-off, and reporting on it notes that additional approval from regulators is needed before the exchange can begin operating. Selig's remarks gave no timeline for it and said nothing about Hyperliquid's own plans.

**The remarks went beyond Hyperliquid.** Selig also said that single-stock perpetual futures are structured as futures contracts, not security-based swaps — because their daily funding payments work like variation margin — and that the lack of an expiry date does not automatically make them security-based swaps. On prediction markets, he said the CFTC would work with the Justice Department when states sue federally registered platforms to challenge the agency's authority, and that the agency regulates lawful derivatives contracts, not casinos or state-licensed gambling venues. Its proposed Rule 40.11 lists the public-policy factors it would weigh when reviewing event contracts. None of this changes Hyperliquid's status today. It does show the direction of the CFTC's thinking: on-chain venues are welcome inside the existing rulebook, and outside it they cannot serve US retail traders.

There is also political context worth stating plainly. Reporting on the remarks notes that President Trump said in August that Selig was working to bring Hyperliquid to the US, without saying whether that would mean registration, a separate domestic product or access through a regulated intermediary. Exchange operators CME Group and ICE have pushed the CFTC to require Hyperliquid to register. Selig's answer does not settle that argument — it describes who would have to hold the licences before anything changes.

## Hong Kong's Warning, Explained

Hong Kong's move is a different kind of action from the CFTC's remarks, and the difference matters. The Securities and Futures Commission did not fine Hyperliquid, freeze its assets or order it to stop trading. It added the platform to its alert list of suspicious virtual asset trading platforms — a public early-warning service covering entities that are unlicensed in Hong Kong and are believed to be, or to have been, targeting Hong Kong investors.

The entry names "Hyperliquid" and "Hyperliquid Hong Kong" and gives the trading website, app.hyperliquid.xyz/trade, as the website in question. The regulator's wording, as quoted across the coverage, is that the entity "purports to provide trading services in digital assets at the above website. It is not licensed by the SFC and is suspected of operating, or holding itself out as operating, a virtual asset trading platform in Hong Kong and/or actively marketing its services to the Hong Kong public." The notice does not identify a separate local office, and it does not discuss how Hyperliquid's trading system works or whether its blockchain has problems.

What the warning does, in practice, is three things. First, it tells Hong Kong investors that the platform sits outside SFC supervision, so the protections that come with a licensed platform do not apply. Second, it warns that clients of unlicensed platforms could lose their holdings if an operator collapses, stops trading, is hacked or misappropriates assets — and that legal remedies may not be available. Third, it advises investors not to respond to offers made by operators appearing on the list. The SFC has not announced a website block or trading suspension for Hyperliquid, and no affected-customer count or loss figure is involved, because no fraud is alleged.

The market reaction was muted. HYPE, Hyperliquid's native token, traded at about $67.89 on 19 November and near $65 on 20 November, down roughly 4% on the day and about 7–8% over the week, and about 34% below its 23 September record of about $97.96, according to CoinMarketCap and Bitcoin.com reporting. That is a normal trading move for a volatile token, not a collapse — and it is worth saying so, because a warning list is not the same as an enforcement action, and the price did not treat it as one.

## Three Warnings in Six Months

Hong Kong is not the first regulator to put Hyperliquid on a warning list. It is the third in about six months, and the sequence is the pattern.

| Regulator | Date | What it did |
| --- | --- | --- |
| UK Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) | 21 May 2026 | Added Hyperliquid and the Hyper Foundation to its warning list |
| Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) | 26 Jun 2026 | Placed Hyperliquid on its Investor Alert List |
| Hong Kong Securities and Futures Commission (SFC) | 18 Nov 2026 | Added Hyperliquid to its suspicious virtual asset trading platforms alert list |

Each listing says the same thing in different words: the platform is not licensed in that jurisdiction and may be offering or marketing services to local residents. None of them alleges fraud. None of them orders a shutdown. What they add up to is a picture of a platform that is large, global and — in the eyes of three major financial centres — operating outside their licensing frameworks. For context, the SFC's suspicious-platforms page holds 69 entries added since January 2020, ranging from copycat websites impersonating the licensed HashKey Exchange to offshore brokers, while only 13 platforms hold full SFC trading licences, from OSL and HashKey Exchange to Bullish, according to the regulator's licensed-platforms register last updated on 16 October.

That pattern is also why the CFTC remarks and the Hong Kong listing belong in the same story. The CFTC chair is describing the only two ways an on-chain venue can legally serve US retail traders. Hong Kong, the UK and Singapore are each saying the platform is not licensed in their market. The message from regulators, taken together, is consistent: the technology is not the problem — the licence is.

## Why This Matters: The US Route Is Narrowing

Until 19 November, the open question about Hyperliquid and the United States was whether the CFTC would treat on-chain venues as a special case. Selig's answer is that it will not. Platforms can keep their blockchain technology, he said, but they must follow the same rules as any other exchange. That closes off the idea that being decentralised, or being built on-chain, is itself a route into the US retail market.

It also matters because of the timing. The CFTC's own approvals of Backpack and tZERO — reported in our earlier guide — came with an explicit limit: the approvals did not authorise Hyperliquid, Polymarket or any other platform, and they created no new rules. Selig's 19 November remarks answer the question that limit left open: what would Hyperliquid itself have to do? The answer is to obtain its own exchange licence or partner with a firm that holds one. Backpack shows the first route is real — three licences in a week. The ICE and TradeXYZ plan shows the second route is being attempted — but it still needs regulatory sign-off, and Selig gave no timeline for it.

There is a further date to watch. Selig has targeted 14 December for a proposal on voluntary registration of crypto firms with the CFTC — the idea that companies could opt into CFTC oversight before being forced to. That proposal, if it appears, would show whether the agency wants to bring more of the industry inside its perimeter. And the CLARITY Act, the bill that would divide crypto oversight between the CFTC and the Securities and Exchange Commission, remains stalled in Congress. A law would settle the CFTC's jurisdiction over crypto for good; a continued stalemate leaves everything to the regulator's discretion — which is why a chair's public remarks carry as much weight as they do right now.

## What This Means for Indian Readers

For Indian users, the direct effect of both developments is small, and the indirect lessons are the ones that matter. Hyperliquid's interface excludes US persons under its terms of use, and the CFTC remarks change nothing about what an Indian user can do today. Hong Kong's warning is aimed at Hong Kong investors, not Indian ones. But the story still matters in India for four reasons.

**Hyperliquid is an offshore venue, and Indian rules treat offshore venues differently.** Hyperliquid is not registered with FIU-IND, India's Financial Intelligence Unit, and it is not licensed by any Indian regulator. Indian exchanges must register with FIU-IND and comply with anti-money-laundering rules; an offshore platform that has not done so sits outside that framework. That does not make using it illegal in itself, but it does mean there is no SEBI or FIU-IND protection if something goes wrong — no Indian regulator to complain to, no Indian deposit-protection scheme, and no Indian court order that can easily reach an offshore operator. The Hong Kong warning makes the same point in Hong Kong's words: on an unlicensed venue, legal remedies may not be available.

**Perpetual futures are the product at the centre of this story, and Indian exchanges do not offer them to retail users.** Perpetual futures are leveraged contracts — they let a trader take a large position with a small amount of money put down — and they are the product Hyperliquid is built around. Indian exchanges do not offer crypto derivatives to retail users, and Indian residents who send money abroad for crypto derivatives face foreign-exchange rules, including the Liberalised Remittance Scheme limits. Using a VPN or a foreign address to get around a geo-restriction would breach the platform's terms and could breach Indian law. The CFTC's insistence that on-chain venues follow the same rules as traditional exchanges is, in effect, the American version of a principle India already applies: the product does not become unregulated because the technology is new.

**The tax reality is unchanged.** None of this changes what an Indian investor owes. Gains on transferring virtual digital assets are taxed at a flat 30% plus a 4% cess — 31.2% in total — with no set-off of losses, and sales attract 1% TDS under Section 194S. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. A US or Hong Kong regulatory statement does not reduce Indian tax, does not create an exemption, and does not change which platforms an Indian resident is allowed to use.

**The scam warning.** A high-profile regulatory week like this one attracts impersonators. Expect fake "Hyperliquid US launch" announcements, fake customer-support accounts, and messages promising early access to a licensed US version of the platform or returns from the ICE partnership. Hyperliquid has not announced a US launch, no application or timetable exists, and the ICE and TradeXYZ exchange has not received regulatory approval. If anyone contacts you about investing through a new US Hyperliquid, or asks for a seed phrase, password or OTP in connection with it, treat it as a scam. Report fraud on India's National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal at cybercrime.gov.in or the 1930 helpline, and never share a seed phrase or password with anyone claiming to help.

A short checklist before using any offshore derivatives platform: check whether it is licensed in your country and whether it is registered with FIU-IND; read its terms of use for geo-restrictions; understand that leverage can wipe out a position quickly and that liquidation is automatic; keep records of every transfer for tax filing; and never trade with money you cannot afford to lose.

## What to Watch Next

Four things will show whether this week was a turning point or just a warning.

The first is the ICE and TradeXYZ plan. If the planned US exchange built on Hyperliquid's HIP-3 technology receives regulatory sign-off, it will be the first live example of Selig's partnership route — a regulated firm holding the licences, with Hyperliquid's technology underneath. If it stalls, the register-or-partner route will look harder than the remarks made it sound.

The second is the CFTC's 14 December voluntary-registration proposal. If it appears on schedule, it will show how the agency wants to bring more crypto firms inside its perimeter — and whether Congress lets it. A proposal is not a rule, and a rule is not a licence, but the direction of travel matters.

The third is the CLARITY Act. The market-structure bill remains stalled in Congress, and the US midterm elections of 3 November produced a Democratic sweep that some investors fear could mean greater scrutiny of the crypto industry. A law would settle the CFTC's jurisdiction over crypto; without one, the regulator's discretion — and the chair's public remarks — carry the weight.

The fourth is whether other regulators follow Hong Kong. The FCA and MAS listings came five months before the SFC's, and the SFC's entry sits at the top of its list with a "(New)" tag. If more jurisdictions add Hyperliquid to their warning lists, the pattern hardens. If the platform responds by seeking licences, the story changes. Either way, the direction is clear: the largest on-chain derivatives venue in the world is being asked, in public, by one regulator after another, to come inside the licensing frameworks — or stay outside the markets they cover.

## The Bottom Line

On 18 November 2026, Hong Kong's Securities and Futures Commission added Hyperliquid to its list of suspicious virtual asset trading platforms, saying it is not licensed in the city and is suspected of marketing its services to the Hong Kong public — a warning, not a fraud allegation and not a shutdown order. On 19 November, CFTC Chair Michael Selig told the Capitol Crypto Forum that on-chain platforms like Hyperliquid must either register as an exchange or partner with an already registered one to serve US retail traders, pointing to Backpack's three licences this week as the registration route and to the planned ICE and TradeXYZ exchange as the partnership route still awaiting sign-off. The UK's FCA and Singapore's MAS had already listed Hyperliquid in May and June. For Indian readers, the lesson is about the shape of regulation, not about a new product to use: the world's biggest on-chain derivatives venue is being told, in one jurisdiction after another, that the technology does not exempt it from the licence — and Indian rules, with FIU-IND registration, FEMA limits and a 31.2% tax on gains, already apply that same principle at home.
