Bitcoin mining difficulty fell 9.13% on 14 November 2026, the biggest single cut since June, as miners switched machines off and the network's computing power slid. The retarget landed at block 975,744, taking difficulty from 148.36 trillion to 134.82 trillion. This guide explains what mining difficulty actually is, why this cut was so large, what the miner-capitulation signals in the data mean, and what Indian readers should — and should not — take from it.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. This guide explains a network event and the economics of mining; it does not recommend buying, selling, mining or holding any asset.

## What Happened: The Facts at a Glance

The facts, as reported on 14 November 2026 by PANews, The Defiant, TokenPost and asic.tools, and as shown by the on-chain data they cite, are:

- **Difficulty fell 9.13% at block 975,744.** The block was mined at 05:02:50 UTC on 14 November 2026 (10:32 AM IST), and the new difficulty is about 134.82 trillion, down from 148.36 trillion. PANews, citing CloverPool data, and The Defiant both report the same figure, and TokenPost confirms the block height and the new value.
- **It is the largest cut since June.** The previous cut of this size was the 10.09% drop on 14 June 2026. An analysis dated 14 November counts this as the third cut of more than 9% in 2026, after 11.16% on 7 February and 10.09% on 14 June. TokenPost notes that of the 23 difficulty adjustments in 2026, 11 have been increases and 12 have been decreases.
- **Difficulty is now well below its peak.** The same analysis puts difficulty 13.6% below its peak of 156 trillion, set on 29 October 2025. The two adjustments before this one had both raised difficulty — by 5.54% on 29 October and 5.92% on 16 October — so the direction has reversed sharply in a single step.
- **Blocks were running slow.** The previous 2,016-block period averaged about 11.01 minutes per block, against the network's 10-minute target. That slowness is the direct trigger for a downward retarget: the protocol eases the puzzle until blocks come back to pace.
- **Computing power fell with it.** Daily hashrate was about 982 exahashes per second (EH/s) on 12 November, 901 EH/s on 13 November and 811 EH/s for the partial day on 14 November, according to mempool.space data cited in the day's coverage. TokenPost reports the network hashrate at 906.93 EH/s, down from 1,001.01 EH/s the previous week.
- **The next retarget is already pointing down.** The adjustment after this one, at block 977,760, was estimated at roughly 6.15% to 6.47% lower, for around 29 November — but that estimate is based on only a few percent of the new epoch being mined, so it is noisy and will move.

## What Mining Difficulty Actually Is

For a beginner, difficulty is easiest to understand as a thermostat. Bitcoin's miners compete to find the next block by guessing numbers — a process called hashing — and the network needs one block roughly every 10 minutes to keep transactions moving on schedule. If more machines join, blocks would come too fast, so the protocol raises the difficulty. If machines leave, blocks would come too slow, so it lowers the difficulty. The adjustment happens automatically every 2,016 blocks, about once a fortnight, and nobody votes on it or announces it in advance: the number is a mechanical consequence of how fast the last 2,016 blocks were found.

That is why the 9.13% cut is informative. It is not an opinion, a forecast or a policy decision. It is the network's own measurement, written into the chain, that computing power fell enough over the last two weeks for blocks to run about 10% slow. The cut itself then makes mining easier for the machines that stay on: at a fixed hashrate, expected earnings per unit of computing power rise by about 10% before costs. The catch, as the day's coverage notes, is that Bitcoin's price fell about 8% over the same stretch, so most of that relief was eaten by the lower price before miners saw any of it.

Two related numbers help to read the situation. The first is hashrate, the total computing power pointed at the network, measured in exahashes per second. The second is hashprice, the dollars a miner earns per day from one petahash per second of computing power. An analysis dated 14 November puts hashprice at roughly $31.60 per PH/s per day, with Bitcoin at about $67,165. A separate market note puts the breakeven level for many miners near $31.89, the last dated Luxor print from 9 November. When hashprice sits near breakeven, the least efficient machines stop paying for their electricity, and their owners switch them off — which is exactly what a falling hashrate and a big difficulty cut describe.

## Why the Cut Was So Big: Miners Under Pressure

Three forces combined to produce the largest cut since June, and all three are visible in the numbers reported on 14 November.

**First, the price fell below many miners' costs.** CoinShares' Q2 2026 mining report put the weighted average cash cost of producing one Bitcoin at public miners at about $75,500, excluding depreciation. A separate market estimate put the average all-in mining cost near $78,000 per Bitcoin — about 17% above the market price on the day. The electricity cost alone varies enormously with the machine and the power price: the 14 November analysis puts it at roughly $27,500 per coin on a latest-generation machine paying $0.04 per kilowatt-hour, and around $120,000 on an older machine paying $0.08. When the price sits below the average cost of production, the weakest operators are mining at a loss, and the rational move is to switch off.

**Second, miners started sending coins to exchanges.** CryptoQuant recorded 2,077 BTC in miner-to-exchange transfers on 10 November, about 16 times the 30-day daily average of 127 BTC, and the largest daily miner-to-exchange movement since February 2026. Around 97% of that transfer came from Antpool, one of the network's largest mining pools. Transfers to an exchange are not the same as sales — the coins may sit there unsold — but miners move coins to exchanges when they need cash, typically to pay power bills and debt. A 16-fold spike in that flow, days before a record difficulty cut, is the classic signature of an industry under cash pressure.

**Third, the price slide and the mining slide fed each other.** Bitcoin fell to about $65,000–$66,755 on 14 November, a three-month low and a weekly loss of about 13.5%, according to CoinDesk and Coinotag. On-chain analyst James Check described the move as the fourth capitulation event since the all-time high, with the sellers mostly being buyers who had entered in September in the $80,000s and were now selling at a loss. Check also said Bitcoin was in a "deep value zone", last seen in February and late June 2026, and before that in late 2022 — while cautioning that his assessment does not amount to a confirmed bottom. Spot Bitcoin ETFs recorded a seventh straight day of outflows on 13 November, and the Fear & Greed index fell to 13, deep in extreme fear. None of that is a prediction; it is the backdrop that explains why machines went dark.

It is worth noting what has *not* happened yet. The Hash Ribbons indicator, which compares the 30-day and 60-day moving averages of hashrate, had not formally flipped as of 14 November: the 30-day average, about 1,012 EH/s, was still above the 60-day average of about 992 EH/s. The indicator lags, and daily hashrate estimates are noisy, but the formal capitulation signal it tracks had not yet triggered. The difficulty cut is the faster, mechanical signal; Hash Ribbons is the slower confirmation. Both are worth watching, and neither is a buy or sell signal.

## What This Means for the Bitcoin Network

For users of the network, the practical effect of the cut is simple: blocks should come back to about one every 10 minutes, and transaction confirmation times should normalise. Fees were already minimal on 14 November — about 1 satoshi per virtual byte for a fast confirmation, according to mempool.space data — because the network was quiet. The cut does not change Bitcoin's supply schedule: the block subsidy remains 3.125 BTC, and the next halving is estimated for around 12 April 2028, about 74,200 blocks away.

For the network's security, the picture is more mixed. A falling hashrate means less computing power guards the chain, but the scale still dwarfs any attack: even after the slide, the network runs at roughly 900 EH/s, and difficulty at 134.82 trillion is still far above where it sat for most of Bitcoin's history. A single large cut after two increases is a normal part of the cycle, not a collapse — difficulty has fallen by similar amounts before, in February and June of this same year, and the network kept running. What the cut does show is that the mining business is going through a genuine squeeze, with weaker operators leaving and stronger ones absorbing the relief the retarget provides.

There is also a structural shift underneath the numbers. The day's coverage notes that hashrate kept falling after the cut, which points to machines actually going offline or being moved to other work, such as AI hosting, rather than just unlucky block timing. Paraguay, for example, is ending cheap power for miners in 2027 as AI demand pushes miners out. Miner stocks led the crypto-equity sell-off on Wall Street on 13 November. The industry is consolidating around whoever can get the cheapest power and the newest machines — a trend that predates this week and will outlast it.

## What It Means for India

Mining in India sits on the wrong side of every number in this story. Bitcoin mining is not banned in India — there is no dedicated law against it — but it is a scale business built on cheap electricity, and Indian residential power tariffs are typically far above the $0.04 per unit that the cheapest miners pay abroad. A home setup in India pays more for power, earns the same reward per unit of computing power as everyone else, and therefore reaches the switch-off point sooner. That is the practical meaning of the $27,500-to-$120,000 range for the electricity cost of one coin: where you sit in that range decides whether you are still mining after a week like this one, and most Indian home miners sit near the expensive end.

Three India-specific points follow from the facts:

- **Pooled mining is how almost all Indian miners participate, if they do at all.** Solo mining with a single machine is a lottery ticket; pools combine thousands of machines and split the rewards. Anybody offering "guaranteed daily mining returns" from a pool or a cloud-mining contract should be treated with the same suspicion as any other guaranteed-returns pitch in crypto — the economics above show that returns are not guaranteed for anyone, including the largest public miners.
- **Mined crypto is taxable.** Rewards from mining count as income from virtual digital assets, taxed at a flat 30% plus a 4% cess, with 1% TDS on transfers. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. Mining costs, including electricity, are generally not deductible against that 30% tax, which makes the economics harder still.
- **The real India angle is the price, not the mining.** Most Indian crypto users are buyers and holders, not miners. For them, the difficulty cut matters as an explanation of what is happening on the supply side of the network — machines switching off, miners selling to cover bills — while their own experience of the week is the price: about $65,000–$66,755 on 14 November, roughly ₹61–63 lakh at the day's exchange rate. The two are connected, but they are not the same story, and a difficulty cut does not tell a buyer what to do next.

## What Indian Readers Should Do — and Not Do

This is an educational moment, not a trading moment. A few practical steps follow from what is actually known:

1. **Do not buy a mining rig because difficulty just fell.** A lower difficulty makes mining easier for the machines already running, but it does not make a new purchase profitable. The price is below the average production cost of public miners, electricity in India is expensive, and the next retarget is already estimated to fall further — which would only help if the price holds, and it may not. Anybody selling you a rig or a cloud-mining contract on the back of this headline is selling you the headline, not the maths.
2. **If you already mine, check your own numbers.** Work out your electricity cost per coin on your machine and your tariff, and compare it with the $31.60 hashprice figure. If your cost is above your revenue, switching off until conditions change is a business decision, not a failure — it is what the network's largest operators are doing right now.
3. **Treat "miner capitulation" headlines with care.** The phrase sounds dramatic, but it describes a normal, repeated mechanism: unprofitable machines switch off, difficulty falls, the network rebalances. It has happened twice already in 2026, in February and June. The useful question is not whether capitulation is happening — the data says pressure is real — but what it means for the network, which is that it keeps running at a lower difficulty.
4. **Do not confuse a difficulty cut with a price bottom.** The cut follows the price fall; it does not predict the next move. The analyst quoted in the day's coverage called this the fourth capitulation event since the all-time high and cautioned that it does not amount to a confirmed bottom. Prices can keep falling after a difficulty cut, and they have done so before.
5. **Watch the next retarget, not the next headline.** The adjustment at block 977,760, due around 29 November, will show whether hashrate has stabilised or is still falling. An early estimate of another 6% cut is noisy with only a few percent of the epoch mined, so it will move — but the direction of that estimate over the next two weeks is the cleanest read on whether miners are coming back or still leaving.

## What This Does Not Mean

Three things are worth stating plainly, because they are easy to get wrong when a big number flashes across the news:

- **This is not a signal that Bitcoin is broken.** Difficulty cuts are designed into the protocol. The network slowed down, measured the slowdown, and corrected itself in a single step. That is the system working as intended, not failing.
- **This is not a signal that Bitcoin is cheap.** "Deep value zone" is one analyst's description of where the price sits relative to past cycles, not a valuation. The same coverage notes that the price is below the average cost of mining a coin, which is a fact about miners' costs — not a promise that the price must rise to meet them. Miners can stay unprofitable for a long time, and prices can stay below production costs for months.
- **This is not the same as a halving.** A halving cuts the new supply of Bitcoin in half on a fixed schedule. A difficulty cut changes how hard it is to mine, not how many coins are created. The next halving is estimated for April 2028, and nothing about this week's retarget moves it.

## The Bottom Line

On 14 November 2026, Bitcoin's mining difficulty fell 9.13% at block 975,744, from 148.36 trillion to 134.82 trillion — the largest cut since June, and the third cut of more than 9% in 2026. Blocks had been averaging about 11 minutes, hashrate had slid from 982 to 901 EH/s in two days, and miners were sending coins to exchanges at 16 times the usual rate, because the price had fallen below the average cost of producing a coin. The protocol did what it is designed to do: it made mining easier, so blocks return to about one every 10 minutes. For Indian readers, the lesson is practical rather than speculative — mining is a scale business built on cheap power, home mining in India rarely pays, and a difficulty cut explains the network, it does not predict the price. The number to watch next is the retarget due around 29 November, which will show whether miners are coming back or still switching off.
