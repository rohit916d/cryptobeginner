A crypto ATM looks like an ordinary cash machine — but instead of handing you rupees, it sells you Bitcoin or other crypto for cash. The idea sounds convenient: walk up, insert cash, scan your wallet, and walk away with crypto. So it is natural to wonder whether these machines exist in India, and whether you can use one.

The short answer is that crypto ATMs are a real thing around the world, but they are not a practical way to buy crypto in India. The country's only well-known crypto ATM was shut down within days of opening, and no licensed network operates here today. This guide explains what a crypto ATM is, how it works, why India is different, what the fees look like, and — most importantly — the safer ways Indians actually buy crypto.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains how crypto ATMs work for learning purposes only.

## What Is a Crypto ATM?

A crypto ATM, sometimes called a Bitcoin ATM or BTM, is a self-service kiosk that lets you buy or sell cryptocurrency using cash. Unlike a bank ATM, it does not connect to your bank account. Instead, it connects to a crypto exchange or operator's system, converts your cash into crypto (or your crypto into cash), and sends the crypto to a wallet address you provide.

There are two main types. A one-way machine only lets you buy crypto with cash. A two-way machine also lets you sell crypto and receive cash. Most machines support Bitcoin, and many also support Ethereum and a handful of other popular coins. You cannot buy every coin at an ATM — the machine only offers the coins its operator supports.

Crypto ATMs first appeared in the early 2010s and spread mainly across the United States and parts of Europe. At their peak in early 2026 there were roughly 39,400 machines worldwide, but that number fell sharply during 2026 to about 27,300 by October, as regulators in several countries tightened rules and some operators shut down. The United States still hosts the large majority of the world's machines.

## How Does a Crypto ATM Work?

Using a crypto ATM is straightforward, which is part of its appeal for beginners. The exact steps vary by operator, but a typical purchase works like this.

First, you choose the "buy crypto" option on the touchscreen and select the coin you want, usually Bitcoin. Next, the machine asks for your wallet address. You can scan the QR code of your wallet app with the machine's camera, or type the address in manually. This step is critical: if you enter the wrong address, your crypto goes to someone else's wallet, and the transaction cannot be reversed.

Then the machine asks for identity verification. In most countries, small purchases need only a phone number, while larger amounts require a government ID scan, a selfie, or both. This is the machine's version of KYC — the same identity check Indian exchanges require. Finally, you insert cash, confirm the amount and the exchange rate on screen, and the machine sends the crypto to your wallet. A receipt is printed or sent by SMS.

Selling works in reverse. You choose "sell crypto," send the crypto from your wallet to the address the machine shows, wait for the network to confirm the transaction, and then the machine dispenses cash. The wait for confirmation can take several minutes, and some machines issue a printed code that you redeem for cash only after the transaction is confirmed.

## Do Crypto ATMs Exist in India?

This is the question most Indian beginners actually want answered, and the answer is disappointing: no, there is no licensed crypto ATM network operating in India today.

India's only real attempt at a crypto ATM happened in October 2018, when the Bengaluru-based exchange Unocoin installed a machine at a mall in the city. The idea was to let customers deposit and withdraw rupees during the RBI banking restrictions of that time. Within a week, the police seized the machine and arrested a co-founder. The machine never returned, and no other operator has successfully run one since.

There are a few reasons for this. First, there is no licensing framework for crypto ATMs in India. Unlike a bank ATM, which operates under clear RBI rules, a crypto kiosk has no regulator to register with and no approved way to handle large cash deposits. Second, Indian law bars cash receipts of ₹2 lakh or more in a single transaction, which limits how a cash-based machine could operate at scale. Third, every crypto exchange serving Indian users must register with the Financial Intelligence Unit of India (FIU-IND) and follow anti-money-laundering rules — a cash kiosk that takes money without proper identity checks would break those rules.

You may still find websites that list crypto ATMs in Indian cities. Treat these listings with caution. Many are old, unverified, or simply wrong — some directories list dozens of machines in India, while others list none. A listing on a website is not proof that a machine exists, works, or is legal. If you ever come across a machine claiming to sell crypto in India, do not use it until you have verified who operates it, whether it is registered anywhere, and what happens to your money if something goes wrong.

It is worth being clear about the legal picture. Holding and trading crypto is not banned in India. You can legally buy crypto on a registered Indian exchange. What does not exist is a legal route for a cash-based crypto kiosk. The machine is the problem, not the crypto.

## Crypto ATM Fees and Limits

Even where crypto ATMs do operate, they are one of the most expensive ways to buy crypto. Fees are typically in the range of 5% to 15% per transaction — far higher than the trading fees on an Indian exchange, which are usually a fraction of a percent. On top of the stated fee, the exchange rate shown on the machine is usually worse than the market rate, which adds a hidden cost.

To see what that means, imagine you insert ₹10,000 in cash into a machine charging 10% in fees. Roughly ₹1,000 disappears in fees before you even account for the worse exchange rate, so you receive noticeably less crypto than ₹10,000 would buy on an exchange. For small amounts, the difference feels minor. For larger amounts, it adds up quickly.

Limits also apply. Most machines cap how much you can buy or sell per day, and the cap is lower if you have only done a basic phone-number check. Full identity verification raises the limit. Operators set these limits partly for their own risk management and partly to comply with anti-money-laundering rules in their country.

Fees and limits are another reason Indian beginners should not chase crypto ATMs. The machines are expensive, the limits are low, and in India they are not even available. The same ₹10,000 buys more crypto, with a clear record, on a registered exchange.

## Risks and Scams Around Crypto ATMs

Crypto ATMs carry real risks, and scammers know how to exploit them. One common trick is the "crypto ATM payment" scam: a fraudster posing as a bank official, police officer, or customer-support agent tells you to deposit cash into a crypto ATM and send the crypto to a wallet address they control. They may claim your bank account is blocked, or that you must pay a fine or a tax immediately. Once the crypto leaves your wallet, it is gone — crypto transactions cannot be reversed, and the police cannot easily trace the money.

Fake machines and fake listings are another risk. A machine with no visible operator name, no customer support number, and no registration details is a red flag. So is any listing or social media post that promises a crypto ATM with unusually low fees or guaranteed profits. Legitimate operators do not advertise through random Telegram groups or WhatsApp forwards.

There are also practical risks. If you type a wallet address wrong, your crypto goes to the wrong place and cannot be recovered. If the machine's exchange rate is far worse than the market rate, you lose money without realising it. And because crypto ATMs deal in cash, they are sometimes used for money laundering, which is one reason regulators in many countries have moved to ban or restrict them — several US states began restricting or banning crypto ATMs during 2026.

A simple rule protects you from almost all of this: never let anyone direct you to a crypto ATM, never send crypto to an address someone else gave you, and never pay a supposed fine, fee, or tax through a crypto ATM. Real banks, police, and government departments do not ask for payment in crypto.

## Safer Ways to Buy Crypto in India

If your goal is simply to buy crypto in India, you do not need a crypto ATM at all. Registered Indian exchanges offer a cheaper, safer, and fully traceable route.

Start by choosing a FIU-IND registered exchange — CoinDCX, WazirX, and ZebPay are well-known examples. Complete the KYC process with your PAN, Aadhaar, and a selfie, just as you would when opening a bank account. Then deposit INR using UPI or a bank transfer. UPI deposits are usually instant and cost little or nothing, which already beats any ATM fee.

Once your account is funded, you can buy Bitcoin or other crypto at the market rate, with the fee shown clearly before you confirm. Your transaction is recorded on the exchange, which matters for two reasons. First, you have proof of what you bought, when, and for how much. Second, you need those records for tax filing.

Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. In India, gains from selling crypto are taxed at a flat 30%, plus applicable cess, and a 1% TDS is deducted on transfers above the threshold. Keep a record of every buy, sell, and transfer, including dates and amounts in INR. You cannot set off crypto losses against other income, so accurate records matter even when you lose money.

If you want to hold crypto for the long term, consider moving it from the exchange to a wallet you control — but only after you understand recovery phrases and how to store them safely. For most beginners, keeping a modest amount on a reputable registered exchange while learning is a reasonable starting point.

## What to Do If You See a Crypto ATM in India

If you spot a machine in a mall, market, or co-working space claiming to sell crypto, pause before touching it. Check who operates it and whether that operator is registered anywhere in India. Search for the operator's name along with words like "scam" or "complaint" and see what comes up. Ask what fees it charges and what exchange rate it uses, and compare that rate with a live market price.

If the machine asks for cash with no identity check at all, walk away — a legitimate crypto business in India must verify its customers. If someone is standing nearby urging you to use the machine quickly, walk away faster. That is the pattern of the payment scams described above.

If you are travelling abroad and use a crypto ATM there, the same rules apply: verify the operator, check the fee and rate before confirming, double-check your wallet address, and keep the receipt. Remember that crypto you buy abroad still has Indian tax implications when you bring it back or sell it, so keep the same records you would for an exchange purchase.

## The Bottom Line

A crypto ATM is a simple idea — cash in, crypto out — but it is an expensive and tightly regulated one. Around the world, the number of machines is shrinking as regulators crack down and operators close. In India, the idea never got off the ground: the single attempt in 2018 was seized within a week, and there is still no licensing route for these machines.

For Indian beginners, that is not a problem. A FIU-IND registered exchange with UPI deposits gives you a better price, lower fees, proper records, and customer support — everything a crypto ATM promises, without the cash, the queues, or the risk of a machine you cannot verify. Learn how the machines work so you can recognise them and the scams around them, but do your actual buying on a registered exchange.
