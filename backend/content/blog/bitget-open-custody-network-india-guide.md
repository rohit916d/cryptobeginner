On 5 November 2026, Bitget announced a new service that tries to solve one of crypto's oldest problems: to trade on an exchange, you have normally had to hand your coins to that exchange first. The Open Custody Network, or OCN, breaks that link. It lets eligible users trade on Bitget's spot, margin and futures markets while their crypto stays with an independent bank or custodian. The exchange gets your orders; the custodian keeps your assets.

The timing is hard to miss. The announcement comes one week after the Binance security incident of 29 October, in which attackers drained roughly $6.3 billion from the exchange's hot wallets, and in the same week that India's CERT-In warned about AI-assisted hackers targeting crypto exchanges and stealing wallet keys. Custody — who actually holds your coins, and what happens if that holder is breached — is suddenly the question every crypto user is asking. Bitget's answer is to separate the two jobs: let the exchange do the trading, and let someone else do the holding.

## What the Open Custody Network Actually Is

In the traditional centralised exchange model, trading starts with a deposit. You transfer your crypto from your own wallet into a wallet operated by the exchange, and only then can you place orders. From that moment, the exchange is both your trading venue and your custodian. If the exchange is hacked, goes bankrupt, or freezes withdrawals, your funds are caught up in the same problem — as Binance users discovered in late October, when deposits and withdrawals were paused while the exchange dealt with the breach.

The Open Custody Network changes the first step. Instead of depositing assets with Bitget, an eligible user keeps them with a participating bank or institutional-grade custodian. Bitget's trading engine then connects to that custodian through a mechanism called asset mirroring. In simple terms, the custodian tells Bitget that the assets exist and are available, and Bitget creates a mirrored balance that the user can trade against. The real coins never move into Bitget's wallets; the trading happens against the mirror.

Bitget's own announcement, carried by GlobeNewswire on 5 November, describes OCN as "a new network connecting banks and institutional-grade custodians with Bitget's trading infrastructure for all eligible users." Its stated aim is to address a longstanding feature of the exchange industry: the concentration of custody and trading inside a single platform. Chief executive Gracy Chen put the idea in one line — users should not have to choose between custody and trading — and the network's slogan makes the same point: "Assets in Bank, Tradings on Bitget."

The service covers spot, margin and futures trading, according to both the announcement and Gadgets 360's coverage of the launch on 5 November. But there is an important qualification: the products available to any particular user vary based on the custodian they use and the settlement model that applies to them. In other words, OCN is not one identical product for everyone — it is a framework, and what you can actually do with it depends on who holds your assets and how settlement is arranged.

## The Custodians Behind the Network

A network like this is only as credible as the custodians in it, and Bitget had already been building that list before the launch. In an operational update on 13 October 2026, the exchange announced institutional custody partners, and Gadgets 360's 5 November report names six of them: Copper ClearLoop, Cactus Custody Oasis, Fireblocks Off Exchange, OSL MirrorEX, Bitfire PrimeMirror and Sygnum Protect.

These are not household names for most retail users, and that is itself informative. They are specialist firms that serve funds, trading desks and other institutions — the kind of clients who hold large balances and need their assets to sit with a regulated or independently audited custodian rather than on a trading platform. The arrangements operate under different settlement models, according to the Gadgets 360 report: some custodians let traders trade directly against their mirrored balances, while others use funding systems run through the custodial service. The practical effect is the same — the assets stay put — but the plumbing differs from custodian to custodian.

It is worth noting that Bitget is not the only exchange moving in this direction. Gadgets 360's coverage points out that banks and crypto exchanges are currently busy developing products that separate storage from trading, and the idea of off-exchange settlement — trading against assets held elsewhere — has been gaining ground across the industry for several years. What is new here is the packaging: a named network, a set of named custodians, and a public launch framed explicitly around the custody problem.

## Why This Launch Matters Now

Three forces make the timing significant, and each of them has been covered on this site in recent days.

The first is the Binance incident itself. When roughly $6.3 billion left Binance's hot wallets on 29 October, the immediate question for users was not about trading fees or token prices — it was whether their money was still there. Binance froze about $3 billion on-chain, promised to cover losses from its SAFU fund and company funds, and has since replenished about 95% of the affected assets and reopened withdrawals in phases. But the episode was a reminder of a structural fact: when your coins sit on an exchange, the exchange's security is your security. A model that keeps assets elsewhere would have changed the shape of that week entirely.

The second is the wider security picture. India's CERT-In issued an advisory on 4 November, CIAD-2026-0058, warning that an AI-assisted actor known as CRAFTY FALCON is actively targeting financial institutions including crypto exchanges, stealing wallet keys and siphoning funds. The advisory names no exchange and gives no loss total, but it describes a campaign that is active and escalating. In that environment, any design that reduces the amount of crypto sitting in exchange wallets is worth understanding — even if you never use it yourself.

The third is the institutional shift. The launch comes in the same week that the India Blockchain Week conference concluded in Mumbai, with tokenisation, institutional finance and custody among its headline themes. The message from that conference, and from Bitget's launch, is the same: the next wave of crypto growth is expected to come from institutions, and institutions will not deposit billions into exchange wallets on trust alone. They want their assets with a custodian they chose, under arrangements they control, while still getting access to exchange liquidity. OCN is built for exactly that customer.

## How Asset Mirroring Works, Step by Step

The technology behind OCN sounds complicated, but the logic is straightforward. Here is the sequence as described in the announcement and the launch coverage:

First, the user opens an account with a participating custodian — a bank or an institutional custody firm — and deposits their crypto there. The custodian holds the actual assets in its own wallets, under its own security and legal arrangements.

Second, the custodian connects to Bitget's trading engine through the Open Custody Network. The connection tells Bitget that a given balance exists and is available for trading.

Third, Bitget creates a mirrored balance for the user. This mirror is a record on Bitget's system that reflects the assets held at the custodian. The user trades against the mirror — placing spot, margin or futures orders as normal — while the real assets stay where they are.

Fourth, when trades settle, the custodian and the exchange reconcile. Depending on the settlement model, the custodian may move funds to cover the net result of trading, or the user's mirrored balance may simply be adjusted. The details vary by custodian, and the announcement does not publish a single settlement flow — which is itself a reason to read the fine print before assuming how any particular arrangement works.

The key point for a beginner is the separation: the exchange never holds your coins, and the custodian never executes your trades. Each side does one job, and the mirror is the bridge between them.

## What OCN Does Not Do

A launch announcement is a sales document, so it is worth being clear about the limits — because the limits are where beginners get hurt.

OCN does not eliminate custody risk; it moves it. Your assets are still held by someone else, and that someone else can be hacked, can fail, or can be caught in a legal dispute. The custodian's security, insurance and legal standing matter just as much as the exchange's did — they are simply a different set of risks to evaluate. The history of crypto is full of custodians as well as exchanges that failed, and "not on the exchange" is not the same as "safe."

It does not remove market risk either. Trading spot, margin or futures against a mirrored balance carries exactly the same price risk as trading against a deposited balance. A leveraged position can still be liquidated, and a falling market still loses money. Nothing about where the assets sit changes what the trade itself does.

It does not guarantee instant withdrawals or settlement. Settlement depends on the custodian and the model in use, and in a crisis — a hack, a market freeze, a legal order — settlement can slow down or stop. The mirrored balance is a claim on assets held elsewhere, and claims take time to settle when things go wrong.

And it does not, on its own, solve the regulatory question. For Indian users in particular, the relevant rules are India's: FEMA rules on cross-border transfers, FIU-IND registration requirements for exchanges serving Indian users, and the tax treatment of crypto gains. An offshore custody arrangement does not change any of those, and it may complicate them. The announcement says "eligible users" without defining eligibility for any particular country, so Indian users should treat availability as an open question, not an assumption.

## Custody Models Compared: Exchange, OCN, and Self-Custody

To see where OCN fits, it helps to compare the three main ways a crypto user can hold assets:

| Model | Who holds the assets | Trading access | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exchange custody (traditional) | The exchange's wallets | Direct, instant | Exchange hack, freeze or failure |
| Open Custody Network | Independent bank or custodian | Via mirrored balance | Custodian risk; settlement delays |
| Self-custody (hot or cold wallet) | You, via your own keys | Must deposit to trade | Lost keys, phishing, user error |

None of these is "the safe one." Each trades one risk for another. Exchange custody is convenient but concentrates risk in one company. OCN spreads the risk between two companies, which is better for large holders but adds a second party to trust and a settlement step in the middle. Self-custody removes counterparty risk entirely — nobody can freeze your wallet — but puts all the responsibility on you: lose your seed phrase and the funds are gone, with no customer support to call.

For most Indian beginners, self-custody with a hardware wallet remains the practical version of this idea. It is the retail equivalent of what OCN offers institutions: your coins stay with you, not with an exchange, and you only deposit what you actually want to trade. The amounts are different and the tools are different, but the principle — separate holding from trading — is exactly the same.

## What It Means for Indian Users

For the ordinary Indian crypto user, the direct impact of this launch is small, and it is worth saying so plainly. OCN is aimed at institutions. Its named customers are market makers and asset managers. The custodians in the network serve funds and trading desks, not retail accounts. An Indian beginner with a few thousand rupees of crypto on CoinDCX or WazirX will not be opening a Copper ClearLoop account this week.

But the indirect impact is real, and it comes in three forms.

First, it sets a direction. When the world's large exchanges start building products that separate custody from trading, that design eventually reaches retail users too — in simpler forms, on more platforms, at lower minimums. The Binance incident made the problem visible; OCN is one of the first structured answers. Indian users should expect to see more products like this, from more exchanges, over the next year.

Second, it is a useful lens for choosing where to keep your own crypto. The question OCN asks — who holds my coins, and what happens if that holder fails — is the same question every Indian user should ask about any exchange they use. If the answer is "the exchange holds everything," that is a reason to keep only trading balances on the platform and move the rest to a wallet you control. If the exchange offers a custodian option or a proof-of-reserves report, that is worth reading before you deposit.

Third, it is a reminder about scams. Every major security announcement produces a wave of fake offers — lookalike accounts, phishing messages and "recovery" schemes that borrow the language of the real news. No legitimate custody service will ever ask you to share a seed phrase, connect your wallet to an unfamiliar site, or send funds to an address to "activate" a service. If a message about OCN or any custody product asks for any of those things, it is a scam.

On tax, nothing about this launch changes the position for Indian users. Gains from crypto in India are taxed at 30%, with 1% TDS on transfers. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. Using an offshore exchange or an offshore custodian does not change the tax treatment of a gain, and it can add reporting complexity — another reason to keep records of every transaction, whichever platform you use.

## How to Read the Next Headline

If you follow this story further, four checkpoints will tell you more than any single headline.

Check whether other exchanges announce similar networks. If custody separation becomes a competitive feature, that tells you the industry is genuinely shifting. If OCN stays a one-exchange product, it may be a niche institutional offering rather than a new standard.

Check whether retail access appears. The announcement talks about eligible users and institutions. If Bitget or another exchange launches a version for ordinary users — with a named custodian, clear fees and a simple onboarding flow — that is the moment this idea becomes relevant to Indian beginners.

Check the custodian list. Six names were announced in October. If that list grows, and especially if it adds custodians with Indian operations or Indian regulatory familiarity, the network is gaining traction. If it stays the same, growth may be slower than the launch suggested.

And check the settlement record. The real test of any custody network is not the launch press release but the first crisis: a market crash, a hack, a custodian failure. How fast do mirrored balances settle, and do users get their assets back without a fight? That evidence will take time to appear, and it matters far more than the announcement.

## The Bottom Line

Bitget's Open Custody Network is a straightforward idea with a large implication: you should not have to hand your coins to an exchange in order to trade on it. By keeping assets with an independent custodian and trading against a mirrored balance, OCN separates the two jobs that exchanges have traditionally combined — holding and trading — and gives large holders a way to access exchange liquidity without putting their assets in the exchange's wallets.

For institutions, that is a genuine improvement, and the six named custodians show the idea has real backing. For Indian beginners, the launch is less a product to use today than a sign of where the industry is heading: after the Binance hack, the CERT-In warning and a year of exchange security failures, custody is becoming a feature exchanges compete on, not an afterthought.

The practical lesson is the same one this site has repeated through every security story of the past two weeks. Know who holds your coins. Keep only what you are actively trading on an exchange. Move the rest to a wallet you control. And remember that no custody arrangement — on an exchange, with a custodian, or in your own wallet — removes the need to stay alert, because the people trying to steal crypto are improving their methods just as fast as the industry is improving its defences.
