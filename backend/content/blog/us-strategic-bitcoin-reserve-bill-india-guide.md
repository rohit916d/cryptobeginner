## US Strategic Bitcoin Reserve Bill Reaches House Vote Queue

A bill that would write the United States Strategic Bitcoin Reserve into federal law — and forbid the government from selling any of its Bitcoin for at least 20 years — has taken its biggest step yet. On 24 November 2026, the House Financial Services Committee filed its formal report on H.R. 8957, the American Reserve Modernization Act of 2026, and the bill was placed on the House Union Calendar as Calendar No. 789. That placement makes it eligible for a vote by the full House of Representatives. No floor vote has been scheduled, and the clock is tight: the current Congress ends on 3 January 2027, leaving roughly five weeks for the bill to clear the House, the Senate and the President's desk.

The development was reported on 26 November by Unchained, CoinEdition, TFTC, BlockBeats, KuCoin and The Block, all working from the same legislative record, and the bill's text is published on govinfo.gov. This guide explains what the bill does, how a bill reaches the House floor, why the 20-year lockup is the headline, what could still stop it, and what — if anything — it means for Indian investors.

## What Happened on 24 November

The bill's journey through Congress has been slow and procedural, which is normal for legislation — and is exactly why this week's step matters.

H.R. 8957 was introduced on 21 May 2026 by Representative Nick Begich, a Republican from Alaska, with Representative Jared Golden, a Democrat from Maine, as co-lead. On 16 September 2026, the House Financial Services Committee approved the bill by a vote of 28 to 21, largely along party lines. That committee vote was the first real test, and it passed.

The next step — the one that happened on 24 November — was the committee filing its report on the bill, published as House Report 119-894, and the bill being placed on the Union Calendar as Calendar No. 789. In plain language: the committee has finished its work, written up its version of the bill with amendments, and handed it to the House as a whole. A bill on the Union Calendar can be called up for a vote by House leadership at any time. Nothing in the record as of 26 November says leadership has chosen to do so, and no floor date has been announced.

That distinction is important. Being eligible for a vote is not the same as having a vote, and it is certainly not the same as becoming law. The bill still needs three more things: a vote in the full House, a vote in the Senate, and the President's signature — all before the 119th Congress adjourns on 3 January 2027. After that date, a new Congress takes over, and any bill that has not passed dies and would have to be introduced again from scratch.

## What the Bill Actually Does

The American Reserve Modernization Act would turn something that currently exists only by executive order into a statute — a law passed by Congress. Here are its main provisions, as described in the reported bill text:

**A statutory Strategic Bitcoin Reserve.** Within 180 days of the law taking effect, the Secretary of the Treasury would have to establish a Strategic Bitcoin Reserve inside the Treasury Department. The reserve would hold Bitcoin already owned by the federal government, acquired mainly through seizure and forfeiture in criminal and civil cases. The bill does not direct the government to buy new Bitcoin with taxpayer money; instead, it tasks the Treasury and Commerce departments with preparing a study within 180 days on acquiring additional Bitcoin.

**A separate Digital Asset Stockpile.** Alongside the reserve, the bill would create a separate stockpile for other digital assets the government holds. The distinction matters: the reserve is for Bitcoin specifically, while the stockpile covers everything else the government has seized.

**A 20-year lock on selling.** This is the provision that has drawn the most attention. The bill would prohibit the federal government from selling, swapping, auctioning or pledging the Bitcoin held in the reserve for at least 20 years from the date the law takes effect. It could not be used as collateral for loans either. Within the two years before that 20-year period expires, the Treasury Secretary must send Congress a recommendation on whether to continue holding the Bitcoin or release it gradually — and any sales after the lock expires would be capped at 10% of the reserve's assets in any two-year period.

**An audited annual proof of reserve.** The bill would require the Treasury to publish an audited annual proof of reserve — a public accounting of exactly how much Bitcoin the government holds. For a government that has historically been vague about the size and handling of its seized crypto, that transparency requirement is a meaningful change in itself.

**A legal foundation for an existing policy.** The bill builds directly on the executive order signed on 6 March 2025, which established a Strategic Bitcoin Reserve and prohibited the sale of Bitcoin deposited in it. An executive order can be reversed by a future president with a stroke of a pen; a statute can only be changed by another act of Congress. That is the real difference the bill would make: it would make the reserve harder to undo.

## How a Bill Reaches the House Floor

For readers who have never followed American lawmaking, the calendar placement is worth explaining, because it is the step that separates bills that might pass from bills that are going nowhere.

In the US House of Representatives, a bill normally starts in a committee — in this case, the Financial Services Committee. The committee holds hearings, debates amendments and votes. If it approves the bill, it writes a report explaining what the bill does and why, and the bill is placed on one of the House's calendars. The Union Calendar is the list of bills that involve spending or revenue and are waiting for a floor vote.

Placement on the calendar is a real milestone: it means the bill has survived committee, which is where most bills die quietly. But it is not a guarantee of a vote. House leadership decides which calendar bills actually get floor time, and with limited days left in a session, many bills that reach the calendar simply never get called. The bill can sit there until the session ends, at which point it expires.

If the House does vote and passes the bill, it moves to the Senate, where it faces the same process — committee, floor vote — and the Senate must pass the exact same text. If the Senate changes anything, the bill goes back to the House. Only after both chambers agree on identical text does it go to the President for signature. Any one of those steps can stall or fail, and in a Congress with five weeks left, time is itself an obstacle.

## Why the 20-Year Lock Is the Headline

Of everything in the bill, the 20-year holding requirement is the part that has dominated coverage — and it is worth understanding why.

Governments that seize Bitcoin in criminal cases have historically sold it. The United States has auctioned seized Bitcoin for years, and each sale was a decision made case by case, by whichever administration happened to be in office. A 20-year statutory lock would remove that discretion entirely for a generation. The government would hold its Bitcoin through market cycles, elections and changes of administration, with no legal way to sell it.

Supporters of the idea argue that this treats Bitcoin the way governments treat other reserve assets, such as gold: something held for the long term as a store of value, not traded for short-term revenue. Critics, including some lawmakers who voted against the bill in committee, argue that tying the government's hands for two decades is inflexible, and that a future government might reasonably want to sell. Both views are political judgements, not settled facts, and the bill has not yet faced a full House vote where they would be tested.

The lock also matters because of what it signals. A government that writes a 20-year hold into law is making a statement about how it views the asset — that it considers Bitcoin worth holding rather than disposing of. That signal is part of why the story has travelled so widely in crypto coverage, even though the bill has not yet passed anything.

## The Clock: Five Weeks to 3 January

The biggest obstacle facing H.R. 8957 is not opposition. It is the calendar.

The 119th Congress adjourns on 3 January 2027, when a new Congress takes over — and, following the midterm elections of 3 November 2026, Democrats take control of both chambers. That leaves a little over five weeks, much of it over the holiday period, for the House to schedule and pass the bill, the Senate to act, and the President to sign it.

No floor vote has been scheduled as of 26 November. House leadership has not said whether the bill will be called up, and the Senate has not taken any action on it. The bill's sponsors have not publicly said they have the votes lined up in either chamber. In the final weeks of a session, leadership typically prioritises must-pass legislation — funding bills and other deadlines — over discretionary measures, which makes the path narrower still.

It is also worth noting that the committee vote in September was 28 to 21, largely along party lines. A bill that passes committee on a party-line vote can still pass the full House, but it needs a majority of the whole chamber, and it needs 60 votes in the Senate to overcome a filibuster unless other procedures are used. None of that has been tested yet.

If the bill does not pass by 3 January, it dies. Its sponsors could reintroduce it in the new Congress, but they would start again from the beginning — committee, report, calendar — in a chamber controlled by the other party. That is why the next few weeks matter more than the last six months did.

## What This Means for Indian Investors

The honest answer is: directly, very little — and that is worth saying clearly, because a bill about the American government's Bitcoin holdings can easily be misunderstood as something that changes the rules for everyone.

**Indian law is unchanged.** This is American legislation about how the United States government manages its own Bitcoin. It does not change Indian tax rules, Indian exchange regulations, or anything about how Indians buy, hold or sell crypto. Crypto income in India is still taxed at a flat 30% (plus applicable surcharge and 4% cess) under Section 115BBH, with 1% TDS under Section 194S on transfers above the specified thresholds, and losses cannot be set off against other income. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

**The market context is indirect.** The reason the story matters to Indian readers is the same reason it matters to crypto holders everywhere: the United States government is one of the largest holders of Bitcoin in the world, through seizures. When that government sells seized Bitcoin, the coins enter the market; when it holds them, they do not. A law that locks those coins away for 20 years would remove a recurring source of selling pressure — but that is a statement about supply mechanics, not a prediction about prices. Prices move on many factors at once, and this bill has not even passed yet. Nothing in this guide should be read as a suggestion about where any asset's price is headed.

**The precedent is the real story.** The more durable angle for Indian readers is the precedent. If the world's largest economy writes a Bitcoin reserve into statute, other governments — including India's — will be asked about their own approach. India has not created a Bitcoin reserve and has given no indication that it plans to; the government's stated position remains cautious, with heavy taxation and no legal-tender status for crypto. But the existence of a statutory American reserve changes the global conversation, and Indian policymakers, regulators and investors will all be watching whether the bill passes.

**Watch the vote, not the headlines.** The useful thing for Indian readers to track is simple: whether the House actually schedules a floor vote, and whether the Senate takes the bill up. Calendar placement is a milestone, not an outcome. If a vote is scheduled, that will be a genuine new development. If the session ends on 3 January with no vote, the bill dies quietly — and that, too, will be worth noting.

## What Could Still Stop the Bill

It is easy to read a headline like "bill reaches House calendar" and assume passage is close. The honest picture is that several things can still stop it:

- **No floor vote is scheduled.** Leadership may simply never call the bill up. With five weeks left and competing priorities, inaction is the most likely way for the bill to die.
- **The House may vote it down.** A 28–21 committee vote does not guarantee a majority of the full House, and floor amendments could change the bill or sink it.
- **The Senate may not act.** Even if the House passes it, the Senate would need to find floor time and votes in the same short window, and the Senate has not begun any work on the bill.
- **The text may change.** If the Senate amends the bill, it must go back to the House, adding another round of votes to an already tight schedule.
- **The session may simply run out.** On 3 January, the bill expires. Reintroduction in the new Congress would start from zero, under a different majority.

None of these outcomes would be surprising. Most bills that reach a calendar never become law, and most bills introduced in a session die with it. The calendar placement is genuinely newsworthy — it is the furthest this idea has ever travelled in Congress — but it is the beginning of the hardest part, not the end of it.

## The Bottom Line

On 24 November 2026, the bill to write the US Strategic Bitcoin Reserve into law reached the House floor queue: the House Financial Services Committee filed its report on H.R. 8957 and the bill was placed on the Union Calendar as Calendar No. 789, eligible for a vote by the full House. If it becomes law, the Treasury would hold the government's Bitcoin for at least 20 years, publish an audited annual proof of reserve, and keep a separate stockpile of other seized digital assets — turning a 2025 executive order into a statute that a future president cannot simply reverse.

But the bill has not passed anything yet. No floor vote is scheduled, the Senate has not acted, and the current Congress ends on 3 January 2027 — roughly five weeks away. For Indian readers, nothing about Indian rules changes either way, and the story's real significance is the precedent: the world's largest economy is one step closer to holding Bitcoin by law, not by executive preference. The next thing to watch is simple — whether the House actually schedules the vote. Until then, the reserve bill is a milestone on the calendar, not a law.
