The FBI has now said it out loud, and that changes the Binance story. On 31 October 2026, the US Federal Bureau of Investigation formally blamed North Korea for the theft of more than $6 billion in virtual assets from Binance, identifying the activity as TraderTraitor. Until that statement, the North Korea link rested on private firms: TRM Labs had attributed the theft to TraderTraitor with high confidence, Chainalysis had reached a similar conclusion, and independent researchers had traced matching patterns. A government attribution is a different kind of statement. It is slower, it is expected to meet a higher evidentiary bar, and it carries diplomatic consequences that a firm's blog post does not.

For Indian readers, this FBI North Korea Binance hack attribution matters for two reasons. First, it closes the biggest open question of the week: who did it, according to the authorities investigating it. Second, it tells us what happens next to the stolen money — the FBI says the attackers are already converting it, mainly into Ethereum, and are expected to keep laundering it through decentralised finance platforms and instant-swap services. That is why the FBI did not just name a culprit; it published wallet addresses and asked exchanges, node operators and analytics firms to block transactions linked to them.

## What the FBI Said, in Plain Language

The FBI's cyber alert, dated 31 October 2026, is short and specific. It says the Democratic People's Republic of Korea — North Korea's official name — was responsible for the theft of more than $6 billion in virtual assets from Binance on or about 29 October 2026. It refers to this specific activity as TraderTraitor, the name US authorities use for a North Korean campaign aimed at crypto companies and their employees.

The alert was issued jointly in effect with two partners: the US Secret Service (USSS) and the criminal investigation division of the Internal Revenue Service (IRS-CI). That partnership matters because it shows the case is being handled as both a cybercrime investigation and a financial-crime investigation, not as a single agency's press release.

Three observations in the alert describe what investigators say is happening to the money right now. First, TraderTraitor actors have been observed converting stolen assets primarily into Ethereum (ETH). Second, those assets are expected to be further laundered by converting them using DeFi platforms and instant-swap services, as the attackers prepare them for further laundering stages. Third, the agencies are urging private-sector entities — including THORNode operators, RPC node operators, instant exchanges, DeFi services, blockchain analytics firms and other virtual asset service providers — to block transactions with, or derived from, the addresses the attackers are using.

The alert also publishes a list of those addresses: Ethereum, Bitcoin and Solana wallets described as holding, or having previously held, assets from the theft, and operated by or closely connected to TraderTraitor actors. Publishing addresses is a practical step, not just a symbolic one. It gives every exchange and service in the chain something concrete to screen against, and it makes it harder for the stolen funds to move quietly through a compliant platform.

## Why a Government Attribution Is Different

Beginners often ask why it matters who says it, when TRM Labs had already said the same thing days earlier. The answer is about authority, evidence and consequences.

Private firms like TRM Labs and Chainalysis trace money on public blockchains. Their work is fast and often very good — TRM Labs estimated about $6.36 billion moved across ten blockchains, most of it within roughly 20 minutes — but it is still a private assessment, and firms do not have to meet the standard a government agency faces before making a public accusation against a state.

A government attribution, by contrast, is an official position. It can feed into sanctions, indictments, diplomatic pressure and coordinated action with other countries' agencies. It also tends to arrive later, because agencies check their findings against classified and law-enforcement sources that private firms cannot see, and because naming a state has consequences beyond the crypto industry. In the earlier Bybit case, for example, the FBI's attribution of the roughly $1.5 billion February 2025 theft to TraderTraitor came five days after the theft. The Binance attribution arrived two days after the 29 October theft, which is fast by that standard — a sign of how much of the groundwork, from the Bybit investigation and years of TraderTraitor tracking, already existed.

That does not mean every detail is now settled. Binance itself has still not named an attacker in its own statements; its investigation updates describe a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop, a service container, and later additional points of breach. The FBI's statement and Binance's technical account answer different questions — who is responsible, versus how the attacker got in — and both investigations are still open. Treat the attribution as the authorities' conclusion, not as a court verdict: no individual has been named or tried in connection with this theft, and North Korea has not been shown in this reporting to have responded to the FBI's statement.

## Who Are TraderTraitor and the Lazarus Group?

The names can be confusing, so here is the simple version. Lazarus Group is the broad name the security industry and governments use for a cluster of North Korean state-backed hacking activity. The US Treasury sanctioned Lazarus and its sub-groups Bluenoroff and Andariel in September 2019, describing them as controlled by North Korea's Reconnaissance General Bureau, its main intelligence agency.

TraderTraitor is a narrower name inside that cluster. It is the name the US government uses for a North Korean campaign aimed specifically at crypto companies and their employees, often through social engineering — for example, fake job offers or malicious coding tests that trick an employee into running malware. Security firms also track the same activity under the alias Jade Sleet.

The pattern is why investigators moved quickly on Binance. TraderTraitor has been linked by the FBI and by private firms to some of the largest crypto thefts of recent years, including the Bybit theft of February 2025 and a roughly $620 million theft in 2022. When the Binance funds started moving in ways that matched previously seen TraderTraitor behaviour — the speed, the chains used, the conversion patterns — investigators were not starting from scratch. They were matching a new incident against a pattern they had spent years documenting.

One caution applies to all of this. Labels like TraderTraitor describe tracked activity — wallets, tools, techniques and infrastructure linked together over many incidents. They do not mean investigators have identified a named individual sitting at a keyboard, and they do not mean every incident under the label was carried out by the same people. They mean the evidence, as investigators read it, points to the same campaign.

## What Has Not Changed: Your Funds and Withdrawals

It is easy to confuse an attribution with a resolution. The FBI statement does not recover any money, does not reopen withdrawals, and does not change what Binance owes its users. Those are separate tracks, and they are moving on their own timeline.

On the funds track, Binance said on 31 October that it had replenished about 90 percent of the roughly $6.3 billion loss, up from about 80 percent in its previous update, and that ten assets its users hold most — Bitcoin, Ether, USDT, BNB, Solana, USDC, XRP, USD1, RLUSD and Zcash — are now fully backed at 100 percent. The funding comes from its corporate treasury, a portion of its futures insurance fund, and a partial deployment of its Secure Asset Fund for Users (SAFU). Binance has repeatedly said it will absorb the full financial impact itself and that users will not bear the loss. A Times of India live blog on 1 November also reported co-founder Yi He as saying Binance will allocate 50 percent of SAFU funds to cover hack losses while keeping the remaining half in the fund; that specific split is reported, not yet confirmed in Binance's own progress notices quoted elsewhere, so treat it as a reported detail rather than a settled figure.

On the withdrawals track, Binance has said it remains on track to reopen withdrawals in phases from Sunday, 1 November, subject to security checks, one token at a time. At the time of writing, that phased restart is the next milestone to watch, not the attribution. A full, independently checkable proof-of-reserves verification — the cryptographic proof that lets individual users confirm their own balance is included in Binance's reported liabilities — is expected by 3 November, after a snapshot taken at 00:00 UTC on 31 October. Until that proof appears, the backing figures remain company statements, however detailed.

The distinction matters for Indian users in particular. India has no deposit insurance for crypto balances, and an exchange's promise to cover losses is a company commitment, not a government guarantee. The FBI attribution strengthens the law-enforcement side of the story; it does not convert Binance's promise into an insured deposit. What protects an Indian user in practice is simpler and less dramatic: withdrawing to self-custody when your window opens, keeping your own records, and not acting on unofficial messages.

## What Indian Users Should Do Now

First, do not rush. Phased restarts mean your token may not be in the first batch, and a delay for your asset does not mean your funds are lost. Check Binance's official status page and official announcements for your token's phase, and ignore screenshots, forwarded messages or social-media posts claiming a different schedule.

Second, expect scams to ride this news. Every major hack produces a second wave of fraud: fake "Binance support" accounts, phishing links promising early withdrawals, and messages claiming your funds need "verification" by sending crypto to a new address. Binance will never ask you to send funds to an address to unlock a withdrawal, and no genuine support agent needs your seed phrase or password. If a message creates urgency — "withdraw now or lose it" — that urgency is itself the warning sign. Our earlier guides on exchange incidents and scam red flags cover these patterns in detail.

Third, keep records in INR. If you later sell crypto after withdrawing, note the dates, the INR values, the platform involved and any fees, because Indian tax reporting needs those figures. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. This is a record-keeping suggestion, not tax advice, and the tax treatment of a sale can differ from the treatment of a loss, so check the current rules rather than guessing.

Fourth, understand what "blocked addresses" can and cannot do. When the FBI asks services to block transactions from listed addresses, it is asking private companies to refuse those transfers. That is not the same as freezing or recovering the money, and it is not a court order or a sanctions designation on its own — each service still needs its own legal basis to act, as analysts noted when the alert was published. Blocking slows laundering and raises the attackers' costs; it does not, by itself, return funds to Binance or to users. About $3 billion in affected assets was already reported frozen on-chain with industry help earlier in the week, and freezing, blocking and recovery are three different steps that are often confused in headlines.

Finally, keep the two India angles separate. The Binance incident is a global exchange security story. India's Enforcement Directorate searches of 27 October over alleged unauthorised cross-border crypto transfers — the Bengaluru and Madhya Pradesh cases involving Mudrex, Saber.money and Instadapp — are a different investigation, under different law, and were covered in a separate guide. Mixing the two into one story ("India is cracking down on Binance") would be wrong: the FBI attribution concerns a theft from Binance, while the ED cases concern alleged FEMA violations in transfers routed through other platforms. Both matter to Indian readers, but they are not the same case.

## What to Watch Next

Three dates and developments will tell us whether this story is stabilising or entering a longer phase.

The first is the phased withdrawal restart itself, due from 1 November. Watch whether it proceeds token by token as promised, and whether any phase is delayed for security reasons. A smooth restart is the strongest signal for users; a delay is not necessarily bad news, but it should come with a clear explanation.

The second is the proof-of-reserves verification expected by 3 November. The snapshot is already taken; the cryptographic proof is what lets users check their own inclusion. Read its scope carefully when it lands: which assets it covers, whether liabilities are included, and whether it is independently reproducible.

The third is the laundering trail. The FBI says conversion into Ethereum is already under way and that DeFi and instant-swap services are the expected next step. Future updates — further frozen funds, new addresses, or coordinated action by other countries' agencies — will show whether the blocking request is working. If the stolen funds keep moving despite the published addresses, that tells us something important about the limits of address-blocking against a determined state-backed actor.

For now, the headline is clear and worth stating plainly: the United States government has formally blamed North Korea's TraderTraitor for the largest crypto theft on record, two days after it happened, and has asked the crypto industry to help stop the money from disappearing further. That is a major escalation in the investigation, even though it does not, on its own, put a single rupee back in any user's account.

## The Bottom Line

The week began with an incident and a question. Binance lost roughly $6.3 billion, private investigators pointed at North Korea's TraderTraitor, and the one thing missing was a government saying so officially. That gap closed on 31 October, when the FBI blamed North Korea, published addresses, and asked the industry to block the laundering trail. It is the most authoritative statement yet in the largest crypto theft on record, and it sets up the next phase: not just an exchange rebuilding its reserves, but a state-backed hacking campaign being pursued across borders.

For Indian beginners, the practical lesson is unchanged but worth repeating. Attribution headlines do not move your funds; phased withdrawals, proof of reserves and your own security habits do. Follow the official channels, keep your records, ignore anyone promising early access, and let the investigation do its work while you protect your own account. That combination — patience plus verification — is what gets users through an incident like this with their funds and their judgement intact.
