# Crypto Gift Tax in India: Rules Beginners Must Know

Birthdays, weddings, festivals — crypto has quietly joined the list of things Indians send each other as gifts. A brother sends a small amount of Bitcoin to his sister. A friend gifts some Ethereum to a newly married couple. It feels as simple as a UPI transfer, and technically it is: a few taps and the coins move from one wallet to another.

But the tax rules behind that simple transfer catch many beginners by surprise. In India, a crypto gift is not just a friendly gesture — it is a transaction the tax system has opinions about. The good news is that the rules are not complicated once you know them. The bad news is that ignoring them can turn a thoughtful gift into a tax notice, a missed disclosure, or an argument with your chartered accountant at filing time.

This guide explains, in plain language, who pays tax on a crypto gift in India, when a gift is exempt, what happens with the 1% TDS, and the records you should keep — so your gift stays a gift and does not become a headache.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains tax rules in general terms; tax law is complex and changes over time, so confirm your specific situation with a qualified tax professional before acting.

## Is Gifting Crypto Legal in India?

Yes. No Indian law prohibits one individual from sending crypto to another. Crypto assets are recognised as Virtual Digital Assets (VDAs) under the Income Tax Act, and transferring them between wallets — whether on an exchange or through a self-custody wallet — is a normal, permitted activity.

What India does regulate is the tax side: how gifts are taxed, who reports them, and what records matter. There is no dedicated "crypto gift law" separate from the general income-tax rules. Instead, the existing gift-tax provisions of the Income Tax Act apply to VDAs just as they apply to cash, shares, or property. Understanding those general provisions is the whole game.

One important distinction up front: a gift is a transfer made **without expecting anything in return**. If you send crypto and the other person sends you money, goods, or a service back, that is not a gift — that is a sale or a barter, and the normal 30% tax on VDA transfers applies. Genuine gifts are the focus of this guide.

## The One Rule That Matters Most: The ₹50,000 Threshold

Under Section 56(2)(x) of the Income Tax Act, when a person receives a gift of property — and VDAs count as property for this purpose — the value of the gift is taxable in the **receiver's** hands if two conditions are both true:

1. The total value of gifts received from that person in a financial year exceeds **₹50,000**, and
2. The giver is **not a "relative"** as defined by tax law.

The tax is charged at the receiver's normal income-tax slab rate, as "income from other sources" — not at the flat 30% VDA rate. The 30% rate applies to gains from *selling* VDAs, which is a different event from *receiving* a gift.

Here is how that plays out in practice:

| Situation | Taxable for the receiver? |
|---|---|
| Gift worth ₹20,000 from a friend | No — below the ₹50,000 threshold |
| Gift worth ₹80,000 from a friend | Yes — the full ₹80,000 is taxable income (once the threshold is crossed, the entire amount counts, not just the part above ₹50,000) |
| Gift worth ₹5,00,000 from your father | No — gifts from relatives are exempt regardless of amount |
| Gift received on your wedding day, from anyone | No — gifts on the occasion of marriage are exempt |

That last point surprises people: wedding gifts are specifically exempt, whatever the amount and whoever the giver is. But note the limit — the exemption applies to gifts received on the occasion of *your* marriage, not to gifts you send at someone else's wedding from your side of the ledger. The receiver's situation is what counts.

### Who Counts as a "Relative"?

Tax law defines "relative" narrowly. It includes your spouse, your brothers and sisters, your spouse's brothers and sisters, your parents and grandparents (and your spouse's), and your children and grandchildren (and your spouse's). It does **not** include friends, cousins, in-laws beyond those listed, colleagues, or romantic partners you are not married to. If you are unsure whether someone qualifies, assume they do not and check with a tax professional — getting this wrong is the most common gift-tax mistake.

## Does the Giver Pay Tax on the Gift?

A genuine gift involves no consideration — the giver receives nothing back. Because there is no sale, the giver generally does not pay the 30% tax on VDA transfers for the gift itself. The tax event, if any, lands on the receiver under the rule above.

Two cautions, though. First, there is no explicit, detailed CBDT clarification specifically about VDA gifts yet, so tax professionals apply the general gift provisions by analogy. For large gifts, that is exactly the situation where professional advice pays for itself. Second, the giver's records still matter: when the receiver later sells the gifted crypto, the gain is calculated from a cost base, and the giver's original purchase records are the natural evidence of that cost. A giver who deletes their records does their loved one no favour.

Also remember: gifting does not erase tax history. If you bought crypto, held it, and then gifted it, you cannot use the gift to dodge tax on a gain you already made — because no gain was realised on the gift itself, there is simply nothing to dodge. But if you sell crypto and *then* gift the rupees, that sale is fully taxable in your hands first. The order of events matters.

## What About the 1% TDS?

The 1% Tax Deducted at Source under Section 194S applies when someone makes a **payment** for the transfer of a VDA. A gift has no payment and no consideration, so TDS generally does not apply to the gift itself. Nobody deducts 1% when you send crypto to your sister as a birthday present.

TDS enters the picture later — when the receiver **sells** the gifted crypto for consideration. At that point, the sale is a normal VDA transfer: the 30% tax applies to the gain, and 1% TDS is typically deducted on the sale value by the Indian exchange handling the transaction. That TDS can usually be set off against the final tax liability when filing, but it cannot be ignored — it appears in the receiver's Form 26AS and AIS, and the return should match it.

Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

## Moving Crypto Between Your Own Wallets Is Not a Gift

Beginners sometimes confuse two very different things. Sending crypto from your exchange account to your own hardware wallet, or from one of your wallets to another, is **not** a gift — you are the same person on both sides. There is no second party, no ₹50,000 threshold, and no gift-tax question.

That said, transfers between your own wallets are still worth recording. If you later sell from the destination wallet, you will need to show the chain of ownership and the original purchase cost. A simple note — date, coin, quantity, from-wallet, to-wallet, transaction hash — takes seconds and saves hours at filing time. The same logic applies to transfers between accounts on different exchanges in your own name.

## How to Gift Crypto Safely: A Practical Checklist

Tax is only half the story. The transfer itself has its own risks, and a mistaken transfer of crypto is usually irreversible. Before you send:

1. **Confirm the address, twice.** Ask the receiver to send you their wallet address by a trusted channel, and verify the first and last characters yourself. Crypto sent to the wrong address is generally gone for good.
2. **Match the network.** If the receiver gives you an address on one network, send on that same network. Sending tokens on the wrong network is one of the most common beginner losses.
3. **Send a small test amount first.** For any meaningful gift, send a tiny amount, confirm the receiver got it, and then send the rest. The extra few minutes are worth it.
4. **Use a reputable route.** Sending through an FIU-IND registered Indian exchange or a well-known wallet keeps a clear record trail, which helps both the receiver's tax reporting and any future dispute.
5. **Tell the receiver it is coming.** An unexpected crypto deposit can look like a scam or a mistake. A quick message — what coin, how much, and that it is a gift — prevents confusion and prevents the receiver from ignoring or mishandling it.
6. **Keep your own record.** Note the date, the coin and quantity, the INR value at the time of the gift, both wallet addresses, the transaction hash, and your relationship to the receiver. Share a copy with the receiver — they will need it when they file.

## Records to Keep: A Simple Template

Both giver and receiver should keep the same basic record. A single row in a spreadsheet is enough:

| Field | Example (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| Date of gift | 12 November 2026 |
| Coin and quantity | 0.002 BTC |
| INR value at time of gift | ₹10,000 (approximate market value that day) |
| Giver and relationship | Brother — relative |
| From wallet / to wallet | Exchange account → receiver's wallet |
| Transaction hash | (paste the actual hash) |
| Notes | Birthday gift; no consideration received |

Keep these records for at least eight years — that matches the window within which tax authorities can revisit older returns — and keep a backup outside your phone. If either side is ever asked to explain the transfer, this one row answers nearly every question.

## Watch Out: Fake "Giveaways" Are Not Gifts

A word of warning, because scammers exploit the language of gifting. Social media is full of fake "giveaways" promising to double any crypto you send — "send 0.01 BTC, receive 0.02 BTC back." These are not gifts. They are advance-fee scams: the crypto you send is stolen, and nothing comes back.

Real gifts flow one way, from someone you know, with nothing asked in return. Any "gift" that requires you to send crypto first, pay a fee to unlock it, or connect your wallet to an unfamiliar website is a scam. The same applies to celebrity impersonators and "exchange support" accounts offering festive bonuses. If a gift comes with conditions, it is not a gift.

## What to Tell Your Chartered Accountant

If you give or receive crypto, bring it up with your CA before filing season, not during it. A short summary is enough: who gave what to whom, the relationship, the date, the quantity, and the approximate INR value at the time. Your CA can then confirm whether the gift is exempt (relative, marriage, or below threshold), whether the receiver needs to report it as other income, and how the receiver's future sale should be reported in Schedule VDA.

If you received a taxable gift, it is reported as income from other sources in your return — and it must be reported even if no TDS was deducted, because gifts do not generate TDS. If you later sell gifted crypto, that sale goes in Schedule VDA with the 30% tax on the gain, and the 1% TDS on the sale is matched against your 26AS/AIS. Two different events, two different sections of the return — and both need the same underlying record.

## The Bottom Line

Crypto gifts in India are simple to make and simple to get wrong. The rules that matter fit on a small card: gifts from relatives and wedding gifts are exempt; gifts from anyone else above ₹50,000 in a year are taxable in the receiver's hands at slab rates; the giver generally pays nothing on a genuine gift; no TDS applies to the gift itself, but TDS and 30% tax apply when the receiver later sells; and moving crypto between your own wallets is not a gift at all.

None of that should stop you from giving. A thoughtful crypto gift — especially a first, small amount that introduces someone to the technology — is a genuinely nice present. Just pair the gesture with a minute of record-keeping and a message to the receiver explaining what you sent. The gift will be remembered for the thought, not for the tax form that followed it.
