## UK FCA Plans Toughest DeFi Rules: What It Means for India

Britain's financial regulator is planning the toughest crackdown on decentralised finance anywhere in the developed world — and it is citing the record-breaking Binance hack as the reason. The UK's Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) plans to require DeFi platforms to be authorised under its incoming crypto rules, even where a service is built to run without any central company or operator in charge. The approach would put Britain on a stricter path than both the United States and the European Union, and it would close off a route that many retail investors use to reach tokenised stocks and decentralised trading.

The plan was reported by the Financial Times on 26 November 2026 and corroborated the same day by Decrypt, CoinEdition, Traders Union, Finwire and Coinpedia, all working from the same FT reporting and FCA statements. This guide explains what the FCA is proposing, why the Binance hack is the trigger, how the UK's approach differs from the US and the EU, what the crypto industry is saying in response, and what — if anything — it means for Indian investors, including the large Indian community living in the UK.

## What the FCA Is Planning

DeFi — short for decentralised finance — is the part of crypto where lending, trading and other financial services run on software and smart contracts instead of through a bank, a broker or an exchange company. A decentralised exchange, for example, lets two people swap tokens directly, with the trade settled by code rather than by a company matching buyers and sellers. The defining idea of DeFi is that there is no central operator to regulate, license or hold responsible.

The FCA's plan challenges that idea directly. According to the Financial Times report, the regulator intends to bring a range of blockchain-based financial services inside its upcoming authorisation regime, including platforms designed to operate without a central controlling entity. In practice, that means the FCA wants to identify who is carrying out a regulated activity — running a trading venue, arranging deals, holding client assets — and require that person or firm to be authorised, whatever the platform calls itself.

Three parts of the plan stand out:

**Authorisation for DeFi platforms.** Any platform carrying out regulated activities would need full FCA authorisation. The regulator's draft guidance, published in October 2026, sets out how its existing rules apply to firms that provide services using or interacting with DeFi. The message is that decentralisation, on its own, is not an exemption from regulation.

**A wall between regulated exchanges and DeFi.** UK-regulated crypto trading platforms would not be allowed to execute retail trades on DeFi platforms. The divide is deliberate: a firm that holds an FCA licence would not be permitted to route its retail customers' orders onto an unauthorised decentralised venue. That would cut off one of the easiest ways ordinary investors currently reach DeFi — through a familiar, regulated app.

**No offshore route for tokenised stocks.** The FCA also plans to require all UK retail orders for equities — tokenised or not — to be executed on a regulated platform. That would block regulated intermediaries from routing British retail investors' tokenised-equity orders to unauthorised offshore DeFi platforms. Jamie Bell, the FCA's head of capital markets, explained the reasoning simply: representing a security on a blockchain should not change its regulatory treatment. A share is a share, whether it sits in a traditional brokerage account or as a token on a blockchain.

The FCA and the Bank of England have also set out a shared vision for tokenisation inside the regulated system — in other words, they are not opposed to putting traditional assets on blockchains. They want it done inside the perimeter they supervise, not outside it.

## Why the Binance Hack Is the Trigger

The FCA did not arrive at this position in the abstract. Its officials pointed directly at the record $6.3 billion hack of Binance in October 2026 — the largest crypto exchange theft ever recorded, and a story this site has followed from the first incident report through the FBI attribution, the freezing orders and the withdrawal restorations.

According to the FT report, the regulator said stolen assets from the Binance hack were routed through decentralised exchanges, which do not freeze compromised assets, before being converted into other tokens that were harder to trace. That sequence — theft, then rapid movement through DeFi venues, then conversion into harder-to-follow assets — is exactly what made recovery so difficult in the weeks after the hack, even as billions of dollars were frozen or intercepted elsewhere.

Jane Moore, the FCA's head of department for payments and digital assets, told the FT that the speed and scale of such transfers can complicate efforts to freeze stolen funds. The point is not that DeFi caused the Binance hack — the hack itself was a security failure at the exchange, involving a compromised laptop, a fake job interview and a tampered download. The point, from the regulator's perspective, is that DeFi venues became the escape route for the stolen money, and that the existing rules give victims and authorities very little leverage over that route.

FCA officials told the FT they were taking a deliberately strict line because they see elevated risks of fraud, scams and money laundering in DeFi, alongside limited avenues for consumers to recover stolen assets or obtain compensation. When a centralised exchange is hacked, there is a company to sue, a regulator to complain to and, sometimes, a safety fund to claim from. When funds move through a decentralised protocol, there is often no company at all — and that, in the FCA's view, is precisely the consumer-protection gap its rules need to close.

It is worth noting the timing. The FCA opened its guidance consultation on DeFi — known as GC26/7 — on 21 October 2026, eight days before the Binance attack. The consultation closed on 18 November. The Binance hack did not create the FCA's DeFi work, but it plainly shaped how the regulator is talking about it, and it has given the stricter approach a concrete, recent example to point to.

## How the UK Differs From the US and the EU

The most striking thing about the FCA's plan is how far it goes beyond what other major regulators are doing. The Financial Times framed it explicitly as a harder line than the United States or the European Union, and the contrast is real.

In the European Union, officials are still debating how to oversee DeFi as they update the bloc's crypto framework, known as MiCA. The European Securities and Markets Authority has proposed rules that would let a crypto exchange provide access to DeFi as a gatekeeper service — in other words, a regulated firm could act as the doorway into DeFi, rather than being walled off from it. That is close to the opposite of the FCA's approach, which would forbid regulated firms from executing retail trades on DeFi platforms at all.

In the United States, there is no comparable plan to force DeFi platforms into an authorisation regime. American regulators have pursued enforcement actions against individual DeFi projects and have debated where the line falls, but the direction of travel has been toward clarifying which activities count as regulated, not toward a blanket authorisation requirement for decentralised platforms. The US approach also leaves more room for the argument that writing code, on its own, is not a regulated financial service.

The FCA's draft guidance does draw a version of that line: writing code alone stays unregulated unless someone facilitates financial services. The question — and the controversy — is where facilitation begins. If a developer maintains a protocol, screens transactions for sanctions, or manages risk parameters, does that make them an operator of a regulated service? The FCA's answer, in its draft guidance, appears to be that it can. That is the part the industry is pushing back on hardest.

For readers in India, the comparison matters because it shows how differently the world's major financial centres are answering the same question. The EU is leaning toward a gatekeeper model, the US is leaning toward case-by-case enforcement, and the UK is leaning toward authorisation. There is no global consensus yet — and the country that gets the balance wrong risks either driving innovation away or leaving consumers exposed.

## The Industry Pushback

The FCA's plan has drawn sharp criticism from the crypto industry, particularly over how broadly the regulator intends to define the businesses caught by its rules.

Aave Labs, one of the largest DeFi lending protocols, has been the most vocal critic. Its chief legal and policy officer, Linda Jeng, described the FCA's position as "highly problematic". In its response to the consultation, submitted on 18 November, Aave warned that the draft guidance could treat basic protocol maintenance, risk management and sanctions screening as signs of centralisation — triggering full DeFi authorisation requirements even when the underlying software remains non-custodial. The argument is that the FCA's test pulls developers in opposite directions: do the responsible thing, like screening for sanctioned addresses, and you risk being classified as the operator of a regulated service; do nothing, and you risk being accused of facilitating illicit finance.

Coinbase representatives have also questioned the scope of the proposed rules, raising similar concerns about where the line falls between building software and running a financial service.

The FCA, for its part, has not backed down. Its officials have said the strict approach is deliberate, and the regulator's public statements have consistently framed consumer protection and financial-crime prevention as the priorities that outweigh the industry's concerns about scope. The consultation has now closed, and the FCA is expected to publish its final guidance in January 2027. That document — not the draft — will be the one firms have to live with.

It is also worth being precise about what the FCA has and has not done. The draft guidance clarifies how existing rules apply; it does not write new law. The broader cryptoasset regime itself does not take effect until 25 October 2027. What the FT report describes is the regulator's direction of travel and its interpretation of its own powers — significant, and newsworthy, but not yet a final rulebook. The final guidance in January 2027 will show how much of the strict approach survives the consultation process.

## What This Means for Indian Investors

The honest answer is that the FCA's plan changes nothing about Indian law — and that is worth saying clearly, because a headline about "tough DeFi rules" can easily be misunderstood as a global change. This is a British regulatory proposal about British-regulated firms and British retail investors. Indian rules are set in New Delhi, not London.

**Indian law is unchanged.** India has no DeFi-specific authorisation framework, and the FCA's plan does not create one. Indian exchanges must register with FIU-IND, India's financial intelligence unit, under anti-money-laundering rules. Crypto income in India is still taxed at a flat 30% (plus applicable surcharge and 4% cess) under Section 115BBH, with 1% TDS under Section 194S on transfers above the specified thresholds, and losses cannot be set off against other income. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

**NRIs in the UK are directly affected.** The most concrete India angle is the large Indian community living in Britain. An Indian national living in London who trades crypto through a UK-regulated exchange, or who holds tokenised stocks, would be squarely inside the FCA's rules. If the final guidance keeps the wall between regulated exchanges and DeFi platforms, that investor would lose a route they may currently use — and would need to understand that a regulated app can no longer route their orders onto a decentralised venue. For NRIs, the practical step is simple: watch the final guidance in January 2027, and check with your own platform what changes before the October 2027 regime start.

**Indian DeFi users should watch the precedent, not the rule.** For Indians using DeFi from India, the FCA's rules do not apply directly. But regulatory precedents travel. When a major regulator takes the strictest available line on DeFi — and justifies it with a hack that every regulator in the world has watched unfold — other regulators notice. India has so far taken a cautious, tax-heavy approach to crypto without a DeFi-specific framework. If the UK's authorisation model becomes the template other countries copy, Indian policymakers will face the same question the FCA has just answered: who, if anyone, is responsible when a decentralised protocol is used to move stolen money? There is no sign India is about to answer that question, but the UK's answer has now entered the global debate.

**The Binance link is the thread to follow.** The reason this story matters to readers of this site is the same reason the Binance hack has dominated crypto news for a month: the hack showed, in real time, how stolen crypto moves. Billions were frozen or intercepted, but the funds that slipped through did so largely via decentralised exchanges and cross-chain swaps — the same venues the FCA now wants to bring inside its perimeter. The FCA's plan is, in effect, a regulatory response to the laundering playbook the Binance hack exposed. Whether authorisation actually stops that playbook is an open question: a protocol with no operator cannot easily be authorised, and the funds can move to venues in other jurisdictions. But the attempt itself is a significant shift, and it is the first major regulatory response to the hack that goes beyond the exchange itself.

**Tokenised stocks are the sleeper issue.** The FCA's plan to require all UK retail equity orders to be executed on a regulated platform could matter more, in the long run, than the DeFi authorisation requirement. Tokenised stocks — shares of listed companies represented as tokens on a blockchain — have been one of the fastest-growing corners of crypto in 2026, with platforms offering round-the-clock trading of familiar names. The FCA's rule would keep British retail investors' tokenised-stock orders inside the regulated system. For Indian investors watching tokenised equities from afar, the lesson is that regulators are treating a tokenised share as a share, full stop — and that the "it's on a blockchain, so the old rules don't apply" argument is losing ground in every major jurisdiction at once.

## What Happens Next

Three dates matter from here. The first is January 2027, when the FCA is expected to publish its final DeFi guidance. That document will show whether the regulator has softened its position after the consultation, or held the strict line the Financial Times reported. The industry's submissions — Aave's warning about protocol maintenance counting as centralisation, Coinbase's questions about scope — will either be reflected in the final text or rejected.

The second is 28 February 2027, the deadline for existing crypto businesses to submit authorisation applications if they want transitional arrangements under the new regime. Firms that miss it, or that cannot meet the requirements, will have to stop serving UK customers or restructure.

The third is 25 October 2027, when the UK's broader cryptoasset regime takes effect and activities such as trading, custody and staking formally come inside the FCA's remit. That is the date the authorisation requirement would actually bite.

Between now and January, the useful thing for Indian readers to track is not the daily commentary but the final guidance itself — and, in parallel, whether the US or the EU responds to the UK's move by tightening their own positions. If Washington or Brussels moves in the same direction, the UK's strict line stops being an outlier and starts being the new normal. If they do not, Britain will be running an experiment the rest of the world watches closely: the strictest DeFi regime in the developed world, built in the shadow of the biggest crypto hack in history.

On 26 November 2026, the Financial Times reported that the UK's Financial Conduct Authority plans to require DeFi platforms to be authorised under its incoming crypto rules — a stricter approach than the United States or the European Union, driven by fraud, scam and money-laundering risks, and by the record $6.3 billion Binance hack, whose stolen funds were routed through decentralised exchanges. The final guidance in January 2027 will show whether that answer survives. Until then, the FCA's plan is a direction of travel, not yet a rulebook — but it is the clearest sign yet that DeFi's unregulated era is ending, at least in Britain.
