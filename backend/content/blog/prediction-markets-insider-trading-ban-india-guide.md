On 13 November 2026, the US House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform released a staff memorandum that could reshape one of crypto's fastest-growing corners: prediction markets. After a six-month investigation, the committee said government officials, military personnel and people with security clearances have been trading on prediction market platforms using non-public information for personal profit — and it recommended that Congress consider new restrictions, up to and including an outright ban on officials trading on these platforms. This guide explains what prediction markets are, what the investigation found, the cases that pushed Congress to act, and what it all means for readers in India.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. This guide explains a regulatory development and how prediction markets work; it does not recommend using, joining or trading on any platform.

## What Happened: The Facts at a Glance

The facts, as published on 13 November 2026 by the House Oversight Committee itself and reported the same day by Crypto Briefing, CryptoNews, Blockchain Academics and The Block, are:

- **The committee released a staff memorandum on 13 November.** The memorandum summarises an investigation into insider trading by government officials, military personnel and individuals holding security clearances who placed trades on prediction market platforms using non-public information.
- **The investigation ran for about six months.** It began on 22 May 2026 with letters to Kalshi and Polymarket, and expanded in September to Hyperliquid Labs, Crypto.com and PredictIt. In total, committee staff obtained nearly 1,700 pages of documents and communications and received five bipartisan staff briefings from the platforms on how they detect and report suspicious activity.
- **The trigger was suspicious betting on real-world events.** Roll Call reported in May that the investigation was spurred by suspicious bets placed around the capture of Venezuelan President Nicolas Maduro and the war in Iran — events where a small number of people knew the outcome before the public did.
- **The committee's central recommendation is new restrictions on officials.** Congress should consider rules ranging from an outright prohibition on federal personnel trading on prediction markets to narrower limits on contracts tied to government actions and political outcomes — applied to officials in all branches of government, not just Congress.
- **The platforms themselves did much of the detecting.** The memorandum credits the platforms: most reported insider-trading incidents were first detected and flagged to regulators and law enforcement by the markets themselves, not by investigators.
- **The memo is a recommendation, not a law.** Nothing is banned yet. Congress would need to pass legislation, and the committee's language is deliberately open — it asks Congress to "consider" restrictions, leaving the exact shape to lawmakers.

## What Is a Prediction Market?

A prediction market is a marketplace for opinions about the future, priced like a stock market. Instead of buying shares in a company, traders buy shares in an outcome: "Will the Federal Reserve raise rates in December?", "Who will win the next election?", "Will Bitcoin close above $70,000 this week?" Each share is priced between 0 and 100, and the price works as a probability. A "Yes" share trading at 62 means the market collectively estimates a 62% chance that the event will happen. When the event resolves, shares in the correct outcome pay out at 100 and shares in the wrong outcome pay nothing.

That simple mechanism is why prediction markets became so prominent in 2026. During the US midterm elections, prediction-market odds were quoted alongside polls in mainstream coverage — including in this site's own midterm results post, which cited prediction markets putting the chance of a Democratic Senate at around 99%. The markets are fast, they aggregate thousands of small judgments into a single number, and they have often been strikingly accurate.

Two platforms dominate the space. Kalshi is a federally regulated US exchange overseen by the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC), where Americans can legally trade event contracts. Polymarket runs two versions: Polymarket US, a federally regulated platform for people inside the United States, and its flagship international platform, which is crypto-based — users trade with stablecoins — and is banned in the United States and a number of other countries. Hyperliquid, Crypto.com and PredictIt also operate prediction-market or event-contract products, which is why the committee's investigation expanded to include them.

The same mechanism that makes these markets useful also makes them vulnerable. If you know the outcome of an event before anyone else — because you are the soldier on the operation, the official drafting the decision, or the engineer who saw the announcement early — you can buy the winning shares while they are still cheap and collect when the event resolves. That is insider trading, and it is the problem the House committee spent six months investigating.

## The Cases That Pushed Congress to Act

The memorandum points to several enforcement cases that show the problem is real and not theoretical. All of them are attributed to the committee's memorandum and to the reporting that covered it:

- **A US soldier charged over Maduro-capture trades.** A US soldier was charged in April 2026 over alleged trades on Polymarket tied to the operation that captured Venezuelan President Nicolas Maduro. The allegation is that someone with advance knowledge of a military operation traded on its outcome before the public knew it had happened.
- **A $1.2 million alleged scheme by a Google engineer.** The memorandum cites an alleged insider-trading scheme worth about $1.2 million involving a Google engineer trading on Polymarket — someone whose job gave them access to information the market did not have.
- **CFTC penalties on Kalshi.** The CFTC imposed penalties against former Congressman George Santos and a White House teleprompter operator over trades placed on Kalshi. The cases show the problem reaches people close to the centre of government, not just distant contractors.
- **Kalshi fined three congressional candidates.** In April 2026, Kalshi fined three congressional candidates over their trading on the platform — people running for office, trading on outcomes connected to the politics they were part of.
- **The Senate acted first.** In April 2026, the US Senate passed a resolution banning its own members from trading on prediction markets. The House committee's memorandum now asks whether that kind of restriction should extend to federal personnel across all branches of government.

The committee's chairman, James Comer (R-Ky.), framed the issue as one of public trust. "Americans are rightly concerned about public corruption," he said in the committee's release. "Congress has a duty to ensure our laws adequately prevent insider trading, hold bad actors accountable, and prohibit government officials and employees from profiting from nonpublic information."

## What the Law Already Says — and What the Memo Wants Changed

One of the memorandum's more surprising findings is that insider trading on prediction markets is already illegal under existing US law. The committee says current federal insider-trading laws apply to prediction-market contracts just as they would to securities and derivatives. In 2010, Congress amended the Commodity Exchange Act to prohibit fraud and manipulation in derivatives markets, and the Stop Trading on Congressional Knowledge (STOCK) Act amended the same law to bar federal agency employees, Members of Congress, congressional employees and judicial officers from using non-public information drawn from their positions to trade commodity futures, options or swaps. A separate provision also bars a covered person from passing insider information to someone else who then places the trade.

So why call for new restrictions if the behaviour is already illegal? The committee's answer is that enforcement after the fact is not enough. Proving that a specific trade used non-public information is slow and difficult, and the harm — a public official profiting from secret knowledge — is done the moment the trade is placed. The memorandum therefore recommends that Congress consider preventive restrictions: an outright prohibition on federal personnel trading on prediction markets, or narrower limits on contracts tied to government actions and political outcomes. It also recommends restricting the types of event contracts that federal personnel, Members of Congress and congressional staff can join regardless of whether they are trading on insider information — in other words, a ban on the activity itself, not just on the abuse of it.

The committee was also notably favourable toward the industry's own surveillance. Four of the five platforms gave the committee substantial documentation or briefed it on internal controls and proprietary technology designed to detect insider trading through suspicious trade patterns. Polymarket recently hired a former law-enforcement expert to lead a team managing insider-trading prevention, and rolled out a surveillance system called PolyGraph, built in partnership with the blockchain analytics company Chainalysis. Kalshi has strengthened its own detection protocols. The one exception the reporting highlights is Hyperliquid, which told the committee it did not "possess the types of records and information" the committee requested about identity verification and know-your-customer policies — a gap the committee noted.

## The Polymarket Hack: Why the Platform Itself Is Part of the Story

Prediction markets entered the news for a different reason only two weeks before the memorandum. On 29 October 2026, Polymarket halted trading on its international platform for several hours after users reported their wallets being drained. The cause, according to Polymarket, was a compromised third-party provider — a performance-monitoring script supplied by a UK-based vendor, DebugBear, which had been tampered with so that it intercepted users' sessions and drained funds. Blockchain analytics firm Bubblemaps estimated about 700 users were affected and roughly $2.7 million was stolen. Polymarket said it would reimburse affected traders, and later reporting put total repayments at $3.9 million, including extra compensation for about 5,500 wallets affected by the trading pause.

The incident matters for this story in two ways. First, it shows that the risks on prediction markets are not only about insider trading — the platforms themselves can be attacked through their vendors, and users' funds can be drained while they are simply logged in. Second, it is part of why the committee's investigation matters: prediction markets are no longer a niche experiment. They handle real money, they move on real-world events, and when they fail — whether through insider trading or a supply-chain attack — real people lose real money. The October incident report published in November put total crypto losses for the month at $6.51 billion across 63 incidents, with supply-chain and third-party failures the largest category once the Binance theft is set aside — the same pattern as the Polymarket attack.

## What Indian Users Should Know

For readers in India, the House Oversight memorandum is a foreign regulatory story — but it carries four points worth understanding.

**First, prediction markets are not a legal option for Indian users.** India has no regulated prediction-market framework, and online betting and gambling are largely prohibited under state laws. Polymarket's international platform, the crypto-based one, is banned in the United States and several other countries. Sending money abroad to place bets on an offshore platform would breach India's foreign exchange rules — the same FEMA framework that the Enforcement Directorate invoked in its November searches of CoinDCX and Delta Exchange, covered in this site's FEMA explainer. The US debate is about whether American officials may trade on these platforms; for Indian users, the platforms are off-limits in the first place.

**Second, the insider-trading lesson applies everywhere.** The reason prediction markets are so sensitive to insider information is that their prices are supposed to reflect what the crowd knows. When one trader knows the outcome in advance, the market stops being a fair aggregation of opinion and becomes a way to extract money from everyone else. The same logic applies to any market — crypto included — where someone with advance knowledge of a listing, a hack, a regulatory decision or an earnings report trades before the news is public. That is why exchanges and regulators treat insider trading as a market-integrity issue, not a victimless shortcut.

**Third, the Polymarket hack is a warning about third-party scripts.** The October drain did not come from a flaw in Polymarket's own code. It came from a trusted vendor's script that ran inside users' browsers with the same access as the site itself. For Indian users, the practical lesson is the same one this site's security guides repeat: never connect a wallet to a site and approve transactions while you are unsure what the site is loading, keep approvals limited, and treat any platform that asks for broad wallet permissions with suspicion. The Across Protocol domain hijack covered on this site in November is the same pattern from a different angle — an attacker compromising something the user trusted, rather than the user directly.

**Fourth, the tax position is unchanged.** Nothing in the US memorandum changes Indian crypto tax rules. Gains on crypto in India are taxed at a flat 30% plus a 4% cess, with 1% TDS on transfers. ## The Bottom Line

On 13 November 2026, the US House Oversight Committee released a memorandum concluding a six-month investigation into insider trading on prediction markets. It found that government officials, soldiers and security-clearance holders have traded on non-public information on platforms including Polymarket and Kalshi, cited cases ranging from a soldier charged over Maduro-capture trades to a $1.2 million alleged scheme by a Google engineer, and recommended that Congress consider restrictions up to an outright ban on officials trading on these markets. The platforms themselves were credited for detecting most of the suspicious activity — Polymarket with its PolyGraph surveillance system built with Chainalysis, Kalshi with strengthened detection — while Hyperliquid was noted for not holding the identity records the committee asked for. Nothing is banned yet: the memorandum is a recommendation, and any ban would need legislation. For Indian users, the practical takeaway is narrower: prediction markets are not a legal option in India, offshore betting would breach foreign exchange rules, and the same insider-trading and third-party risks the committee identified are worth understanding before trusting any platform with your money. The next fact that will matter is whether Congress turns the recommendation into a bill — and that will be a new event, not a repeat of this one.
