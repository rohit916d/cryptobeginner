MSCI, the index company whose benchmarks decide what trillions of dollars of passive fund money can hold, has confirmed that Strategy — the world's biggest corporate holder of Bitcoin — will be removed from its indexes. The decision, announced in MSCI's November review filing on 11 November 2026, means Strategy's shares leave the MSCI USA Index at the close of 30 November, with the change effective on 1 December. For Indian beginners, the story is a useful lesson in how stock-market plumbing works: an index provider's rule change can force billions of dollars of share sales without a single Bitcoin changing hands, and without the company selling anything at all. This guide explains what MSCI decided, why it decided it, what the $2.8 billion forced-selling figure really means, and what — if anything — it changes for Indian investors.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a published index decision; it does not recommend buying, selling or holding any asset, and it does not predict where prices will go.

## What MSCI Decided

The facts, as reported on 11 November 2026 by Reuters, BeInCrypto, Coindoo and KuCoin, are:

- **Strategy will be removed from the MSCI USA Index at the close of 30 November 2026**, and from the MSCI World index, with the removal effective on 1 December. MSCI confirmed the cut in its November review filing on Wednesday, 11 November.
- **The reason is a new test MSCI adopted on 15 October 2026.** Companies without substantial operating assets now count as "non-operating" companies and lose eligibility for MSCI's global indexes. MSCI's position is that such companies have fund-type characteristics, which its benchmarks exclude.
- **Strategy still runs a software business**, but its balance sheet is dominated by **850,364 Bitcoin, bought for $64.16 billion**, according to its latest SEC filing. That is the imbalance MSCI's rule targets: a company whose value is mostly a pile of an investment asset, rather than an operating business.
- **Strategy is not alone.** Japan's Metaplanet, another Bitcoin treasury company, and Yellow Cake, a UK-based uranium-buying company, were also marked for deletion under the same screen. Six companies in total were removed under the new test, and another 19 sit on a watchlist, according to MSCI's final impact workbook. Ethereum treasury platform SharpLink was among the names on the watchlist.
- **Strategy's shares closed at $122.35 on Wednesday, up 1.06%**, and slipped in after-hours trading as the list landed, according to BeInCrypto. Reuters reported the shares fell in extended trading on Wednesday.

## Why MSCI Removed Strategy: The "Non-Operating Company" Test

To understand the decision, it helps to understand what an index is for. An index like the MSCI USA is a list of companies that represent the US stock market. Passive funds — index funds and ETFs — buy exactly the companies on the list, in the proportions the index sets. When a company leaves the list, those funds must sell it. They do not get to decide; the list decides for them.

MSCI's new rule, adopted on 15 October 2026, asks a simple question about each company: is this mainly an operating business, or mainly a pile of investment assets? A company that mostly holds Bitcoin, uranium or some other treasury asset looks, to MSCI, more like an investment fund than an operating company. And MSCI's benchmarks exclude funds. That is the whole logic of the decision — it is a classification question, not a judgement on Bitcoin, and not a finding that Strategy did anything wrong.

Strategy's story is what made the test matter. The company, formerly MicroStrategy, started as a software firm and then began buying Bitcoin in large quantities, raising money by selling shares and debt to buy more. The strategy worked as a pitch to Wall Street: buy the stock, get exposure to Bitcoin through an ordinary brokerage account. Over time, though, the Bitcoin pile grew so large that it dwarfed the software business. At 850,364 Bitcoin bought for $64.16 billion, the balance sheet is, in MSCI's view, a fund wearing a software company's clothes.

Strategy pushed back during MSCI's consultation. The company argued that the proposal "is misguided, flawed, and conflicts with established securities laws and accounting principles", while also saying the removal would not be material to its business. Its founder and chair, Michael Saylor, put it more bluntly: "MSCI should be a mirror of the market, not an arbiter of it." MSCI went ahead anyway. The rule had been proposed a year earlier — MSCI first floated excluding digital-asset treasury companies whose holdings were 50% or more of total assets, then broadened the review into what it called "non-operating companies" — and the November review was the first time the final test was applied.

## What "Forced Selling" Actually Means

The headline number attached to this story is $2.8 billion, and it needs careful reading. JPMorgan analysts estimated last year that if Strategy were removed from MSCI's indexes, funds tracking those indexes would have to sell about $2.8 billion worth of Strategy shares. Two caveats matter. First, the estimate was based on MSCI's earlier proposal, not the final rule, so the real figure could be different. Second, the selling applies only to Strategy's shares, not to its Bitcoin — the index rules do not require the company, or anyone else, to dispose of the cryptocurrency.

Here is how the mechanics work. An index fund that tracks the MSCI USA Index holds Strategy shares because the index tells it to. On 30 November, when Strategy leaves the index, the fund's instructions change: it must sell. Multiply that across every passive fund tracking MSCI indexes, and the selling adds up. JPMorgan's $2.8 billion is an estimate of that total. There is a second, larger number in the same analysis: JPMorgan estimated that a further $8.8 billion could leave Strategy's stock if other index providers follow MSCI's lead and apply similar rules. That has not happened — it is a conditional estimate, not a fact — but it is the number the market is watching next.

Strategy itself has tried to play down the impact. In its response to MSCI's consultation, the company said holdings by funds linked to MSCI's global investable market indexes amounted to roughly 13 million shares, about 3.1% of its basic share count at the time, and noted that this was less than a single day's trading volume. That is a fair point about scale: $2.8 billion sounds enormous, but in a stock that trades hundreds of millions of dollars a day, the selling is spread across funds and, in practice, often absorbed over days around the effective date. What the company cannot control is the signal. Being removed from a major index is a stamp that says "this is not an ordinary operating company", and that classification can affect which funds are allowed to hold the stock at all.

There is also a detail in Strategy's recent filings that adds context to the company's own Bitcoin-buying pace. According to Coindoo's reading of Strategy's 9 November Form 8-K, only 28.9% of the cash raised from share sales last week went to buying Bitcoin, while $188 million went to buybacks of its preferred stock, STRC. That does not change the MSCI story, but it is a reminder that Strategy's cash has more than one destination, and that the company's Bitcoin accumulation has slowed relative to its fundraising.

## Does This Touch Strategy's Bitcoin?

No — and this is the part most often misunderstood in the coverage. The 850,364 Bitcoin on Strategy's balance sheet are not part of the MSCI transaction. MSCI's decision contains no instruction about them, and the index rules do not require Strategy to sell any of its Bitcoin. The forced selling is selling of Strategy shares by index funds. The Bitcoin stays where it is unless Strategy itself chooses to sell it.

That distinction matters for two reasons. First, it limits the direct market impact: the selling pressure is on one company's stock, not on Bitcoin itself. Second, it clarifies what the decision is really about. MSCI is not saying Bitcoin is a bad asset, or that Strategy's Bitcoin is at risk. It is saying that a company whose balance sheet is mostly an investment asset belongs in a different category from the operating companies its indexes are designed to represent. Whether that classification is right or wrong is a genuine debate — Saylor's "mirror of the market" argument is that indexes should reflect what investors actually buy, not decide what counts as a real company — but the debate is about index methodology, not about Bitcoin's value.

One more fact worth stating plainly: Strategy's Nasdaq listing is untouched. The company remains a listed, tradeable stock. What changes is which benchmark funds are allowed to hold it, and how much passive money flows into it automatically. Active fund managers can still buy it if they choose, and the company can still raise money and buy Bitcoin. The door that closed is the automatic one — the passive flows that arrive simply because a stock is in an index.

## Metaplanet, Yellow Cake and the Watchlist

Strategy was the headline, but the November review applied the same test to other companies, and the pattern is instructive. Metaplanet, the Tokyo-listed company that also buys and holds Bitcoin, was removed alongside Strategy. Yellow Cake, a UK company that buys and holds physical uranium, was removed too. What the three removed names share is a treasury asset — Bitcoin or uranium — that outweighs the operating business. The rule is asset-neutral: it does not single out crypto. Any company whose balance sheet is dominated by a stored commodity or investment asset can fail the test.

The watchlist is where the story may continue. Nineteen companies sit on it, including SharpLink, an Ethereum treasury platform. Being on the watchlist does not mean removal; it means MSCI is still assessing whether those companies meet the new test. Under the methodology, existing constituents are judged over two review periods, so a watchlisted company has time — and, in principle, a chance to change its profile — before any decision. The next review, and whether other index providers such as FTSE Russell or S&P Dow Jones adopt similar tests, are the two things to watch. If they do, JPMorgan's $8.8 billion figure becomes the relevant number; if they do not, the impact stays confined to MSCI-tracking funds.

## What It Means for Indian Investors

For most Indian crypto users, the honest answer is: very little, directly — but the story is worth understanding, because it clears up a confusion that catches beginners out.

**If you hold actual Bitcoin on an Indian exchange, this decision does not touch your coins.** MSCI's indexes have nothing to do with your wallet, your exchange account, or the price of Bitcoin on Indian exchanges. The removal is about one company's shares, and your Bitcoin is a different asset entirely.

**If you bought MSTR shares through a global investing platform, you own foreign shares, not Bitcoin.** Under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme, Indians can invest in US-listed stocks through global brokers. If you hold MSTR that way, the index removal may affect the share price through passive-fund selling around 30 November, but it does not change your Bitcoin exposure — because you never owned Bitcoin in the first place. You owned shares in a company that owns Bitcoin, and those are different things, with different risks. The company's Bitcoin can rise while its shares fall, or the reverse, because the share price also reflects the company's debt, its fundraising, its preferred stock, and now its index status.

**The tax treatment is different too, and this is where beginners get confused.** In India, gains on crypto assets are taxed at a flat 30% plus a 4% cess — 31.2% in total — with no set-off of losses against other income, and sales attract 1% TDS. That is the rule for crypto. Foreign shares like MSTR are taxed under the capital-gains rules for overseas equity, which are different. Buying MSTR shares does not give you crypto tax treatment, and selling them does not trigger the 1% TDS that applies to crypto sales on Indian exchanges. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

**The wider lesson is about index investing itself.** Many Indian investors hold index funds — including global index funds — precisely because they are automatic and low-cost. This story shows the other side of that automation: when the index changes, every fund tracking it must trade, whether or not any fund manager thinks the trade is a good idea. That is a feature, not a bug — it is what keeps index funds cheap and predictable — but it means index decisions can move individual stocks in ways that have nothing to do with the company's business. For a beginner, the practical takeaway is simple: know what you own, know why you own it, and do not mistake a stock that holds Bitcoin for Bitcoin itself.

## What to Watch Next

Three dates and one question will decide how much this story matters beyond 12 November.

- **30 November 2026:** the close at which Strategy leaves the MSCI USA Index. Passive funds tracking the index must trade around this date, and it is the real test of how much selling pressure the removal creates.
- **1 December 2026:** the removal takes effect. By then, the mechanical part of the story is over.
- **The next MSCI review:** the 19 watchlisted companies, including SharpLink, will be judged over two review periods. Watch for whether any of them are removed, and whether any change their profile to stay in.
- **Other index providers:** the open question is whether FTSE Russell, S&P Dow Jones or others adopt a similar "non-operating company" test. If they do, the $8.8 billion estimate becomes relevant. If they do not, MSCI's decision stays a one-provider event.

There is also a longer question underneath all of this: whether the "Bitcoin treasury company" model — raising money in the stock market to buy Bitcoin — still works as well once the automatic index flows are gone. Strategy built its pitch on stock-market access to Bitcoin. MSCI's rule says that model no longer counts as an operating business. The company can still operate, still raise money and still buy Bitcoin, but it will have to do so without the passive flows that came with index membership. Whether that matters to its share price over time is something the market will decide, and no one can predict it.

## The Bottom Line

On 11 November 2026, MSCI confirmed in its November review filing that Strategy — the world's biggest corporate Bitcoin holder, with 850,364 Bitcoin bought for $64.16 billion — will leave the MSCI USA Index at the close of 30 November, under a new test that treats companies without substantial operating assets as "non-operating" and ineligible for its global indexes. JPMorgan estimated last year that the removal could force about $2.8 billion of Strategy shares out of passive funds, with a further $8.8 billion at stake if other index providers follow — but both figures are estimates, the first based on an earlier proposal, and neither involves selling a single Bitcoin. Metaplanet and Yellow Cake were removed under the same screen, and 19 companies sit on a watchlist. For Indian investors, the practical point is a clear one: MSTR shares and Bitcoin are different assets, taxed differently and moved by different forces, and an index decision about the shares does not touch the coins. The real test comes on 30 November, when the tracking funds must trade — and until then, the story is a lesson in how the plumbing of the stock market works, not a reason to buy or sell anything.
