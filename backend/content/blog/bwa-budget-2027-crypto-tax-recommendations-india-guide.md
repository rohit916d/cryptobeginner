# BWA Budget 2027 Crypto Tax Recommendations: India Guide

India's crypto industry has taken its tax complaint directly to the Finance Ministry. On 21 November 2026, the Bharat Web3 Association (BWA) — the industry body for India's Web3 sector — announced that it had submitted recommendations to the ministry during pre-budget consultations ahead of Union Budget 2027, asking for a fairer tax framework and clearer rules for the country's crypto platforms. The submission asks for five changes: a lower 1% TDS, permission to set off and carry forward losses, a review of the 30% tax on crypto gains, clearer tax obligations for offshore exchanges serving Indian users, and clearer FEMA guidance for Indian exchanges that want to access global liquidity. This guide explains each proposal in plain language, why the industry is asking for it now, and what it would — and would not — mean for an Indian crypto user.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a reported policy proposal; it does not recommend any token, investment or action, and it does not predict any tax change or price movement.

## What BWA Submitted, and When

The submission was announced by BWA itself in a post on X on 21 November 2026, and reported the same day by Inc42, Coinpedia, The Crypto Times, adbytes.media and CoinGabbar. According to BWA's statement, the association presented the virtual digital asset (VDA) industry's recommendations to the Ministry of Finance during the pre-budget consultations that precede the Union Budget. The statement's headline reads: "BWA Presents Union Budget 2027 Recommendations: Towards Fair Taxation, Stronger Compliance and Regulatory Certainty for India's VDA Sector."

BWA described the submission as focused on "a fair, balanced and effective taxation framework that encourages compliance, strengthens investor protection, enhances tax revenues and supports the growth of India's domestic Web3 ecosystem." That framing matters, because it is the industry's answer to a familiar objection: that easing crypto taxes would cost the government revenue. BWA's argument, as reported, is the opposite — that the current rules push users and volume to offshore platforms, where Indian tax collection and oversight reach less effectively, and that fairer rules would keep more activity on compliant domestic exchanges, where it can be taxed and monitored.

The timing is also significant. The submission landed in the same week that Bitcoin fell to a two-year low near $55,000 and then whipsawed back above $60,000, a move this site covered in its previous two posts. During that sell-off, roughly $2 billion of leveraged crypto positions were liquidated in a single day — real losses for real traders, with no tax relief available under the current rules. The industry's tax ask is not new, but the market backdrop has made the loss set-off question feel concrete rather than abstract.

## The Five Proposals, Explained

BWA's submission contains five main recommendations. Here is what each one means, in plain language.

### 1. Reduce the 1% TDS

TDS — tax deducted at source — is the 1% that is withheld on transfers of specified virtual digital assets under Section 194S of the Income Tax Act. Every time a qualifying crypto transfer happens, 1% of the transaction value is deducted and deposited with the government on the seller's behalf. The tax itself is not lost: it is credited against the seller's final tax bill. But the deduction happens on every transfer, including transfers between a user's own wallets in some cases, and it locks up working capital until it can be claimed back at filing time.

BWA wants the government to lower the 1% TDS rate on eligible VDA transfers. The association has not named a replacement rate, and the reporting does not specify one either, so this is a direction of travel rather than a specific number. The industry's argument is that a 1% charge on every transaction is heavy for a market where traders move in and out of positions frequently, and that it discourages activity on compliant Indian exchanges, where the TDS is actually collected, relative to offshore venues where it often is not. A lower rate would reduce the friction of trading onshore without removing the reporting trail that TDS creates.

### 2. Allow loss set-off and carry-forward

This is the proposal that affects the most ordinary users. Under India's current rules, gains from transferring virtual digital assets are taxed at a flat 30%, and losses cannot be set off against gains from other crypto assets or against other income, and they cannot be carried forward to a future year. Only the cost of acquiring the crypto can be deducted when calculating the gain. In practice, that means a trader who made a profit on one token and a loss on another pays tax on the full profit, with no relief for the loss — even though, taken together, they may have made little or no money at all.

BWA wants investors to receive more normal tax treatment: losses from VDA transactions should be allowed to be set off against gains, and where eligible, carried forward to future tax periods. The association has not set out the detailed rules it would support — for example, how many years a loss could be carried forward, or whether set-off would be limited to crypto gains only — so the practical shape of any change would depend on what the government actually writes into law. But the principle is clear: tax should apply to net gains, not to gross winning trades while losing trades are ignored.

The proposal has particular resonance after the November sell-off. When roughly $2 billion of leveraged positions were liquidated in a day, many traders lost money that, under current rules, brings no tax relief at all. A liquidation loss is already a loss with no tax relief attached; BWA's proposal would change that, at least in part, by letting losses reduce taxable gains.

### 3. Review the 30% tax rate

The flat 30% tax on income from transferring virtual digital assets — under Section 115BBH — is the most widely known part of India's crypto tax framework, and BWA wants the government to review it. The association has not proposed a replacement figure, and the reporting does not suggest one. The ask is for a review, not a specific new rate.

The industry's argument, as reported, is that a 30% rate with no deductions beyond the cost of acquisition and no loss relief makes compliant Indian platforms less attractive than offshore venues. The counter-argument, which the government has effectively made by leaving the rules unchanged in the Union Budget 2026, is that a high, simple rate is easy to administer and discourages speculative trading. BWA's submission asks the ministry to weigh those considerations again — but it is a request for reconsideration, not a negotiation over a number, and the government has given no indication of which way it leans.

### 4. Clearer rules for offshore exchanges serving Indian users

The fourth proposal is about the level playing field. BWA wants Indian tax provisions — particularly TDS obligations — to apply more clearly to offshore VDA platforms that serve Indian users, with stronger enforcement of registration and reporting obligations. Offshore entities serving Indian users should, in BWA's words, be subject to appropriate Indian legal and regulatory requirements.

The logic is straightforward. Indian exchanges that are registered with the Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU-IND) must collect TDS, follow know-your-customer rules, and report transactions. Offshore platforms that serve Indian users without the same obligations can offer a cheaper, less friction-filled experience — not because they are better businesses, but because they are not carrying the same compliance costs. BWA argues that this gap pushes users offshore, which weakens both tax collection and consumer protection. The proposal does not explain how offshore platforms would be brought into compliance, which is the hard part — enforcement across borders is difficult — but the direction is clear: the same rules should apply to the same users, wherever the platform is based.

### 5. FEMA clarity for global liquidity

The fifth proposal concerns the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), and it is the most technical of the five. BWA wants clear guidance on how FIU-registered Indian exchanges can access global liquidity through offshore exchanges. Right now, the rules in this area are uncertain: ongoing enquiries and differing readings of FEMA have left Indian platforms unsure what arrangements are permitted when they connect to offshore liquidity pools.

Why does this matter? Liquidity — the depth of buyers and sellers available at any moment — is what makes an exchange work well. A platform with thin liquidity has wider spreads between buying and selling prices, which costs users money on every trade. Global liquidity would let Indian exchanges offer tighter prices and a better experience, but only if the arrangement is clearly legal under FEMA. BWA is asking the government to issue guidance, developed in consultation with the relevant authorities, that settles the question. The proposal is aimed at FIU-registered Indian platforms, not at individual users moving funds offshore themselves.

## Why the Industry Is Asking Now

Three things make this submission different from a routine wish list. First, it comes from BWA itself, the industry body, rather than from a single exchange or company, which gives it more weight as a statement of the sector's collective position. Second, it follows BWA's own institution-building: on 28 October 2026, the association launched a voluntary crypto self-regulatory body in New Delhi, chaired by former Union government secretary Rohit Kumar Singh, with voluntary guidelines on market integrity, cybersecurity, consumer protection, listings, grievance redressal and anti-money-laundering measures. That move was covered in this site's earlier post, and it was designed to show the government that the industry is willing to regulate itself — the tax submission is the other half of the same argument: give us workable rules, and we will comply with them.

Third, the timing connects to a broader reporting shift. Information cited alongside BWA's proposals gives April 2027 as the scheduled start of cross-border reporting under the OECD's Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework (CARF), under which countries will exchange information about crypto transactions. As reporting requirements expand, the industry's argument is that the tax framework should be fair enough to keep users onshore, where that reporting actually happens, rather than driving them to venues outside India's reach.

The government, for its part, has left the core crypto tax provisions unchanged in the Union Budget 2026, as The Crypto Times noted in its coverage — the Budget offered no relief on the 30% VDA tax or the 1% TDS. The 2026–27 Budget also left the rules unchanged, according to information submitted alongside BWA's recommendations. So the industry is asking the ministry to reconsider a position it has now held through two budgets.

## What Would Change — and What Would Not

It is important to be clear about the status of all five proposals: they are requests, not decisions. BWA's statement reports no commitment from the ministry, and CoinGabbar's coverage makes the point explicitly — nothing changes until the Budget and the Finance Bill, and the next real signal comes when the finance minister presents Union Budget 2027. Any tax change would appear there first, and the 30% rate, the 1% TDS and the loss treatment are the three lines that matter most.

Until then, the current rules remain in force. Gains on transferring virtual digital assets in India are taxed at a flat 30%, with 1% TDS on transfers, and losses cannot be set off against other income or carried forward. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

If the proposals were accepted, the effects — as BWA frames them — would be: loss set-off would create a fairer tax base for active traders; clearer TDS rules for offshore platforms would narrow the competitive gap with domestic exchanges; and FEMA guidance would give Indian platforms a clear path to tap global liquidity. Reform could also bring stricter checks on offshore platforms, since clearer rules are easier to enforce. But these are the industry's own framing of the possible effects, not predictions. The government may accept some proposals, reject others, or accept none of them — and it may also accept the direction of a proposal while writing it into law in a very different form from the one BWA described.

There is also a point that matters for beginners reading the coverage: a tax proposal is not a tax change, and it is not a reason to change how you trade or file. Anyone making decisions based on the possibility of a lower TDS or loss set-off would be acting on a hope, not a rule. The safe approach is to keep records under the current rules — every transfer, every gain, every loss — so that if the rules do change, you can show what happened, and if they do not, your filing is already correct.

## How This Fits India's Crypto Story in 2026

The BWA submission sits at the intersection of two stories this site has followed all year. One is the enforcement story: the Enforcement Directorate's searches in October, the FIU's notices to offshore platforms, the RBI and FIU's scrutiny of offshore prepaid crypto cards, and the fraud cases that have made headlines in Andhra Pradesh and elsewhere. The other is the industry-building story: BWA's self-regulatory body, the Devcon 8 conference in Mumbai, and the steady growth of India's domestic exchange ecosystem.

The two stories point in the same direction. The government is tightening enforcement against platforms and actors that operate outside Indian rules, while the industry is asking for rules that are fair enough to make operating inside them attractive. BWA's submission is, in effect, the industry's side of that bargain: clearer, fairer tax rules in exchange for more activity on compliant, registered, reporting platforms. Whether the government accepts the bargain is a decision for the Budget — but the fact that the industry body is making the case in formal pre-budget consultations, rather than only in public statements, shows how seriously it is being taken.

For an Indian beginner, the practical takeaway is simple. The tax rules you actually live under have not changed, and they will not change until the Budget says so. The proposals are worth understanding because they affect the platforms you use and the costs you pay — but they are not a reason to act now. Keep your records, understand the current 30% and 1% TDS rules, and watch the Budget for the real decision.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What did BWA ask for in its Budget 2027 recommendations?

### Will India's crypto tax rules change in Budget 2027?

The next real signal will be the Budget itself.

## The Bottom Line

BWA's Budget 2027 submission is the Indian crypto industry's most formal tax ask yet: five proposals, presented to the Finance Ministry in pre-budget consultations, covering the 1% TDS, loss set-off, the 30% tax rate, offshore platform obligations and FEMA clarity for global liquidity. The proposals are backed by an industry body that has spent the year building its own self-regulatory framework, and they land at a moment when the November sell-off has made the loss set-off question painfully concrete for many traders. But they are proposals, not decisions — nothing changes until the Budget and the Finance Bill say so. For now, the rules are what they were: 30% on gains, 1% TDS on transfers, no loss set-off. Understand the proposals, keep your records, and wait for the Budget.
