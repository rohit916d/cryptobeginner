# APY vs APR in Crypto Staking: Reward Numbers Explained

Open almost any staking page and two numbers compete for your attention: APR and APY. Understanding APY vs APR is one of the smallest skills in crypto, and one of the most useful, because the same staking offer can look noticeably better or worse depending on which number a platform chooses to show you. This guide explains both in plain language, with a worked rupee example, so Indian beginners can read any reward rate calmly and correctly.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose.

## APR: The Simple Rate, Without Compounding

APR stands for Annual Percentage Rate. It is the simple yearly rate: the reward calculated only on the amount you originally staked, ignoring anything you earn along the way.

Think of it like simple interest on a school maths problem. If a hypothetical offer paid 10% APR for a full year, and you staked tokens worth ₹10,000 at the start, the maths gives a reward of ₹1,000 worth of tokens over that year — 10% of ₹10,000, nothing more. It does not matter in this calculation whether rewards arrive daily, weekly, or in one payment at the end. APR never counts rewards earning further rewards.

APR is the more honest starting point for comparison because it makes the fewest assumptions. It assumes only that the rate stays the same for a year — itself a generous assumption in crypto, where most staking rates are variable.

## APY: The Same Rate, With Compounding Added

APY stands for Annual Percentage Yield. It answers a slightly different question: what would the effective yearly rate be if every reward you received was immediately added back to your stake, so that the rewards themselves started earning rewards too? That process is called compounding.

Return to the same hypothetical 10% example. If rewards are calculated and reinvested monthly, you do not earn exactly 10% over the year. After the first month, your balance is slightly larger, so the second month's reward is calculated on a slightly larger amount, and so on. By the end of the year, the effective yield is about 10.47%. Compounded daily, it rises to about 10.52%.

| How rewards are added back | Effective yearly result on a 10% rate |
|---|---|
| Never (simple rate — this is APR) | 10.00% |
| Reinvested monthly | About 10.47% APY |
| Reinvested daily | About 10.52% APY |

Two lessons hide in this table. First, APY is always equal to or higher than APR for the same offer — that is maths, not generosity. Second, at modest rates the compounding gap is small: roughly half a percentage point here. It grows dramatic only when the rate itself is very large.

## APR vs APY at a Glance

| Question | APR | APY |
|---|---|---|
| What it measures | Simple yearly rate | Effective yearly rate with compounding |
| Counts rewards earning rewards? | No | Yes |
| Assumes you reinvest rewards? | No | Yes |
| Assumes the rate stays fixed for a year? | Yes | Yes |
| Which number looks bigger? | Smaller | Bigger |
| Better for comparing two offers? | Yes, as a common base | Only if both offers compound the same way |

## Why Platforms Prefer to Show APY

Most staking pages display APY in large type for a straightforward reason: it is the bigger number, and bigger numbers attract attention. APY quietly assumes three things that often do not happen in real life.

First, it assumes you actually reinvest. If you withdraw rewards, spend them, or let them sit unstaked, there is no compounding, and your real return drifts back toward the APR. Some platforms compound automatically; on others you must claim rewards and stake them again yourself, sometimes paying a network fee each time that eats the benefit on small amounts.

Second, it assumes a full, uninterrupted year. A rate earned for three weeks and then changed or ended does not deliver the advertised yearly figure.

Third, it assumes the rate stays the same. On most networks and platforms, staking rates move with how many people are staking, network rules, and platform policy. Today's figure is a snapshot stretched across an imaginary year, not a promise about the next twelve months.

## Five Things a Reward Percentage Does Not Tell You

A percentage, by itself, hides more than it reveals. Before any number impresses you, check these five things.

**1. The rate is paid in tokens, not rupees.** A 10% reward means 10% more tokens. If the token's price falls 30% against the rupee while you stake, your holding in rupee terms has shrunk, extra tokens included. A rising token count is not the same as a rising rupee value.

**2. The rate can change or end.** Promotional rates are often introductory, paid for a limited period or up to a limited amount, after which a much lower standard rate applies. Read which rate applies to your full amount, and for how long.

**3. Your crypto may be locked.** Many offers require a lock-in, and unstaking afterwards can take days. During that wait your tokens are exposed to price movement but cannot be moved or sold.

**4. Fees and conditions sit underneath.** Platforms may charge fees on rewards, set minimum amounts, or pay the best rate only on a small first portion of your balance. The banner rate is rarely the rate on every rupee.

**5. Staking carries its own risks.** On some networks, validators that misbehave can be penalised — slashing — and a share of staked funds can be lost. Platforms and smart contracts can also fail or be hacked. None of that appears in a percentage. Treat any unusually high, supposedly risk-free return as a warning sign: legitimate staking does not work that way.

## How to Compare Two Staking Offers Fairly

When two offers use different numbers, convert them to the same basis before comparing. First, note each offer's APR; if only APY is shown, look for how often it compounds and whether compounding is automatic. Second, check whether the rate is fixed or variable, and whether it is a short promotional rate. Third, compare lock-in and unstaking periods side by side. Fourth, read the fee and minimum conditions. Fifth, remember both percentages are in token terms, so the token's own price risk matters as much as the reward rate — often more. Judge an offer on these real terms, not its banner.

## Tax and Records for Indian Stakers

Staking rewards also create paperwork. Rewards are crypto assets, and selling, swapping, or spending them later can create a taxable event, so record every reward: the date, token and amount received, its rupee value that day, and the platform. Good records make annual filing far easier and help reconcile any tax deducted at source shown by an Indian exchange. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the difference between APY and APR in staking?** APR is the simple yearly rate, calculated only on your original staked amount. APY is the effective yearly rate after compounding, meaning it assumes your rewards are added back to your stake and themselves start earning rewards. For the same offer, APY is therefore always equal to or higher than APR.

**Why is APY always higher than APR for the same offer?** Because APY includes interest on interest. If rewards are paid and reinvested often — daily, for example — each new reward increases the balance that the next reward is calculated on. The more often compounding happens, the bigger the gap between the APR and the APY becomes.

**Does a high APY mean I will earn that much in rupees?** No. Staking percentages are calculated in the token, not in rupees, and most rates are variable rather than fixed. If the token's price falls while you are staking, the rupee value of your holding and your rewards can fall too, even if the number of tokens you hold has grown.

## The Bottom Line

APR tells you the simple rate; APY tells you the rate if compounding works perfectly for a full year at an unchanged rate. Neither is a guarantee, and neither says anything about the token's price, lock-ins, fees, or risk. Read every staking percentage as a starting question — "under what conditions?" — rather than an answer.
