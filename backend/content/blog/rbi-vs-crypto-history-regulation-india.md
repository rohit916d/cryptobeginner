In 2013, when most Indians had never heard of Bitcoin, the Reserve Bank of India issued a quiet warning about "virtual currencies." In 2018, it cut crypto off from the banking system entirely. In 2020, the Supreme Court pushed back. In 2022, the government taxed crypto at 30% instead of banning it. And today, in 2026, India still has no dedicated crypto law at all.

This decade-long tug of war between the RBI and crypto has shaped every rule an Indian investor follows today. If you want to understand *why* your exchange asks for KYC, *why* 1% disappears from every trade as TDS, and *why* the government says "crypto is legal but not legal tender," you need this history. Here it is, in plain language.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Regulatory details below are based on publicly known announcements and laws as of October 2026 — rules can change, so always verify current law before acting.

## 2013: The First Warning Nobody Noticed

On December 24, 2013, the RBI issued its first cautionary notice on virtual currencies, warning users about security, financial, legal, and consumer-protection risks. At the time, Bitcoin was a tiny experiment in India — a few enthusiasts, no major exchanges, barely any media coverage. The notice read like a formality, but it established the RBI's instinct from the start: **skepticism**.

## 2016–2017: Bitcoin Booms, Warnings Multiply

During Bitcoin's first big bull run, Indian exchanges like ZebPay and Unocoin grew rapidly. The RBI responded with repeated warnings through 2016 and 2017, reminding users that it had not licensed anyone to deal in cryptocurrencies and that users were doing so at their own risk. The message was clear: the central bank did not consider crypto its business, and it did not want ordinary citizens confused into thinking otherwise.

Meanwhile, in November 2017, the Finance Ministry set up a high-level inter-ministerial committee chaired by S.C. Garg to study digital currencies and propose regulations. A separate RBI internal working group was also examining the issue. For the first time, it looked like India might get formal rules.

## April 2018: The RBI Drops the Hammer

On April 6, 2018, the RBI issued the circular that would define the next two years. It prohibited all banks and regulated financial institutions from dealing in — or providing services to anyone dealing in — cryptocurrencies. The deadline: three months to wind down existing relationships.

The effect was immediate and brutal. Indian exchanges lost banking access. You could not deposit INR into an exchange account through UPI or bank transfer. Prices on Indian platforms detached from global markets. Several exchanges shut down or moved operations abroad.

But Indian entrepreneurs adapted. WazirX — then a young startup — pioneered a **peer-to-peer (P2P) system** that matched buyers and sellers directly, letting trades continue without banks touching crypto. The ban had not killed Indian crypto; it had only pushed it into creative workarounds.

## 2018–2019: The Ban Bill That Never Passed

While the industry fought for survival, the Garg Committee submitted its report (July 2018) with a draft "Banning of Cryptocurrency & Regulation of Official Digital Currency Bill." It proposed an outright ban on private cryptocurrencies, with penalties as severe as 10 years in prison. It frightened every Indian crypto investor — but the bill was never tabled in Parliament. It died quietly, a paper threat.

## March 2020: The Supreme Court Says No

On March 4, 2020, the Supreme Court of India delivered its landmark judgment in *Internet and Mobile Association of India v. Reserve Bank of India*. The court struck down the RBI's April 2018 circular, ruling that it was **disproportionate** — the RBI had cut off an entire industry without showing proportionate harm.

Crucially, though, the judgment granted crypto no positive legal status. It simply said the RBI's method was excessive. Crypto was legal to trade again — banks could serve exchanges — but it remained in a legal grey zone: neither legal tender nor prohibited. That grey zone, more than anything, defines India to this day.

## 2021: The Bill That Never Came

In late 2021, the government listed "The Cryptocurrency and Regulation of Official Digital Currency Bill" for the Winter Session of the Lok Sabha. Headlines screamed that India would ban private cryptocurrencies. Investors panicked; prices on Indian exchanges briefly crashed as people rushed to sell.

Then the bill was deferred. And deferred again. Eventually it was shelved entirely. It remains one of the most misunderstood episodes in Indian crypto history: **a bill that was listed but never introduced changed the market without changing a single law.**

## 2022: Taxation Arrives — The Government's Real Answer

The turning point came in the Union Budget of 2022 (announced February 1, 2022, by Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman). Instead of a ban, the government chose taxation:

- **30% flat tax** on gains from transferring virtual digital assets (Section 115BBH), plus applicable cess — with no deduction except the cost of acquisition
- **No loss set-off** — losses from one crypto trade cannot offset gains from another, nor be carried forward
- **1% TDS** on crypto transfers (Section 194S), effective July 1, 2022, which quietly made high-frequency trading much harder

The tax regime said something subtle: the government was not blessing crypto, but it was not banning it either. It was treating crypto as a taxable activity while keeping it outside the monetary system.

**Tax note:** crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

## 2022–2023: CBDC and the G20 Play

While taxing private crypto, the RBI built its own alternative: the **e₹ (digital rupee)** CBDC. The wholesale pilot launched in November 2022 and the retail pilot in December 2022. Adoption has grown slowly — the RBI itself has acknowledged modest rather than spectacular uptake — but the signal matters: the RBI believes in the technology of digital money, just not in *private* digital money it cannot control.

In 2023, India's G20 presidency became the stage for a global push. The New Delhi Leaders' Declaration endorsed a coordinated framework for crypto assets based on work by the Financial Stability Board and the IMF — regulation and data collection rather than a global ban.

## March 2023: Crypto Comes Under Anti-Money-Laundering Law

One of the most consequential moves: the government brought virtual digital asset service providers under the **Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA), 2002**. Exchanges and wallet providers became "reporting entities" that must register with **FIU-IND** (Financial Intelligence Unit–India), verify customer identity (KYC), and report suspicious transactions.

This is why every Indian exchange now demands full KYC before you can trade. Offshore exchanges serving Indian users were not exempt — and enforcement followed.

## December 2023–2024: The Offshore Crackdown

In December 2023, FIU-IND issued show-cause notices to nine offshore exchanges operating in India without registration. In January 2024, the government ordered their URLs blocked. Some of the world's biggest platforms suddenly went dark for Indian users.

The aftermath set the template: major offshore exchanges, including Binance and KuCoin, registered with FIU-IND in 2024 after facing penalties — Binance reportedly paid a penalty of over ₹18 crore. The message to the industry: **serve Indian users, follow Indian rules, or lose Indian users.**

## 2025–2026: Where Things Stand Today

As of October 2026, India's crypto framework is a patchwork, not a single law:

- **No dedicated crypto law.** Parliament has still not passed a crypto-specific statute. A September 2026 Parliamentary panel hearing on virtual digital assets drew headlines, but no legislation has emerged.
- **Taxation continues unchanged.** The Union Budget 2026 left the 30% tax and 1% TDS intact. (The Income Tax Act, 2025, which took effect April 1, 2026, carries these provisions forward.)
- **FIU-IND registration is the operating licence.** By mid-2026, around 54 Virtual Digital Asset Service Providers were registered — domestic exchanges like CoinDCX, CoinSwitch, WazirX, ZebPay, and Mudrex, plus offshore names that complied.
- **RBI's stance is unchanged.** It still opposes any legal-tender status for private crypto, remains hostile to private stablecoins, and wants crypto walled off from the banking and payments system.
- **SEBI is in the picture.** Discussion of a multi-regulator model has surfaced: SEBI overseeing tokens that behave like securities and exchange-like activity, the RBI handling monetary and cross-border aspects, and the Finance Ministry keeping tax policy.

## What This History Means for You as a Beginner

Three practical lessons come out of this timeline:

1. **Trade only on FIU-IND-registered platforms.** The offshore crackdown showed that unregistered platforms can lose access overnight. Registered Indian exchanges (CoinDCX, ZebPay, WazirX, CoinSwitch, Mudrex and others) operate under Indian compliance rules.
2. **Budget for the tax regime.** The 30% flat tax with no loss set-off means your profitable trades carry a heavy tax cost — and losses cannot help you. The 1% TDS on transfers also eats working capital if you trade frequently. Understand these costs before your first trade.
3. **Watch the rules, not the rumours.** The 2021 "ban bill" panic taught Indian investors one lesson: headlines about crypto legislation are often wrong or premature. Follow official announcements from the RBI, the Finance Ministry, and FIU-IND — not viral WhatsApp forwards.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is cryptocurrency legal in India right now?

Yes. Buying, holding, and selling crypto is legal in India — the Supreme Court confirmed this in its 2020 judgment striking down the RBI's banking restriction. But legal does not mean unregulated: crypto is not legal tender, gains face a 30% flat tax plus applicable cess, 1% TDS applies to most transfers, and exchanges must register with FIU-IND.

### Why does the RBI oppose cryptocurrency?

The RBI's view has been steady for over a decade: private crypto threatens monetary policy and the rupee's dominance, creates financial-stability risks, and exposes consumers to fraud and volatility. It prefers its own digital rupee (e₹ CBDC). The 2020 Supreme Court judgment stopped the RBI from severing crypto from the banking system, but it did not change the RBI's opinion — or require the government to treat crypto as currency.

### Will India ever pass a dedicated crypto law?

Uncertain — and that uncertainty is exactly why beginners should stay flexible. The 2021 bill was listed and shelved; since then the government has regulated through tax law and PMLA/FIU-IND rules instead. A September 2026 Parliamentary hearing on virtual assets kept the conversation alive, but no law has been passed. Rely on official announcements, not speculation.
