Something changed in crypto security in 2026, and the numbers now say so out loud. Security firm Trail of Bits has reported that crypto hack incidents in 2026 are running 71% above the average annual level seen from 2022 through 2025, and it points to a specific reason: attackers are using artificial intelligence to find vulnerabilities faster. That finding, reported on 1 November 2026, landed in a week when the crypto world was already reeling from the theft of roughly $6.3 billion in assets from Binance on 29 October. The two stories are connected, but they are not the same story — and understanding the difference is the key to staying safe.

This guide explains, in plain language, what AI-powered crypto hacks actually are, why they surged in 2026, and what an Indian beginner can practically do about them. It is not about any one coin, exchange or investment decision.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This article explains security trends in general terms; it is not advice about any individual's funds, exchange choice, or holdings.

## The 2026 Hack Surge, in Plain Numbers

When security researchers say hacks are "71% above the four-year average," they are talking about the number of incidents, not the rupee value of any single theft. The comparison period is 2022 through 2025, a stretch that already included several very large incidents. Beating that average by nearly three-quarters, with weeks of the year still to run, is a meaningful jump.

It is also important to understand what such a figure does not prove. It does not mean every platform is unsafe, and it does not mean your personal wallet will be attacked tomorrow. Incident counts rise for several reasons at once: there is more value stored in crypto systems than before, there are more apps and protocols to attack, and attackers have better tools. The 2026 figure is best read as a warning light on the dashboard, not as a prediction about any individual user.

The week's biggest example shows why the count and the value can tell different stories. The Binance theft alone, at roughly $6.3 billion, is one of the largest financial thefts on record, and it dominates headlines. But the Trail of Bits finding is about the broader pattern: many incidents, across many targets, adding up to a worse year than the recent average. A beginner who only watches the biggest headline misses the everyday pattern — smaller protocol exploits, phishing campaigns and social-engineering tricks — that actually touches ordinary users most often.

## What "AI-Powered Attacks" Actually Means

The phrase sounds like science fiction, so it helps to break it into three ordinary jobs that AI makes faster.

First, finding weaknesses in code. Crypto apps, exchanges and decentralised finance protocols are software, and software has bugs. Traditionally, finding a bug that could be turned into a theft took skilled researchers days or weeks of reading code and testing ideas. AI tools can now scan large codebases, suggest places where the logic looks wrong, and generate test cases far faster than a person working alone. Trail of Bits itself has shown the defensive side of this: in its joint Patch the Planet work with OpenAI, AI-built tooling helped catch a signature-forgery bug in a zero-knowledge system that could have cost millions. The same speed-up, in the wrong hands, lets attackers test far more targets in far less time.

Second, writing convincing traps. A large share of crypto theft does not involve breaking code at all. It involves tricking a person into handing over access: a fake exchange website, a support agent who is not who they claim to be, a job offer or investment group that builds trust over weeks. AI makes these traps cheaper and more convincing, because it can write fluent messages in many languages, copy the style of a real company, and personalise the approach using publicly available information. For Indian users, this matters doubly, because scams now arrive in Hindi and Hinglish as fluently as in English, and they often ride on real news — such as a famous exchange incident — to look urgent and believable.

Third, moving stolen money quickly. After the Binance theft, blockchain-intelligence firm Chainalysis reported patterns it had not seen before: thousands of automated transactions, including a single sequence involving 1 TRX, and roughly $325 million in stablecoins swapped into about $285 million worth of Ethereum in around 20 minutes. Automation at that scale is designed to outrun the freezing of funds by exchanges and the tracing work of investigators. It is a reminder that the race after a theft is also a race against time, and software is now running that race on both sides.

None of this means AI has made human skill irrelevant. The most serious attacks still combine automated discovery with human judgement: choosing the target, chaining weaknesses together, and deciding when to strike. What AI has changed is the pace. Defenders and attackers alike now work at machine speed, and the gap between a weakness appearing and someone trying to use it has shrunk.

## Why 2026, and Why Now?

Several forces came together in 2026. Crypto systems grew more complex, with more moving parts — lending protocols, bridges between blockchains, smart wallets with new features such as delegated permissions — and every new feature is a new place for a bug to hide. At the same time, AI coding and analysis tools became widely available, so the barrier to running a serious code-scanning operation fell sharply. And the value sitting in these systems remained enormous, which keeps the payoff for a successful attack very high.

There is also a feedback loop. Each major incident teaches the next attacker something: which techniques worked, which defences were slow, and how investigators responded. Security discussions at a recent summit in Mumbai, noted in the same Trail of Bits reporting, focused on exactly this problem — how auditing practices must adapt when attackers can probe code faster than traditional review cycles allow. Audits still matter enormously, but a single audit is a snapshot in time, not a permanent guarantee. Even well-audited projects have been exploited after their audits, which is why ongoing monitoring and quick patching now matter as much as the original review.

For India, the timing has a local dimension. India is one of the world's largest crypto user bases by number of people, even though crypto is not legal tender here and is taxed heavily. That combination — many users, high attention, and a fast-moving news cycle — makes Indian users a prime audience for scam messages. When a global incident dominates headlines, fake "withdraw now," "claim your refund" and "support desk" messages follow within hours. The Binance reopening window is a textbook example: as the exchange works through a phased restart of withdrawals, scammers have every incentive to send fake links to users who are anxiously waiting for access to their funds.

## What This Means for Indian Beginners

Start with a realistic picture of your own risk. If you hold a small amount on a reputable, FIU-India-registered exchange and you use strong login habits, your risk profile is very different from that of a protocol developer or a large trader. Most beginners are not targeted by sophisticated code attacks. They are targeted by phishing, fake apps, fake support accounts and too-good-to-be-true offers. The 2026 surge raises the background threat level, but it does not change the single most important fact: the attacker usually needs you to do something — click, type, approve or share — before they can take anything.

Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. Security habits, unlike tax rules, do not change with budgets: keeping records of your transactions in INR, and knowing which platform holds your funds, also makes it far easier to spot an unauthorised transaction early.

India's regulatory backdrop adds one more reason to prefer registered, transparent platforms. Virtual digital asset service providers covered by India's anti-money-laundering rules must register with the Financial Intelligence Unit-India and meet customer-verification and reporting obligations. Registration is not a safety guarantee, and it does not protect you from a hack, but it does mean the platform operates under Indian oversight, with a grievance process you can actually use, rather than existing entirely outside the system.

## A Practical Safety Checklist for 2026

These steps are habits, not purchases, and they cost nothing:

**1. Only open your exchange or wallet yourself.** Never follow a link from a message, email, social media post or search advertisement to log in. Type the address yourself or use the official app you installed from the official store. During incident news cycles, this single rule blocks most phishing.

**2. Treat your seed phrase like the keys to your house.** No genuine support agent, exchange employee or "verification team" will ever ask for it. Anyone who does is a scammer, whatever logo or job title they use. Store it offline, on paper, somewhere private — never in a screenshot, email or cloud note.

**3. Turn on app-based two-factor authentication.** An authenticator app is stronger than SMS codes, which can be intercepted or redirected. Keep backup codes offline, not in your inbox.

**4. Use withdrawal allowlisting if your exchange offers it.** This locks withdrawals to addresses you have approved in advance, so even a compromised login cannot instantly send funds somewhere new. There is usually a waiting period for adding a new address, and that delay is a feature, not an inconvenience.

**5. Send a small test transaction first.** Before moving a large amount to a new address, send a tiny amount, confirm it arrived, and only then send the rest. A wrong or tampered address is far cheaper to discover this way.

**6. Keep long-term holdings off exchanges where possible.** Exchanges are for trading; a personal wallet — ideally a hardware wallet for larger amounts — means your funds are not sitting in a pool that a single incident can drain. This does not make you invulnerable, but it changes your risk from "exchange-wide event" to "my own device and habits."

**7. Pause during breaking news.** If a message claims your funds are at risk and demands immediate action, that urgency is itself a warning sign. Real exchanges announce through their official channels and give you time. Check the official announcement page or app, not the message that scared you.

**8. Verify before you approve.** Smart wallets and decentralised apps increasingly ask you to approve permissions — sometimes broad ones. Read what you are approving, reject permissions you do not understand, and revoke old approvals periodically through your wallet's settings.

None of these steps requires trusting any single company or product. They work because they reduce the number of ways an attacker can reach you, and because they slow an attacker down — which, in a year when speed is the attackers' main advantage, is exactly the right defence.

## The Other Side of AI: Defence Is Speeding Up Too

It would be misleading to end on fear, because the same technology is strengthening defence. Automated auditing now catches bugs that human reviewers might miss, and it does so continuously rather than once before launch. Blockchain monitoring firms trace stolen funds in near-real time and share blocklists with exchanges within hours, which is why billions of dollars from the Binance theft were frozen on-chain before they could be fully laundered. And exchanges have shown they can absorb enormous losses from their own funds — Binance has said it will cover the $6.3 billion impact itself, using its treasury, its SAFU safety fund and its futures insurance fund — rather than passing the loss to users.

The honest summary of 2026 is therefore two-sided. Attacks are more frequent and faster than in the 2022–2025 average, and AI is a real reason why. But detection, tracing and recovery are also faster than they were, and the basic habits that protect a beginner have not changed. What has changed is the cost of ignoring them: in a machine-speed threat environment, a single careless click travels further, faster, than it used to.

The best response is not to panic, and not to quit crypto entirely out of fear. It is to treat security as part of the hobby, like checking the address before you send money or keeping your bank password private. Learn how the common attacks work, set up the protections once, and then get on with using crypto at a pace you understand.
