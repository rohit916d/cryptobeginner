Germany's largest bank now holds crypto for its clients. On Wednesday, 11 November 2026, Deutsche Bank said its digital asset custody service is live for paying clients, with Bitcoin, Ether and three stablecoins in custody. The launch was reported the same day by Reuters and The Block, and covered by Cointelegraph, Decrypt, Bitcoin.com and others. It is a quiet milestone rather than a dramatic one: no prices moved, no new coin was created, and no Indian investor can use the service. But it is the clearest example yet of a major global bank treating crypto as an asset it is willing to hold for other institutions — under a licence, with rules, and with the bank's own name on it. This guide explains what actually launched, who can use it, how it fits into Europe's MiCA framework, and what Indian investors should and should not take from it.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile — never invest money you can't afford to lose. This guide explains a published banking development; it does not recommend buying, selling or holding any asset, and it does not predict where prices will go.

## What Happened: The Facts at a Glance

The facts, as reported on 11–12 November 2026 by Reuters, The Block, Cointelegraph, Decrypt, Bitcoin.com, CryptoCompass and The Bit Journal, are:

- **Deutsche Bank's crypto custody service went live on 11 November 2026** for paying clients, according to the bank's own statement reported by Reuters. The Block reported the launch the same morning.
- **It holds five assets at launch:** Bitcoin (BTC), Ether (ETH) and three stablecoins or e-money tokens — USDC, EURC and EURAU. Tokenised financial instruments are on the bank's roadmap, not in the launch.
- **It is institutional only.** The service is aimed at clients of the bank's Corporate Bank and Investment Bank — companies, asset managers, hedge funds, brokers, custodians and governments. Retail customers are explicitly excluded.
- **The bank manages the wallets and private keys** on clients' behalf. Custody means the provider stores and secures the keys; the client owns the assets.
- **It is booked in Frankfurt.** According to Bitcoin Magazine, the service is booked at Deutsche Bank AG in Frankfurt, with clients in other EU countries served through the EU passport — the right to offer a service licensed in one member state across borders.
- **The launch followed a long build-up.** The bank first announced the plan on 16 September 2026, saying it would go live with its first clients during 2026 once the regulatory process was complete. It applied for a German custody licence in June 2023, and Decrypt notes it has been preparing a crypto custody offering since at least 2021.
- **The regulatory sequence is documented.** According to Cointelegraph, the European MiCA register lists Deutsche Bank for custody and transfer of crypto assets, with an entry dated 12 October 2026 and BaFin, Germany's financial regulator, as the competent authority. The sequence is therefore clear: announcement on 16 September, register entry on 12 October, launch on 11 November.
- **A separate asset-management agreement was signed the same day.** Deutsche Bank and Deutsche Asset Management — the majority-owned asset manager formerly known as DWS, with €1.22 trillion (about $1.36 trillion) in assets under management as of 30 September 2026, according to The Block — signed a memorandum of understanding to explore cooperation on tokenised funds, crypto exchange-traded commodities and stablecoins. That agreement is exploratory; it does not confirm that any tokenised product is available.
- **Asian expansion is planned for 2027.** In October, Gerald Podobnik, co-head of Deutsche Bank's Corporate Bank, told Reuters the bank plans to take the custody platform to Asian financial centres in 2027.
- **What is not disclosed:** the bank has not said which clients have signed up, what custody costs, or what value of assets it holds. The custody site lists no trading or exchange service.

## What Crypto Custody Actually Is

To understand why a bank holding crypto matters, it helps to understand what custody means. In crypto, whoever controls the private keys controls the coins. A private key is a secret code that authorises spending; lose it and the coins are gone, share it and someone else can take them. For an individual, the usual choices are to keep the keys yourself — self-custody, typically in a hardware wallet — or to leave them with an exchange, which holds them for you.

Institutional custody is a third option, built for organisations that cannot do either of those things comfortably. A fund, a company or a government body holding Bitcoin cannot realistically keep a hardware wallet in a drawer, and it may not want its assets sitting on an exchange's balance sheet. A regulated custodian solves that problem: it stores the keys in secure systems, handles transfers through controlled processes, and carries the operational and legal responsibility that a large organisation needs. The client still owns the assets; the custodian is the keeper.

That is what Deutsche Bank is now offering. The bank manages clients' wallets and private keys, allowing them to hold and transfer the supported assets. The service operates through Deutsche Bank AG in Frankfurt, with access provided through the bank's Autobahn gateway and Corporate Bank portal, according to Bitcoin.com's report. It is, in other words, crypto custody delivered through the same institutional plumbing the bank already uses for traditional assets.

There is one important limit the bank itself states: crypto-assets are not covered by a deposit-guarantee scheme like regular bank deposits. That warning appears in the bank's own September announcement. Custody with a bank is not the same as a bank deposit, and the protection that covers money in a savings account does not extend to crypto held in custody.

## The MiCA Framework Behind the Launch

The launch did not happen in a vacuum. It happened under MiCA, the EU's regulation on markets in crypto-assets, which since the end of its transition period has required authorisation for any custody of crypto assets on behalf of clients. That is why the register entry matters: the European MiCA register lists Deutsche Bank for custody and transfer of crypto assets, dated 12 October 2026, with BaFin as the competent authority. The bank said it received BaFin approval before going live.

MiCA is the reason this story is a banking story rather than a crypto story. Under MiCA, custody is a regulated activity with defined requirements — the custodian must be authorised, must meet operational standards, and must be supervised. Deutsche Bank's path through that process took years: a licence application in June 2023, an announcement in September 2026, a register entry in October, and a live service in November. That is a slow, deliberate sequence, and it is the opposite of the unregulated exchange model that dominated crypto's early years.

The contrast with recent European developments is worth noting. Just days before this launch, Binance said it would close its French accounts by 31 December 2026 after its AMF registration lapsed and it failed to secure MiCA authorisation — a story covered in detail in our earlier guide. Deutsche Bank's launch and Binance France's closure are two sides of the same coin: MiCA is sorting the market into authorised providers and everyone else. A bank that spent three years getting authorised is now live; an exchange that did not secure authorisation is leaving.

## What the Launch Is — and What It Is Not

Because bank-crypto headlines are easy to overread, it is worth being precise about what this launch is and is not.

**It is a custody service, not a trading service.** The bank holds and transfers assets for clients. It does not run an exchange, and the custody site lists no buying or selling service. A client that wants to buy Bitcoin still has to buy it elsewhere and then move it into custody.

**It is for institutions, not for you.** The service is aimed at eligible corporate and institutional clients in Europe. Retail customers are excluded, and the bank's custody site says the service is for its Corporate Bank clients. Nothing changes for a private customer at a Deutsche Bank counter, as CryptoCompass put it. An individual investor in India — or in Germany, for that matter — cannot open a custody account.

**It is five assets, not the whole market.** Bitcoin, Ether, USDC, EURC and EURAU. The three stablecoins are telling: USDC is the dollar stablecoin issued by Circle, EURC is Circle's euro stablecoin, and EURAU is AllUnity's euro stablecoin. The bank is holding the assets its institutional clients actually use — the two largest cryptocurrencies and regulated stablecoins in dollars and euros. Tokenised financial instruments are planned for later, subject to client demand and the bank's product approval, risk and regulatory processes.

**It is a launch, not an endorsement.** The bank's own materials warn that digital assets carry material risks, including price volatility, fraud, cyber incidents and failures of market participants. Custody does not make crypto safe, and it does not promise protection from losses. The launch did not move prices, and as CryptoCompass noted, it would not be expected to: custody is a service for holdings, not a buy order.

**The asset-management agreement is exploratory, not a product.** The memorandum of understanding with Deutsche Asset Management covers possible tokenised money market funds, crypto exchange-traded commodities and stablecoins. The Bit Journal's summary is careful on this point: the agreement points to areas of work, not a launch schedule. Tokenised fund issuance, stablecoin services and settlement capabilities should be treated as prospective rather than live parts of the custody offer.

## Why a Bank Launch Matters at All

If the service is institutional-only, European-only and does not move prices, why does it matter? Because of what it signals about where crypto sits in the financial system.

Five years ago, the question for a bank was whether to touch crypto at all. Deutsche Bank has been preparing an answer since at least 2021, applied for a licence in 2023, and has now gone live under a regulatory framework that did not exist when it started. That is a long, expensive commitment, and it was made by Germany's largest lender — not a crypto-native startup, but a bank with a corporate banking business that serves companies, asset managers and governments.

The launch also sits in a wider pattern. Coinbase received final approval for a US national trust charter in November 2026, with its charter effective 6 November. The New York Stock Exchange picked Avalanche to provide the core ledger technology for a tokenised securities platform. Deutsche Bank's custody launch is the European, bank-led piece of the same pattern: regulated institutions building the infrastructure for digital assets inside the existing financial system, rather than outside it.

For the crypto market, the practical effect is plumbing, not price. Custody is the kind of infrastructure that makes it possible for a pension fund, an insurer or a corporate treasury to hold Bitcoin without building its own key-management operation. It does not create demand by itself, but it removes one of the reasons institutions have stayed away. That is a slow effect, measured in years rather than days, and it is the opposite of a price prediction.

## What It Means for Indian Investors

For most Indian crypto users, the honest answer is that this launch changes very little directly — but the comparison with India is instructive.

**No Indian bank offers this.** No bank in India offers crypto custody to institutions or to individuals, and no Indian bank has announced plans to do so. Indian crypto users hold their assets on FIU-IND-registered Indian exchanges, on foreign exchanges, or in self-custody. The Indian system is built around exchanges registered with the Financial Intelligence Unit, KYC requirements, and the tax framework — not around bank custody. Deutsche Bank's launch shows what the other model looks like: a regulated bank holding crypto under a licensing framework. India has not adopted that model, and there is no sign it is about to.

**You cannot use the service from India.** The service is for corporate and institutional clients in Europe, booked in Frankfurt and served across the EU. An Indian company cannot simply open an account either — the service is for the bank's Corporate Bank clients, and the bank has not announced any India offering. The Asian expansion planned for 2027 targets Asian financial centres, but no details have been published about which centres, which clients or which assets.

**The tax rules are unchanged.** Gains on crypto assets in India are taxed at a flat 30% plus a 4% cess — 31.2% in total — with no set-off of losses against other income, and sales attract 1% TDS. That applies whether you sell Bitcoin, Ether or a stablecoin, and whether the asset is held on an Indian exchange or anywhere else. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

**The stablecoin angle is the most relevant part for India.** The launch holds three stablecoins, including USDC, and that matters because India has its own stablecoin quirk: USDT has traded at a premium of roughly 6–8% in India in recent days, meaning Indians pay around ₹100 or more for a token designed to be worth one dollar (about ₹94 at the current exchange rate). A bank holding USDC and euro stablecoins for European institutions does not change that premium, but it does show how stablecoins are becoming part of regulated banking infrastructure in Europe — the same tokens Indian users pay a premium for are now being held in custody by Germany's largest bank. If you buy stablecoins on an Indian exchange, the premium is part of your real cost, and it is worth checking the actual rupee price rather than assuming a stablecoin costs a dollar.

**Bitcoin's rupee price is what you actually see.** Bitcoin traded near $73,600 on 12 November, which works out to roughly ₹69 lakh per coin at the current exchange rate. The dollar figures in the Deutsche Bank coverage are global numbers; on an Indian exchange, the price you see is the dollar price converted at the rupee rate, plus any premium the exchange charges. When the rupee weakens against the dollar, Bitcoin's rupee price can rise even while its dollar price falls — another reason the headline dollar number is only half the story for an Indian holder.

**The MiCA contrast is the real lesson.** Europe now has a framework under which a bank can be authorised to hold crypto, and under which an exchange that fails to secure authorisation has to leave. India has a different framework: exchanges register with FIU-IND, comply with anti-money-laundering rules, and operate under the tax regime. Neither framework is a model for the other, but the comparison is useful. In Europe, the question is now which institutions are authorised. In India, the question is still which exchanges are registered and compliant. The Deutsche Bank launch is a reminder that regulation, not hype, is what decides which institutions can hold crypto — and that the answer differs by country.

## What to Watch Next

Three things, all of them data rather than predictions, will show whether this launch is the start of a wider bank-custody trend or a single bank's project.

- **The 2027 Asian expansion.** Gerald Podobnik told Reuters in October that the bank plans to take the custody platform to Asian financial centres in 2027. Which centres, which clients and which assets will show whether the service is a European project or a global one. India is not named, and there is no reason to expect it to be.
- **The Deutsche Asset Management agreement.** The memorandum of understanding covers tokenised money market funds, crypto exchange-traded commodities and stablecoins. If any of those become real products, that will be a separate, bigger story than custody alone. Until then, it is an agreement to explore, not a launch.
- **Other banks following.** Deutsche Bank is the first of Europe's largest banks to go live with MiCA-authorised custody, but it is unlikely to be the last. If other major banks announce similar services, the pattern will be confirmed. If none do, the launch will stand as a single bank's decision rather than an industry shift.

None of these is a signal to act on by itself. They are the same kind of data a weather report gives you: useful for understanding conditions, useless as a promise about tomorrow. The sensible use of a story like this one is to understand the market you are in, not to time it.

## The Bottom Line

On 11 November 2026, Deutsche Bank said its digital asset custody service is live for paying clients in Europe, holding Bitcoin, Ether and three stablecoins — USDC, EURC and EURAU — for institutional and corporate clients only. The launch followed a September 2026 announcement, a MiCA register entry on 12 October 2026 with BaFin as the competent authority, and a licence application dating back to June 2023. A separate memorandum of understanding with Deutsche Asset Management explores tokenised funds and crypto exchange-traded commodities, but that agreement is exploratory and no product has been announced. Asian expansion is planned for 2027. For Indian investors, the practical points are that the service is not available in India, no Indian bank offers an equivalent, and the tax rules — 30% plus a 4% cess, with 1% TDS — are unchanged. The launch matters because it shows a major global bank holding crypto under a licence, inside the regulated financial system, and because it sits alongside the Coinbase trust charter and the NYSE tokenised-securities platform as part of the same pattern: regulated institutions building crypto infrastructure, slowly, under rules, and without hype.
