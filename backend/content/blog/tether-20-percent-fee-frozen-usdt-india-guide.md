## Tether Sought 20% Fee From Hack Victims: India Guide

Tether sought a fee of about 20% from some hack victims before returning their frozen USDT, according to a Bloomberg investigation published on 24 November 2026 — and one of the victims at the centre of the report, a Singapore exchange called Bittok, is still waiting more than five years after its $50 million hack. The story matters for Indian crypto users more than it might first appear: USDT is the most-used stablecoin in India, the token Indians routinely pay a premium of around 6% to hold, and the same freeze-and-reissue machinery that trapped Bittok's funds is the machinery that froze more than $1.5 billion of USDT within 30 minutes of the Binance hack in October.

The investigation, carried on 25 November by BeInCrypto, CoinRepublic, Gadgets 360, crypto.news and FinanceFeeds, all citing Bloomberg's reporting, describes a gap between what Tether can do in minutes and what victims can get back in years. Tether can stop stolen USDT from moving almost instantly. Getting it back is a separate process — slow, discretionary, and, in the cases Bloomberg examined, expensive.

This guide explains what the investigation found, how freezing differs from returning, what Tether's published policy actually says, why the 20% figure is not the same as a general charge, and what the whole episode means for Indian USDT holders.

## What Happened: The Facts at a Glance

The story, as reported on 24–25 November 2026 by Bloomberg and carried the same day by BeInCrypto, CoinRepublic, Gadgets 360, crypto.news and FinanceFeeds, is:

- **Tether sought fees of about 20% from some hack victims.** According to the Bloomberg investigation, Tether quoted a 20% "reissuance fee" to some victims seeking the return of frozen USDT. The fee was quoted in correspondence about specific cases — it was not announced as a general policy, and the reporting does not say the fee was paid in the cases examined.
- **The central case is Bittok, hacked in June 2020.** Hackers took $50 million (roughly ₹461 crore) from the Singapore-based exchange. Tether froze part of the stolen USDT. According to emails Bloomberg reviewed, a Tether lawyer later told the firm that bought Bittok that a 20% reissuance fee would apply to anything returned. More than five years later, none of it has come back.
- **Tether's published policy says something different.** For users who accidentally send stablecoins to an incompatible address or smart contract, Tether's published token recovery policy accepts requests above $1,000 and charges up to 10% of the recovered value or $1,000, whichever is higher. The reported 20% figure for hack victims exceeds that published rate — and, as CoinRepublic noted, it does not amount to an officially announced revision of the company's recovery policy.
- **Tether says it has reissued $1.2 billion in USDT so far.** A company spokesperson told Bloomberg that the fees help cover expenses associated with returning frozen funds, including legal costs, and confirmed the $1.2 billion reissuance figure. Tether says it currently holds more than $7 billion in frozen USDT.
- **Tether's CEO says there is no law guiding the process.** Paolo Ardoino told Bloomberg, in the words quoted by BeInCrypto, "The problem is there is no guidance, there is no law that guides this." He said the company returns funds in stages in cases involving civil disputes, to limit its exposure to legal risks, because several parties can claim the same frozen tokens.
- **The report landed in the shadow of the Binance hack.** After North Korean hackers drained about $6.3 billion from Binance in October 2026, Tether froze more than $1.5 billion in USDT within 30 minutes, and Circle froze $1.1 billion in USDC. The investigation examined what happens after those freezes — the part of the story that gets far less attention than the freeze itself.

None of this is an accusation that Tether broke a law. No regulator has said the fees are unlawful, and Tether has not been charged with anything in connection with the report. What the investigation documents is a practice — a fee, quoted in private correspondence, that is higher than the company's published rate — and a process in which victims wait years for money that was frozen in minutes.

## Freezing Is Not Returning: How USDT Recovery Works

To understand why a victim can wait five years for money that was frozen in half an hour, it helps to understand what Tether actually controls. USDT is a centrally issued stablecoin. Unlike Bitcoin, where no company can stop a transaction once it is confirmed, Tether maintains a blacklist at the smart-contract level: it can mark specific wallet addresses so that the USDT in them cannot move. That is the freeze. It is fast, it is unilateral, and it is the tool Tether used within 30 minutes of the Binance hack.

Returning the money is a different operation entirely, called reissuance. Tether cancels the frozen tokens and mints fresh ones for the person entitled to them. That sounds simple, but it is where the complications live. Freezing only requires Tether to be confident the tokens are stolen. Reissuing requires Tether to be confident about who the rightful owner is — and in a hack, that question is rarely clean. The exchange that was hacked may have changed hands, as Bittok did. Creditors, insurers, liquidators and law enforcement may all claim the same tokens. A court order may be needed, and the relevant court may be in another country. Each of those steps takes time, and each of them creates legal risk for Tether if it hands the money to the wrong party.

That is the logic behind Ardoino's comment that there is no law guiding the process. There is no statute that tells a stablecoin issuer how to return frozen tokens, how quickly it must do so, or what it may charge. Tether's published recovery policy covers a narrow, simple case — a user who sent tokens to the wrong address by mistake — and sets a fee of up to 10% or $1,000 for that case. Hack victims fall outside that policy. Their cases are handled case by case, in correspondence, with fees quoted per case. The 20% figure Bloomberg found came out of that case-by-case process, not out of a published tariff.

The scale of the frozen pile makes the stakes clear. Tether says it holds more than $7 billion in frozen USDT and has reissued $1.2 billion to date. The gap between those two numbers is not, by itself, evidence of wrongdoing — some frozen tokens belong to cases that are still in litigation, some to addresses whose owners cannot be identified, and some to sanctions designations that will never be lifted. But the gap is the reason the fee matters: if returning frozen funds routinely costs the victim a fifth of the amount, the freeze starts to look less like a rescue and more like a toll.

## The 20% Figure vs Tether's Published 10% Policy

The distinction between what Tether publishes and what Bloomberg reported is the heart of the story, and it is worth laying out precisely.

| | Published recovery policy (accidental transfers) | Reported hack-victim practice (Bloomberg) |
|---|---|---|
| Who it covers | Users who sent stablecoins to a non-compatible address or smart contract by accident | Victims of hacks seeking return of frozen USDT |
| Minimum request | More than $1,000 | Not published |
| Fee | Up to 10% of recovered value or $1,000, whichever is higher | About 20%, quoted in correspondence (Bittok case) |
| Basis | Published on Tether's website | Private correspondence reviewed by Bloomberg |
| Status | Official policy | Not an announced revision to any policy |

Three points in that table deserve emphasis. First, the 20% figure is not a general charge. The reporting is careful on this point: it does not say Bittok paid the fee, and it does not say Tether applies 20% to every recovery request. What it says is that Tether sought — quoted — a fee of about 20% in at least one case, and that the fee was described as a reissuance fee. Second, the two columns cover different situations. A user who mistyped an address is not the same as an exchange whose hot wallet was drained, and Tether's published policy was written for the first case, not the second. Third, the absence of a published policy for hack victims is itself the problem the investigation highlights. When the rules are not published, the fee is whatever the correspondence says it is, and the victim has no document to point to.

Tether's defence, as put to Bloomberg, is that the fees cover the real costs of the recovery process, including legal expenses. That is a plausible claim on its face: tracing stolen funds, verifying ownership, dealing with competing claims and obtaining legal comfort are all expensive. But the absence of a published schedule means victims cannot judge whether the fee matches the cost, and cannot compare it with anything. A 20% fee on a $50 million recovery is $10 million. On a small victim's frozen balance, the same percentage is the difference between recovering something meaningful and recovering almost nothing — and, as FinanceFeeds noted, victims who cannot afford the fee face the choice of accepting the cost or leaving their funds frozen indefinitely.

## The Bittok Case: Five Years and Still Waiting

Bittok is the human centre of the investigation, and its timeline is the clearest illustration of the gap between freezing and returning. In June 2020, hackers took $50 million from the Singapore-based exchange. Tether froze part of the stolen USDT. That is where the fast part of the story ends.

What followed, according to the emails Bloomberg reviewed, was a correspondence in which a Tether lawyer told the firm that later bought Bittok that a 20% reissuance fee would apply to anything returned. The fee was never paid, and the funds were never returned. More than five years after the hack, Bittok's frozen USDT is still frozen. The exchange itself has changed hands in the meantime, which is one of the complications Tether points to: when the victim company has been sold, who exactly is entitled to the recovery is a question that needs an answer before any tokens move.

It would be a mistake to read Bittok as a typical case. It is, by the reporting's own account, an extreme one — a 2020 hack, a change of ownership, a fee quoted and never paid, and a recovery that has not happened at all. But extremes are useful because they show the outer edge of a process. If a victim can wait five years and still have nothing, then the process has no effective time limit, and no published rule that would let a victim know where they stand. That is the finding that matters most for ordinary users: not that every victim is charged 20%, but that the system has no published answer to the question "how much, and how long?"

There is also a second, quieter lesson in the Bittok case. Tether says it has reissued $1.2 billion in USDT to date — a real number, and evidence that the recovery process does work for some victims. The investigation does not claim otherwise. What it claims is that the process is opaque, slow, and, in at least one case, expensive. Both things can be true at once: the system can return $1.2 billion and still leave a $50 million victim waiting five years.

## Why This Matters Now: The Binance Hack Connection

The Bloomberg investigation did not appear in a vacuum. It followed the October 2026 Binance hack, in which North Korean hackers stole about $6.3 billion, and the speed with which Tether and Circle responded. Tether froze more than $1.5 billion in USDT within 30 minutes. Circle froze $1.1 billion in USDC. Those freezes were widely praised at the time as evidence that stablecoin issuers could act as a backstop against crypto crime — and they were. But the investigation asks the follow-up question that the praise skipped: what happens to the frozen money next?

The answer, on the evidence Bloomberg gathered, is that it enters a slow, discretionary process with no published rules and, in at least one case, a 20% fee. That does not undo the value of the freeze — frozen tokens cannot be spent by the hackers, which is the point — but it changes the picture of who the freeze protects. It protects the system from the hackers. Whether it protects the victims depends on what happens afterwards, and that part of the story had not been examined until now.

The pattern has repeated since October. On 2 November, after a breach at the Brazilian exchange Mercado Bitcoin, Ardoino confirmed that Tether had frozen more than $4.5 million in linked USDT. In September 2026, Tether said its cooperation with US authorities had helped freeze nearly $550 million in Iran-linked USDT during the year, including more than $344 million across two addresses in April and another $130 million across four Tron wallets in July. Each of those freezes created a pile of tokens that will, eventually, need a decision: return, hold, or forfeit. The Bittok case suggests those decisions can take years, and that the terms are set in private.

Tether is, according to crypto.news, working on direct emergency communication channels with major exchanges to improve coordination during security breaches — a practical step that should make the freeze side of the process faster and cleaner. But faster freezing does not, by itself, fix slower returning. If anything, it increases the pile of frozen tokens awaiting decisions, which makes the absence of published return rules more, not less, important.

## What This Means for Indian USDT Holders

Start with what has not changed. USDT is legal to hold and trade in India, and nothing in the Bloomberg investigation changes Indian law. Gains on crypto transfers are still taxed at a flat 30% (plus applicable surcharge and 4% cess) under Section 115BBH, with 1% TDS under Section 194S on transactions above the specified thresholds, and losses cannot be set off against other income. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. No Indian exchange has reported any Tether freeze affecting its users, and no Indian user's funds are known to be caught in the cases Bloomberg examined.

What the investigation does change is the risk picture for the token Indians hold most. USDT is the dominant stablecoin in India, and Indians pay a premium to hold it. On 5 November 2026, one USDT cost ₹100.18 on Indian exchanges against a dollar reference of ₹94.64 — a premium of 5.97%. By the 19 November fix, the premium on USDT had reached 10.54%, part of a record spike in which Bitcoin, Ethereum and XRP all cost more than 10% extra in India. Indians are, in other words, paying well above the dollar rate for a token whose issuer can freeze it at will and whose return process, on the reported evidence, is slow and can cost a fifth of the amount.

That premium makes the custody question sharper. USDT held on an exchange is exposed twice over: first to the exchange being hacked, and second to Tether freezing the stolen tokens and then taking years — and possibly a fee — to return them. The Indian cases of the past month show how real the first exposure is. The Enforcement Directorate's searches at CoinDCX and Delta Exchange in early November concerned INR deposits allegedly converted to USDT and routed offshore. The HashPe case involved ₹112.91 crore collected partly in USDT and Bitcoin. The Delhi Police bust of a Binance P2P phishing syndicate in November involved USDT drained through fake buyers. In each of those stories, USDT was the vehicle — which is precisely why understanding who controls it matters.

The practical lesson is about custody, not panic. Keep only trading amounts on exchanges. Hold the rest in a wallet you control, and understand that even then, USDT in your own wallet can still be frozen by Tether if it is linked to a hack — self-custody protects you from an exchange being hacked, not from the issuer's blacklist. If you want a stablecoin that no company can freeze, that is a property of decentralised stablecoins, not of USDT — and those come with their own risks, including weaker pegs and thinner liquidity. There is no version of a dollar-pegged token that carries no trade-off; the honest choice is between different trade-offs, made with open eyes.

A final caution applies to the scams that follow stories like this one. Whenever a stablecoin issuer is in the news, fraudsters impersonate it. Messages will circulate claiming your USDT is frozen, that you must pay a "release fee" to unfreeze it, or that Tether is offering to return your funds if you share a seed phrase, a private key, a password or an OTP. Tether will never ask you for any of those things, and no genuine recovery process begins with a stranger messaging you on WhatsApp or Telegram. Treat any such message as a scam, verify anything through the exchange's own app or official support channel, and report suspected fraud to cybercrime.gov.in or the 1930 helpline.

## The Bottom Line

The Bloomberg investigation does not say Tether stole anything, broke any law, or announced a new 20% charge. What it documents is narrower and, in its own way, more important: a stablecoin issuer that can freeze billions in minutes, return them only over years, and quote a fee of about 20% in private correspondence — a fee higher than its own published 10% rate for a different kind of recovery — with no published rules for the cases that matter most. Bittok's $50 million has been frozen since June 2020. More than five years later, it has not come back.

For Indian USDT holders, the takeaway is not to abandon USDT, and it is certainly not a prediction about its price. It is a clearer picture of what holding USDT actually means: you are trusting a company, not just a blockchain. That company can stop your tokens from moving, can take years to give them back, and can charge for the privilege — and none of those terms are written down where you can read them before you buy. Knowing that does not make USDT unusable. It makes the decision to hold it an informed one, and the decision about how much to keep on an exchange, and how much in your own wallet, a decision worth making deliberately.

The next facts to watch are the ones Tether has not yet provided: whether it will publish a recovery policy for hack victims, whether the Bittok funds are ever returned, and whether regulators — in the US, or in India through FIU-IND — decide that a company holding more than $7 billion in other people's frozen tokens needs published rules for giving it back. Until those arrive, the story is what it is: a freeze that works in minutes, a return that can take years, and a fee that victims only discover when they ask.
