The Federal Reserve's October meeting minutes landed at 12:30 AM IST on 19 November 2026, and they were more hawkish than many traders had hoped. The minutes showed that almost all officials at the 27–28 October meeting saw further interest rate increases as likely appropriate — and that a couple even raised the possibility of a move larger than 25 basis points in December if inflation data came in badly. Bitcoin, which had briefly recovered above $65,000 after Nvidia's strong results late on Tuesday, fell below $63,000 as the minutes were digested, and US spot Bitcoin ETFs lost a net $239.9 million on Tuesday, according to Farside Investors. This guide explains what the Fed minutes actually said, why they moved Bitcoin, what markets now expect from the December meeting, and what — if anything — it means for crypto users in India.

## What Happened: The Facts at a Glance

The story, as reported on 18–19 November 2026 by PlainBTC, CoinDesk, Forex.com, CoinGabbar, XTB and World Macro News, is:

- **The minutes were released on 18 November.** The Federal Reserve published the minutes of its 27–28 October Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meeting at 2:00 PM ET — 12:30 AM IST on 19 November. The minutes are the detailed record of what officials discussed at that meeting, not a new policy decision.
- **Almost all officials saw more hikes as likely.** The minutes showed that almost all officials considered further rate increases likely to be appropriate, and that a couple floated a move larger than 25 basis points in December if inflation data were unfavourable, according to the Federal Reserve's own release as cited by PlainBTC and World Macro News.
- **A Fed governor called for a half-point hike the same day.** Fed Governor Christopher Waller told CNBC on Wednesday 18 November that the central bank may need a half-point increase in December, PlainBTC reported. Waller had already said before the October inflation data that a core CPI reading of 0.3% or higher should make a 50-basis-point hike a live consideration — and core CPI then printed exactly 0.3%.
- **Bitcoin fell below $63,000.** On Kraken, Bitcoin hit a low of about $62,213 around 10 a.m. Central time and traded near $62,800 shortly after the minutes came out, down about 3% from about $64,877 at midnight UTC, according to PlainBTC. Ether fell about 3.5% to roughly $1,760 over the same period. CoinDesk put Bitcoin about 26% below its October high of $87,200.
- **December hike odds climbed toward certainty.** Traders priced in a 95% chance of at least a quarter-point hike at the 8–9 December meeting, according to CME Group's FedWatch tool as cited by CNBC, with CoinGabbar putting the 25-basis-point probability at 96.5% against just 3.5% for a 50-basis-point move. Forex.com reported markets now pricing nearly a 70% chance of another hike at the 27 January meeting, and above 45% for the 17 March decision — implying the current 4.00% benchmark rate could eventually approach 4.75%.
- **The dollar strengthened and gold fell.** The dollar index moved near 103.78, a 19-month high, and spot gold sat near $3,732, close to a one-year low, according to Trading Economics data cited by World Macro News.
- **Bitcoin had briefly rallied first.** Bitcoin rose above $65,000 on Kraken after Nvidia's strong results late Tuesday, CoinDesk reported, but failed to keep pace with rising stock futures and was off more than 2.5% by late morning, at about $63,000.

None of this means the Fed has decided to raise rates in December. The minutes record a discussion from late October; the decision comes on 8–9 December, and two more major data releases will land before it. What changed on 18 November was the balance of expectations — and in crypto, expectations move prices fast.

## What the Minutes Actually Said

To understand why the minutes mattered, it helps to know what they are. The FOMC is the committee inside the US Federal Reserve that sets the country's benchmark interest rate. After each meeting it publishes a short statement; about three weeks later it publishes the minutes, a much longer record of what officials actually debated. Traders read the minutes for the arguments behind the decision — who wanted what, what would change their minds, and how the committee is leaning for the next meeting.

At the 27–28 October meeting, the Fed kept its benchmark federal funds rate in a range of 3.75% to 4.00% by a 10–2 vote. The two dissenters, Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack, each wanted a 25-basis-point hike instead, according to the official statement. A 10–2 vote with two hawkish dissents was already a signal that the committee was split, but the statement itself said little about what would come next.

The minutes filled in that gap, and the picture they painted was more hawkish than the statement alone. Almost all officials saw further rate increases as likely appropriate, according to the Federal Reserve's release. A couple of officials said a hike larger than 0.25 percentage points could be considered in December if inflation data were unfavourable. That is the detail that moved markets: not just that a hike is expected, but that the size of the hike is an open question, and that the answer depends on data that has not arrived yet.

Waller's comments on the same day sharpened the message further. The Fed governor told CNBC on Wednesday that a half-point increase is probably needed at the December meeting, according to PlainBTC, and XTB's daily summary noted that he pointed out nearly 70% of items in the core services basket are rising at a rate exceeding 3%. Waller is one official, not the committee, and his view does not decide the outcome — but when a governor speaks the same day the minutes are released, traders listen to both together.

It is worth noting what the minutes did not do. They did not announce a rate change, they did not commit the Fed to any December move, and they did not contain any new economic forecasts — those come with the December meeting itself. The minutes are a window into a meeting that happened three weeks ago, and the officials in that meeting were reacting to the data available then. Since then, the October inflation print came in hot, which is one reason the hawkish reading of the minutes landed so hard.

## Why the Minutes Moved Bitcoin

Bitcoin is often described as an asset that does not care about interest rates, because it has no earnings and no central bank. In practice, the link between rate expectations and crypto prices has been one of the clearest patterns of the past few weeks, and the minutes release showed it again.

The chain runs like this. When traders expect US interest rates to rise, or to stay high for longer, two things happen at once. First, holding dollars and US government bonds becomes more attractive, because those assets pay a higher return. Money that might have gone into riskier assets, including crypto, has a safer place to sit. Second, higher expected rates tend to strengthen the dollar, because investors buy dollars to earn those higher returns. A stronger dollar usually pressures assets priced in dollars, including Bitcoin, and it also makes Bitcoin more expensive in rupee terms for Indian buyers even when the dollar price is flat.

The minutes pushed both levers in the same direction. They raised the expected path of rates — not just December, but January and March too — and the dollar rose to a 19-month high while gold fell toward a one-year low. Bitcoin, as a non-yielding risk asset, fell with gold rather than rising with stocks. That is the same pattern seen after the hot October inflation print on 10 November, when Bitcoin slipped about 1% within minutes to around $73,600, and after the Nasdaq sell-off on 6 November, when Bitcoin fell with technology stocks.

There is a second, quieter channel that CoinDesk highlighted on 18 November: the basis trade. Crypto's basis trade pays traders for holding Bitcoin against short perpetual futures contracts, and it now yields only around 3% to 5% a year, near or below Treasury yields, according to data provider Talos. When the return from holding Bitcoin this way falls below what a government bond pays, some of that money moves elsewhere — in this case, CoinDesk reported, toward stock perpetuals. It is a technical, institutional flow, but it is one more reason crypto has been struggling to keep up with the rebound in stock markets even when the news for stocks is good.

A third channel is leverage. A large share of crypto trading happens with borrowed money, and leveraged positions are fragile by design. When the price moves sharply against a leveraged trader, the exchange forcibly closes the position — a liquidation — and that forced selling pushes the price further, which liquidates the next set of positions. The minutes did not produce a liquidation cascade on the scale of the 10 November CPI print, when roughly $200 million in leveraged longs were closed within five minutes, but the mechanism is the same: on scheduled-news days, prices can gap several percent in minutes, and leveraged traders absorb the worst of it.

Finally, XTB's daily summary noted reports of Bitcoin reserve sales by leading miners seeking liquidity to fund a transformation toward AI data centres, and put the industry fear index at 11 points, signalling extreme investor pessimism. Those are background pressures, not consequences of the minutes, but they help explain why Bitcoin had so little cushion when the hawkish news arrived. A market that is already fearful falls further on bad news than a confident one.

## The Hike Path Markets Now Price

The most useful number to take from 18 November is not the price of Bitcoin. It is the path of rate expectations, because that path is what traders will be watching — and repricing — at every data release between now and December.

| Meeting | What markets price in | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 8–9 December 2026 | ~95–96.5% chance of a 25-basis-point hike; ~3.5% for 50 basis points | CME FedWatch via CNBC / CoinGabbar |
| 27 January 2027 | Nearly 70% chance of another hike | CME FedWatch via Forex.com |
| 17 March 2027 | Above 45% chance of a further hike | CME FedWatch via Forex.com |
| Implied end point | Benchmark rate could approach 4.75% from 4.00% today | Forex.com |

A few things about this table deserve emphasis. First, these are probabilities from futures markets, not promises from the Fed. The December meeting is still three weeks away, and the odds can move as fast as they flipped in the week after the October inflation print, when the probability of rates staying unchanged went from 23.7% to zero in about a week. Second, the January and March figures are new: before the minutes, the market conversation was mostly about December. A 70% chance of a January hike means traders now expect the tightening to continue into 2027, not stop after one move. Third, the 50-basis-point possibility has not gone away. CoinGabbar put it at just 3.5%, but the minutes explicitly mention that a couple of officials raised it, and Waller argued for it publicly on the same day. If the next inflation print or the 4 December jobs report comes in hot, that number could rise quickly.

The calendar between now and the December meeting is short but crowded. The US jobs report arrives on 4 December, and the next inflation print lands on 10 December — the day after the meeting begins, so it will be in officials' hands as they decide. The December meeting itself comes with fresh economic projections, which will show where officials think rates are heading over the next year. Each of those releases can move the odds, and the odds can move crypto prices, in either direction.

## What This Means for Crypto Users in India

For Indian readers, the Fed minutes story has three practical layers: the rupee price, the tax rules, and the calendar.

The rupee price is the most immediate. At roughly $62,800, one Bitcoin is worth about ₹58.6 lakh at current exchange rates — ET Markets showed Bitcoin at ₹58,59,879 on 19 November at 01:38 IST, down 1.58% on the day, with Ethereum at ₹1,64,140. The dollar's strength matters here in a way it does not for an American holder: when the dollar rises against the rupee, Bitcoin becomes more expensive in rupees even if its dollar price does not move, and when the dollar price falls, the rupee price falls by more. The Crypto Times India Ethereum Premium Index, which tracks how much more Indians pay for Ether than the world price, stood at +10.88% at its 18 November fix and about +11.63% in live sampling early on 19 November — a reminder that Indian prices carry their own premium on top of the global move.

The tax rules are unchanged, and it is worth saying so plainly because market-moving news often creates the impression that something else has changed too. Crypto gains in India remain taxed at a flat 30% with 1% TDS on covered transfers, and losses cannot be set off against other income. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

The calendar is the part a beginner can actually use. The next Fed decision is on 8–9 December, with the US jobs report on 4 December and the next inflation print on 10 December before and during it. Each of those releases is a scheduled moment when prices can move sharply in minutes. That does not mean anyone should trade around them — the minutes after a data release are among the most volatile of the month, spreads widen, and stop-losses can be hit before the market settles. It means that if you see Bitcoin move several percent on a Wednesday evening or a Friday afternoon in the next three weeks, the first place to look is the US data calendar, not a rumour on social media.

One more India-specific point: the minutes story is not about Indian regulation at all. Nothing in the minutes changes the rules for Indian exchanges, the 30% tax, the 1% TDS, or the FIU-IND registration requirement. The link is indirect — through the dollar, through global risk appetite, and through the prices Indian exchanges quote — but it is real, and it is the same link that has driven most of the big crypto moves of the past month.

## What the Minutes Did Not Decide

It is easy to read a headline like "Fed minutes signal more rate hikes" and conclude that the December hike is a done deal. It is not, and the minutes themselves say so in their own way.

First, the minutes are a record of a meeting, not a vote on the future. The officials who spoke in late October were reacting to the data available then. The October inflation print, which came in hotter than expected, arrived after the meeting — and it is that print, as much as the minutes, that pushed December hike odds toward certainty.

Second, the minutes make the December decision explicitly conditional. A larger-than-25-basis-point move was raised as something that "could be considered" if inflation data were unfavourable — not as something agreed. The same conditional logic applies to the hike itself: the officials who saw further increases as likely were expressing a view, not making a commitment, and the December meeting will have fresh data, fresh forecasts, and a fresh vote.

Third, the market's own pricing shows the uncertainty. A 95% probability of a 25-basis-point hike still leaves room for a different outcome, and the 3.5% assigned to a 50-basis-point move is small but not zero. The January and March probabilities — 70% and above 45% — are even further from certain. Odds are a measure of expectations, and expectations can change as fast as they flipped this week.

None of this is a reason to ignore the minutes. A hawkish tilt from almost all officials, a public call for a half-point hike from a governor, and a market that now prices tightening into March is a real shift in the backdrop for crypto. But it is a shift in the backdrop, not a verdict on where any price will go — and anyone presenting it as a prediction is adding something the minutes do not contain.
