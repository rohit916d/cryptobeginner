A dollar and a dollar-linked token sound as though they should cost roughly the same number of rupees. India's crypto market has spent 2026 demonstrating why that assumption fails. On 5 November 2026, one USDT cost ₹100.18 on Indian exchanges while the dollar reference rate stood at ₹94.64 — a premium of 5.97%. On the morning of 6 November, the gap was still there: about 6.14%, or roughly ₹100.30 per USDT. This is the USDT premium in India, and it is one of the most India-specific features of the country's crypto market. It affects every Indian who buys a stablecoin, yet it is rarely explained in plain language.

## What the USDT Premium Is

USDT, or Tether, is the world's largest dollar-pegged stablecoin. It is designed to track the US dollar, and globally it usually trades within a fraction of a cent of $1.00. On 5 November 2026, for example, USDT's own global price was $0.9989 — essentially a dollar. But inside India, the same token costs materially more in rupees than a dollar costs in the currency market. The difference between the two, expressed as a percentage, is the premium.

The Crypto Times publishes a daily India USDT Premium Index that measures this gap in a consistent way. Every trading day between 11:30 AM and 12:30 PM IST, it records the buy and sell prices of USDT on five Indian exchanges — CoinDCX, Giottus, ZebPay, WazirX and KoinBX — takes the middle price on each, drops any quote that fails its quality checks, and uses the median across exchanges as the day's India price. It then compares that price with what a dollar costs in the wider world, using the previous day's European Central Bank reference rate for the rupee and USDT's own global dollar price. The premium is the gap between the two, in percent.

The index is careful about what it is and what it is not. It is not a forex rate. It is not India's official reference rate. It is not a black-market dollar rate, and it is not a trading signal or a fee charged by anyone. It is simply a measurement of what Indians pay for a dollar-pegged token on regulated exchanges, compared with what a dollar costs elsewhere. And the measurement matters because the gap has been large all year — the index's daily readings have stayed between 2.56% and 7.19% since July 2026, with a 30-day average of 5.12% and a series high of 7.19% on 30 October.

Here is how the premium has moved in recent weeks, according to the index and related reporting:

| Date | USDT premium in India | What it meant in rupees |
|---|---|---|
| 13 October 2026 | 4.27% | The lowest of the October readings, before the climb |
| 16 October 2026 | 5.37% | The gap widened as demand outpaced supply |
| 20 October 2026 | 6.71% | Premium climbing through the second half of October |
| 22 October 2026 | 6.90% | One USDT measured at ₹100.89 against a reference near ₹94.47 |
| 30 October 2026 | 7.19% | Series high — the widest gap in the index's recent history |
| 5 November 2026 | 5.97% | One USDT at ₹100.18 against a reference of ₹94.64 |
| 6 November 2026 (morning) | ~6.14% | About ₹100.30 per USDT, sampled at 05:45 IST |

Two details in the 5 November reading are worth understanding. First, the premium is adjusted for USDT's own global price. Because USDT traded at $0.9989 globally that day rather than exactly $1.00, the unadjusted gap was 5.85%; removing USDT's own small discount leaves 5.97% as the purely Indian effect. Second, prices differ between exchanges even on the same morning: on 6 November, WazirX was the cheapest of the five exchanges tracked, at about ₹100.20 per USDT, while Giottus quoted about ₹100.73. The premium is a market-wide pattern, not a single exchange's price.

## Why USDT Costs More in India

The short answer is supply and demand, shaped by Indian rules. The longer answer has four parts, and each one is specific to India.

**India buys more crypto than it sells.** Indian demand for crypto — and therefore for USDT, which is the token most traders use to enter and exit positions — is consistently strong. USDT is the on-ramp and the parking place: traders buy it to enter the market, hold it between trades, and use it to move value. When more people want to buy USDT than sell it, and the supply available on Indian exchanges is limited, the price rises. That is the basic mechanism, and it operates every day.

**Taxes slow the inflow.** Two Indian tax rules make it harder for fresh USDT to flow into the country. The first is the 30% tax on crypto gains. The second is the 1% tax deducted at source (TDS) on every crypto transfer above a threshold. Together, they raise the cost of moving crypto through Indian channels and discourage the high-volume flows that would otherwise close the price gap. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing.

**Banking friction limits supply.** Indian banks have been cautious about crypto-related flows for years. That caution means the pipes through which USDT enters India — market makers and liquidity providers who source tokens from abroad — operate with more friction and less volume than they would in a fully open market. When those providers pull back, even slightly, the domestic pool of USDT shrinks and the premium rises. The Crypto Times puts it plainly: a jump in the premium means dollars are scarce onshore; a fall means supply is catching up.

**Regulatory uncertainty adds a risk premium.** When market participants cannot predict what regulators will do next, that uncertainty itself gets priced into transactions. Purushottam Anand, founder of Cryptocurrency Legal, has said that part of the premium may reflect exactly this — a risk premium driven by regulatory uncertainty. Traders value the USDT already circulating inside India more highly when they are unsure how easily more of it will arrive.

It is important to note what the premium is not. According to the index's publisher, India has no parallel currency market of the kind Argentina or Nigeria have had. The premium comes from taxes, banking friction and demand inside a regulated market — not from capital controls. That distinction matters, because it means the premium is a feature of how India's crypto market is structured, not evidence of a hidden exchange rate.

## The June 2026 Spike: When the Premium Hit 8.5%

The most dramatic illustration of how the premium works came in June 2026. After an enforcement action tightened the supply of USDT into India, the premium surged past 8.5% — more than double its normal range of 3% to 4%. The Economic Times reported that USDT changed hands at about ₹102.88 over that weekend, while the official dollar-rupee rate stood at about ₹94.65. Buyers were paying roughly 8% to 9% above the official dollar value simply to obtain digital dollars.

The trigger was an Enforcement Directorate action. On 17 June 2026, the ED searched six premises in Bengaluru under the Foreign Exchange Management Act, targeting five crypto payment firms it alleged had moved more than $265 million in unauthorised cross-border transfers using digital assets over about two years. According to the ED's findings, the firms operated an informal remittance channel: rupees were deposited into company accounts, converted into stablecoins, sent across borders and sold on Indian exchanges, bypassing the documentation requirements that formal remittance routes demand under FEMA and India's anti-money-laundering framework.

The market reaction was immediate. Market makers and liquidity providers who source tokens from abroad pulled back after the enforcement action was announced, tightening the domestic pool of USDT just as the off-ramp infrastructure feeding it came under pressure. Fewer incoming tokens meant sellers had less inventory to meet domestic demand, and the premium roughly doubled. The episode showed, in a single weekend, that the premium is not a fixed fee: it is a live measure of how easily digital dollars can reach India, and it moves sharply when that supply is disrupted.

Enforcement scrutiny of cross-border crypto flows has continued since June. In late October 2026, the ED carried out fresh searches in Bengaluru and Madhya Pradesh in a separate investigation into unauthorised foreign-platform balances — a different case from the June action, covered in detail in our guide to the ED crypto searches and FEMA rules. Each round of scrutiny reinforces the same dynamic: tighter oversight of informal channels means the regulated supply of USDT matters more, and the premium reflects that.

## October Into November: The Premium Stays High

The June spike was not a one-off. Through October, the premium climbed steadily rather than returning to its old 3% to 4% range. The index recorded 4.27% on 13 October, 5.37% on 16 October, 6.71% on 20 October and 6.90% on 22 October, when one USDT was measured at ₹100.89 against a dollar reference near ₹94.47. By 30 October it reached 7.19%, the highest reading in the series. The 5 November fix of 5.97% and the 6 November morning reading of about 6.14% show the gap easing from that peak but remaining well above the historical norm.

One factor that did not move the premium is the rupee itself. The Financial Express reported on 13 October that the rupee had recovered toward ₹95 per dollar after Reserve Bank of India measures aimed at limiting its decline. A stronger rupee reduces the rupee cost implied by a $1 token, but it cannot create more stablecoin supply inside India. That is why the crypto premium can stay high even while the currency market stabilises: the two prices answer different questions. The conventional USD/INR rate answers what one dollar costs in the currency market. A stablecoin quote answers what buyers and sellers are accepting for a crypto asset designed to track that dollar — through a specific set of trading channels, with their own liquidity and spreads.

The October build-up also coincided with a volatile period for crypto more broadly. Bitcoin fell below $76,000 in early November as a rout in US tech stocks spread to digital assets, and Indian exchanges saw heavy selling. But the premium is not simply a mirror of the Bitcoin price: it measures the local supply of digital dollars, which depends on Indian taxes, banking and enforcement — factors that move on their own timetable.

## What the Premium Means for Indian Users

For an Indian buying crypto, the premium is a real, everyday cost. If you convert ₹1,00,000 into USDT at a 6% premium, you receive roughly ₹6,000 less in digital dollars than the exchange rate alone would suggest. That cost sits on top of exchange fees and the 1% TDS, and it applies whether you are buying Bitcoin, holding stablecoins between trades, or sending value to someone else. It is worth understanding before you transact, because it changes the maths of every trade that passes through USDT.

The premium also matters when you sell. Selling USDT back into rupees happens at the same elevated local price, so the premium partly works in both directions — you pay more to buy in, but you also receive more rupees when you sell out. The net effect depends on how the premium moves between your buy and your sell. If the premium widens while you hold USDT, the rupee value of your holding rises; if it narrows, it falls. That is one more reason the premium is worth watching as a market indicator rather than ignoring as background noise.

There is a temptation to look for a way around the gap, and it is worth addressing directly. Buying USDT on an offshore exchange does not remove the cost: moving rupees abroad to buy crypto runs into India's foreign-exchange rules, and gains remain taxable in India regardless of where the trade happens. Informal channels that promise USDT at or near the global price operate outside the regulated system, and they are the channels enforcement agencies have targeted — the June 2026 ED action is the clearest example. For most Indian users, the honest conclusion is that the premium is a cost of buying digital dollars inside India's regulated market, and the way to manage it is to understand it, compare prices across exchanges, and factor it into every decision — not to chase a shortcut.

It also helps to know how to read the premium as a signal. A rising premium generally means dollars are getting scarcer onshore — demand is outrunning supply, or supply channels are tightening. A falling premium means supply is catching up. Neither movement is a forecast about Bitcoin's price or a recommendation to buy or sell. The premium measures the plumbing of India's crypto market, not the direction of the market itself.

## How to Check the Premium Yourself

You do not need a special tool to see the premium. The method is simple, and anyone can repeat it:

1. **Find the USDT price in rupees** on an Indian exchange — the price at which USDT is actually trading, not a converted global price.
2. **Find the dollar reference rate** — the USD/INR rate from a reliable source such as the Reserve Bank of India or the European Central Bank reference.
3. **Divide and compare.** If USDT costs ₹100 and a dollar costs ₹94.64, the premium is (100 − 94.64) / 94.64, or about 5.7%.
4. **Adjust for USDT's own price.** USDT does not always trade at exactly $1.00 globally. If it is at $0.9989, as on 5 November, a small part of the gap is global, not Indian. The Crypto Times index makes this adjustment; a rough check does not need to, but it is worth knowing the adjustment exists.
5. **Compare across exchanges.** Prices differ between platforms — on 6 November, WazirX quoted about ₹100.20 while Giottus quoted about ₹100.73 — so a single exchange's price is not the whole market.

A daily index like the one published by The Crypto Times does this calculation every trading day at a fixed time, which makes it possible to track the trend rather than a single snapshot. But the underlying idea is accessible to any user with two numbers and a calculator.

## The Bottom Line

USDT costs about 6% more in India than a dollar does, and the reason is structural. India buys more crypto than it sells, while a 30% tax on gains, 1% TDS on transfers and cautious banking make it harder for fresh USDT to flow in. When demand outruns supply, buyers pay more rupees per token than the dollar rate implies. Enforcement actions that tighten supply — like the ED's June 2026 searches in Bengaluru — can push the premium past 8.5% in a weekend, and through October and into November 2026 it has stayed elevated, between 5% and 7%.

For Indian users, the premium is not a mystery and not a scam. It is a visible, measurable cost of buying digital dollars inside India's regulated market — one that shows up in every trade that passes through USDT. Understanding it means understanding the market you are actually trading in, with its own taxes, rules and supply chains, rather than assuming a dollar-pegged token costs the same everywhere. The next time you see USDT quoted near ₹100 while the dollar sits near ₹94.64, you will know exactly what that gap is, why it is there, and what would have to change for it to close.
