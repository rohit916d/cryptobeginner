Binance $3.64 billion outflows became the biggest crypto headline on 2 November 2026, just one day after the world's largest crypto exchange reopened withdrawals following its record $6.3 billion security breach. According to Reuters, reporting on 2 November, Binance customers pulled a net $3.64 billion — about 2.1% of the exchange's assets — as the platform went back online on Sunday, and Binance said withdrawals were stabilising after an initial spike.

If you are an Indian user with funds on Binance, or you are simply trying to understand what an "outflow" actually means, this guide explains the new numbers in plain language: what happened after reopening, why two respected sources report different totals, what a stabilising outflow looks like, and what Indian users should check before they move anything. It is an explainer, not a recommendation, and it makes no prediction about prices or about any exchange.

> **Educational content only.** This is not financial advice. Crypto markets are volatile, and exchange situations can change quickly — this guide explains reported events, not what you should do with your money.

## What Happened After Binance Reopened

To understand the outflow figure, it helps to recap the sequence. Binance suffered a security incident in late October 2026, in which attackers moved an estimated $6.3 billion of assets from platform-linked wallets in roughly 20 minutes. Binance paused withdrawals while it investigated, and promised to cover user losses from company funds and its SAFU safety fund.

On 1 November, withdrawals reopened in phases. The first phase covered 15 tokens and networks, prioritised by user demand over the previous week, starting with Bitcoin and then adding Ether, BNB, and some USDT and USDC stablecoin networks. Our earlier guide covered that reopening in detail, including the phased schedule and the 95% replenishment figure.

The new development on 2 November is what happened next: the first real test of that reopening. When withdrawals had been paused for days, a backlog of users wanted their funds at the same time. Reuters reported on 2 November that Binance customers pulled $3.64 billion in net terms, and that Binance said withdrawals were stabilising after an initial spike, with deposit volumes remaining substantial. The Economic Times, Dawn, and other outlets carried the same Reuters reporting on 2 November, confirming the headline figure across multiple sources.

Binance also said it had now covered 95% of the stolen assets, and that it had set aside around $6.34 billion of company funds to cover the losses. In other words, the exchange's public position is that user funds remain safe and that the company, not users, will absorb the financial impact of the hack. That claim is central to the story, and it is also the claim that the next verification steps — phased withdrawals continuing, and proof-of-reserves checks — are meant to test.

## What "Net Outflows" Actually Means

The word "outflows" is used loosely in headlines, so it is worth being precise. There are three related but different numbers, and confusing them is the most common source of misunderstanding.

**Gross withdrawals** are the total amount users took out. If 100,000 users each withdrew crypto, the gross figure adds all of those withdrawals together.

**Deposits** are the total amount users put in during the same period. Even during a crisis, some users deposit fresh funds to trade, to buy assets they see as cheap, or to move funds between their own accounts.

**Net outflows** are withdrawals minus deposits. Binance's $3.64 billion is a net figure: it reflects what clients withdrew and deposited after the event, as of 15:30 GMT on the reporting day, according to Reuters. That is why the net number can be smaller than the gross withdrawals — new deposits offset part of the money leaving.

For context, Binance said it had $171 billion in total assets under management as of 28 October, and 335 million registered users globally. Against that base, $3.64 billion is about 2.1% of assets. That percentage matters because it puts the headline in proportion: a very large absolute number can still be a small share of a very large platform. At the same time, a percentage alone does not settle the question of safety — what matters is whether the remaining assets are liquid, accessible, and actually held for users, which is exactly what proof-of-reserves verification is supposed to show.

## Why Binance and DefiLlama Disagree

One detail in the 2 November reporting deserves special attention, because beginners often assume that a disagreement between sources means one of them is wrong. In this case, the two figures measure different things.

Crypto analytics platform DefiLlama showed outflows of $5.81 billion on its dashboard, up from $4.36 billion earlier, according to Reuters. Binance did not immediately respond to a request for comment on the updated DefiLlama figure, but Binance said its own last update on X was closest to accurate, and that the DefiLlama figure did not contain the flows deposited to Binance's own new hot wallet.

A hot wallet is an online wallet an exchange uses for day-to-day withdrawals and deposits. After a security incident, an exchange typically moves funds to new wallets as part of its security reset. When an analytics dashboard tracks wallet movements on public blockchains, transfers between an exchange's own old and new wallets can look like outflows, even though the funds have not left the exchange's control. Binance's argument, in short, is that DefiLlama's on-chain tracking counts some internal movements as outflows, while Binance's own figure counts only genuine customer deposits and withdrawals.

Neither explanation can be fully verified from outside the exchange on the day of reporting. The practical lesson for beginners is simpler: when two figures differ, do not pick the scarier one by default. Ask what each figure counts, who produced it, and whether it is gross or net. A dashboard and an exchange statement are both useful, but they are not the same measurement, and the gap between them is itself information about how hard exchange flows are to track from the outside.

## Is This a "Bank Run"?

After a major hack, many headlines ask whether an exchange faces a bank run. The comparison is understandable, but it can also mislead, because a crypto exchange and a bank work differently.

A traditional bank lends out most of its deposits, so if every depositor asked for their money at once, the bank could not pay everyone immediately. That is why bank runs are so dangerous, and why deposit insurance exists. A crypto exchange, by contrast, is supposed to hold customer crypto assets in full — one unit of customer Bitcoin backed by one unit of Bitcoin held by the exchange — rather than lending those assets out as a bank would. If that standard is met, then in principle every user could withdraw at once, although in practice the process would be slow because of security checks, network capacity, and phased reopening.

That "if" is doing a lot of work. The reason users rush to withdraw after a hack is not just impatience: it is uncertainty about whether the full-backing standard really holds, whether the exchange's own funds are enough to replace what was stolen, and whether further phases will open on time. The outflow surge is therefore best read as a stress test, not as a verdict. A platform that processes a large, orderly wave of withdrawals and keeps opening new phases is demonstrating liquidity in practice. A platform where withdrawals stall, delay repeatedly, or stop for particular assets is sending a different signal, whatever its statements say.

For Indian users, there is an extra layer. Binance operates globally, and Indian users access it alongside users from many other countries. An outflow figure reported globally does not tell you how many Indian users withdrew, or whether any particular asset or network used by Indian users is affected. Always check the specific asset and network you hold, rather than assuming a global headline applies evenly to every coin.

## What "Stabilising" Means — and What It Does Not Prove

Binance said withdrawals were stabilising after an initial spike. That phrase is worth unpacking, because it is easy to over-read.

An initial spike is normal after a reopening. Users who have waited days to access funds tend to act in the first hours, producing a sharp peak. Stabilising means that the rate of withdrawals after that peak has fallen back toward a more ordinary level, rather than accelerating further. It does not mean withdrawals have stopped, that every asset is available, or that the situation is fully resolved. Later phases were still scheduled, subject to security checks, and some wallets and assets remained under audit at the time of reporting.

Two other signals from the same reporting are worth noting. First, Binance said deposit volumes had remained substantial. In a panic where users had lost all confidence, deposits would be expected to collapse; substantial deposits suggest that at least some users were still willing to bring funds onto the platform, whether to trade or to reposition. Second, Bitcoin recovered slightly through the weekend to trade above $78,000 on Monday, according to Reuters, after hitting a six-week low on Friday, while Ether also recovered a little to $2,287 after a two-month low. Those price moves are context, not proof of safety, and this guide makes no prediction about where prices go next.

The next hard checkpoints, rather than any single day's outflow total, are the ones to watch: whether later withdrawal phases open as scheduled, whether the full proof-of-reserves verification due around 3 November is published, and whether the remaining roughly 5% of stolen assets is replenished. Each of those is a concrete, checkable event, unlike a vague claim that confidence has returned.

- **31 October 2026:** Replenishment reaches about 90%, with 10 major assets reported as 100% backed, and the FBI formally attributes the attack to North Korea's TraderTraitor unit.
- **1 November 2026:** Withdrawals actually resume in phases, starting with Bitcoin and then Ether, BNB, and selected stablecoin networks; replenishment reaches about 95%.

Keeping this timeline in mind prevents a common error: treating each day's headline as a brand-new crisis, when it is often the next chapter of the same incident. The outflow story is chapter five, not a separate hack.

## What Indian Users Should Check Before Moving Funds

If you hold crypto on Binance, or you are considering moving funds, the following checklist is educational, not a set of instructions to buy, sell, or withdraw. Work through it calmly, and do not rush a transfer because of a headline.

**1. Check whether your asset is actually enabled.** Withdrawals reopened in phases, by token and network, based on user demand. An asset that is not yet in an open phase cannot be withdrawn, however urgent the headline feels. Check Binance's own status updates for your specific asset and network before attempting anything.

**2. Test with a small amount first.** If you do move funds, a small test transfer confirms that you have the right network, the right address format, and a working destination wallet. Crypto transfers to the wrong network or address can be difficult or impossible to recover, and a test transfer limits that risk.

**3. Understand network fees and timing.** Different networks charge different fees and confirm at different speeds. During busy periods, fees can rise and confirmations can slow. A delayed withdrawal is not automatically a sign of trouble — it can also reflect network congestion or security checks — but unexplained, repeated delays for a specific asset are worth noting.

**4. Decide where funds go, before you move them.** Moving crypto to your own wallet means you control the private keys, but also that you alone are responsible for backups and security. Keeping crypto on an exchange means easier trading, but also that you rely on the exchange's security and liquidity. Neither option is risk-free, and the right choice depends on how actively you trade, how much you hold, and how confident you are in managing your own wallet security. Never share your seed phrase or private keys with anyone, including anyone claiming to help with a withdrawal.

**5. Keep records for tax and safety.** If you later sell crypto for rupees, that sale can be a taxable event in India. India applies a flat 30% tax on crypto gains, with 1% TDS on many transfers, and losses cannot be set off against other income. Tax note: crypto tax rules can change — the 30% rate and 1% TDS described here were current as of September 2026. Verify the latest rules before filing. Simply withdrawing crypto to your own wallet is not the same as selling it, but keeping a record of every movement — date, asset, amount, network, and destination — makes tax filing and any later dispute far easier.

**6. Beware of copycat scams.** After a major hack and reopening, scammers impersonate exchanges, offer "priority withdrawal" services, and send fake support links. Binance will not ask you for your password, seed phrase, or a payment to unlock withdrawals. If a message creates urgency and asks for credentials or money, treat it as a scam until proven otherwise, and verify through Binance's official channels only.

**7. Do not make decisions from a single number.** A $3.64 billion outflow, a $5.81 billion dashboard figure, and a 2.1% share of assets are three different lenses on the same event. Read the method behind each number, check whether later phases open, and look for proof-of-reserves verification, rather than reacting to the largest figure in the headline.

## Outflows, ETFs, and the Wider Market

The 2 November reporting also placed the Binance outflows in a wider market context, and beginners sometimes wonder whether the two are connected. According to one analysis of the same day's data, US spot Bitcoin ETFs recorded $643.2 million in net outflows on Friday, the first full trading session after the Binance breach. That is a separate flow — money leaving ETF products, not the exchange — and it reflects broader market nervousness after a record hack, rather than a direct transfer from Binance to ETFs or vice versa.

Similarly, laundering activity continued to be tracked across cross-chain venues, with Chainalysis reporting that perpetrators moved hundreds of millions of dollars through Tron and onward routes. That tracking matters for law enforcement and for the recovery effort, but it does not change what an individual user should check about their own account. Keep the three stories separate in your mind: customer outflows from Binance, ETF flows in traditional markets, and criminal laundering of stolen funds. They share a cause — the hack — but they are different flows, measured by different sources, with different implications.
